"Digital arrest": the scam that keeps you on camera until the money moves

A caller in uniform tells you a parcel in your name held contraband, or that your Aadhaar turned up in a money-laundering case. Then comes the sentence the whole fraud rests on: stay on this video call, and tell nobody. This is how that call is built, why it works on people who are not fools, and exactly what to do — including the conversation to have with your parents before it ever rings.

There is no such thing as a digital arrest

If you read nothing else here, read this. "Digital arrest" is not a legal term, not a procedure, and not a power any Indian agency holds. It does not appear in the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Information Technology Act, or any rule made under them. No police force, no CBI officer, no Narcotics Control Bureau team, no Enforcement Directorate official, no customs officer and certainly nobody from TRAI has ever placed a person under arrest over a video call, because the law does not contain a way to do it.

An arrest in India is a physical act performed by a person who is standing in front of you. The arrested person must be told the grounds of arrest, is entitled to consult a lawyer, and must be produced before a magistrate within twenty-four hours — a guarantee that sits in Article 22 of the Constitution and is repeated in the BNSS. Custody means a place, with a register, and a magistrate at the end of the day. None of that survives translation to a WhatsApp video call, which is why no version of it exists.

There is no lawful arrest that happens on a screen, no lawful custody you serve in your own bedroom, and no lawful account you pay into to clear your name.

Three more absolutes worth memorizing, because every variant of the scam breaks at least one of them. No Indian investigating agency will ask you to stay on camera. No Indian agency operates a "verification account", an "RBI escrow account", or a "refundable security deposit" that clears your name. No genuine investigation requires you to keep it secret from your family. Those three sentences are enough to defeat the entire fraud, and they hold no matter how convincing the uniform, the case number, or the document is.

The one place online contact is real is service, not custody. Section 63 of the BNSS lets a summons be served in electronic form, and courts do a good deal of business over video now. But a summons names a court or an officer, a place, and a time, and asks you to be there. It does not open a camera, it does not ask for money, and it does not tell you not to speak to anyone.

What the scam actually is

Strip the theatre away and this is impersonation plus extortion, delivered by phone — vishing at the front end, coercion at the back. It sits in the social-engineering family described in types of cyberattacks: no software flaw is exploited anywhere in it. What is exploited is a person's instinct to cooperate with authority while frightened and alone.

The scale is the part people underestimate. Digital-arrest frauds became prominent enough in India that the Prime Minister devoted a segment of Mann Ki Baat to them in October 2024, with advice that is genuinely good and worth repeating: stop, think, then act. Government figures put losses to this one pattern in the region of two thousand crore rupees for 2024 alone, inside a much larger cyber-fraud total. Treat all such numbers as indicative rather than exact — they come from different agencies over different reporting periods, they only ever count complaints that were actually filed, and most researchers believe under-reporting here is heavy because the victims are ashamed.

What is not in doubt is the industrial shape of it. The Ministry of Home Affairs and the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) have publicly reported blocking large numbers of accounts and calling identities tied specifically to this fraud — tens of thousands of messaging accounts and over a thousand video-calling IDs, on the figures released in late 2024. Much of the calling is run out of organized centres in Southeast Asia, some of them staffed by trafficked workers who are themselves victims. This is not one improvising con artist. It is a workforce with scripts, shift rosters, and a supervisor who steps into frame when a call needs escalating.

I have volunteered as a registered cyber expert supporting law enforcement since 2020. The thing that stays with me about this particular fraud is not its technical sophistication, because there is almost none. It is that the victims describe the same experience in the same words, and those words are never "I was greedy". They are "I could not think".

The anatomy of the call

The call has a shape, and each stage does one job. Understanding the job is what makes the stage visible while it is happening to you — which is the only moment the knowledge is worth anything.

Stage one: the hook. Usually a recorded voice rather than a person — a courier company about a parcel, or a telecom regulator about a SIM registered in your name. It is deliberately mundane and slightly annoying, and it ends by offering to connect you to an officer. The recording exists to do the filtering cheaply: almost everyone hangs up, and the few who press the key have already shown they are worried enough to engage. Nothing is being investigated. The parcel does not exist.

Stage two: the handoff. You are transferred to a "police officer", and the medium changes to video. The change of medium is the point. A video call feels like being seen, which makes it feel official, and it puts you in a room with a person who can now watch your face for which threats land. It also means you cannot quietly do anything else — such as look something up — without being observed.

Stage three: the authority cue. A backdrop that reads as a police station, a uniform, a badge held to the lens, a case number, and within minutes a document arriving on WhatsApp with a seal, a letterhead, and your own name and identity number typed into it. None of that is hard to fabricate, and it does not need to be good — it needs to be present. We are trained from childhood to route around our own judgment when a uniform is in the room; the psychology literature on obedience to authority is essentially one long demonstration that the costume does most of the work. Seeing your real Aadhaar number on a fake notice is the moment most victims stop testing the story and start trying to comply with it.

Stage four: the isolation instruction. This is the load-bearing beam of the whole fraud. It arrives as an order dressed in procedure: the investigation is confidential, you are under digital custody, you must not disconnect, you must move to a room alone, you must not tell your family because they may be treated as accomplices. Sometimes the caller demands the camera stay on overnight. Everything else in the scam is decoration. This step is the mechanism, because the fraud dies the instant the victim says the story out loud to a second person, and the caller knows it. Every other stage exists to buy compliance with this one.

Stage five: manufactured time pressure. A team is on its way. The warrant issues in two hours. The offence is non-bailable. Your accounts will be frozen tonight and your family will find out tomorrow. Urgency is not incidental here — it is a deliberate denial of the only thing that would save you, which is time to think. Real procedure is slow and generates paper. Fraud has to be fast, because its story cannot survive being checked.

Stage six: the escalation ladder. The first demand is almost never the big one. It is small, framed as procedural, and framed as temporary: transfer your balance to a "verification account" so it can be confirmed as clean, and it will be returned within hours. Once that first transfer happens, the victim is no longer defending their innocence — they are defending the decision they just made. That is sunk cost operating exactly as designed. The demands then climb: fixed deposits broken, gold sold, loans taken, relatives borrowed from, each step justified by the ones before it. The calls can run for days, with "shift changes" and permission granted to eat or sleep, because the custody fiction is what keeps the ladder standing.

Stage seven: silence. They stop answering when the money stops. Some victims are then contacted again weeks later by a different group offering to recover the funds for a fee. That is the same list being worked twice.

Why it works on careful people

Most coverage of this scam is quietly contemptuous of the people it happens to, and that contempt is both wrong and dangerous — wrong because the reported victims are doctors, professors, engineers, retired bureaucrats and senior corporate staff, and dangerous because believing you are too smart for it is the state the scam wants you in.

The honest explanation is physiological. A credible threat of arrest triggers an acute stress response before any deliberate reasoning happens: the amygdala flags danger and the body commits to it, with adrenaline and cortisol arriving in seconds. Under that load, attention narrows to the threat and drops peripheral cues — the psychologist's term is cue utilization, and the everyday word for it is tunnel vision. Working memory degrades. The capacity for careful, sequential reasoning is precisely the capacity that goes offline first, and it stays offline while the threat is still on the screen. The caller never disconnects for exactly this reason: the fear has to be continuously renewed, because a person who is left alone for twenty minutes starts thinking again.

Layer three things on top of that. Authority — we comply with uniforms by default, and questioning one feels socially expensive even when nothing is at stake. Isolation — the one repair mechanism humans have for a distorted belief is another person's face, and the script removes it in the first few minutes. Commitment — after the first payment, admitting the fraud means admitting a loss you have not yet let yourself feel, so the mind reaches for the story where it was all necessary and refundable.

So the correct model is not "gullible people get scammed". It is that a specific engineered state — frightened, watched, alone, hurried — makes almost anyone compliant, and the entire call is a machine for producing that state and maintaining it. Intelligence is not a defense. Not being alone is.

Which is why the countermeasure is not to be cleverer during the call. It is to have a rule that fires before your judgment is needed, and to make the rule so short it survives adrenaline. Mine is one sentence: nobody who is really from the government minds being called back on a number I looked up myself.

The tells: a mid-call checklist

This is the part to read while it is happening, or to send to someone who is in it right now. The first item is on its own because it is sufficient by itself — you never need to reach item two.

  • Anyone on a video call telling you that you are under arrest, in custody, or being investigated live. That is the whole test. It is not a thing that exists. Stop there.
  • An instruction to stay on camera, or not to disconnect. No investigation in India has ever needed this. It exists only to keep you under supervision.
  • An instruction not to tell your family, or to move somewhere alone. Confidential investigations do not work by isolating the person being investigated from their own household.
  • Any request for money, in any wrapper. Verification account, escrow, clearance fee, security deposit, refundable bond, settlement — it does not matter what it is called. Every one of them is fraud. There is no lawful mechanism by which paying money to an account proves your innocence.
  • A request for an OTP, banking credentials, a screen share, or an app install. No officer needs any of these, and a screen share hands them your accounts directly.
  • A notice that arrives by WhatsApp, Telegram or email instead of being served. Even electronic service under the BNSS goes through a court's process — it does not arrive mid-call from the person accusing you.
  • Refusal to let you call back. Ask to end the call and ring the station on a number you find yourself. A real officer will find this reasonable. A scammer will explain why it is impossible, and that explanation is your answer.
  • Threats aimed at the exits. If you are told that calling a lawyer, telling your spouse, or hanging up will worsen your position, you are being managed, not investigated.
  • Odd numbers or numbers that look right. Unfamiliar international prefixes are a hint. But caller ID is display data, not proof of origin, and it can be made to show a real station's number — so a familiar-looking number tells you nothing reassuring. Absence of this tell is not safety.

"TRAI will disconnect all your SIMs in two hours." TRAI is a regulator, does not investigate crime, and does not call citizens about SIM misuse. It has said so publicly.
"A warrant issues in two hours unless you cooperate now." Warrants are issued by courts and generate paper. Urgency in a phone call is a tactic, not a legal position.

If it is happening right now

Six things, in order, and none of them require you to win an argument with the caller.

  1. End the call. Do not explain, negotiate, or announce that you have worked it out. Hang up and turn the camera off. This is not rude and it is not an offence, and it is the single action that ends the fraud at any stage.
  2. Put the phone down and walk to another person. Say the story out loud to them — a spouse, a neighbour, a colleague, a son or daughter on a different line. Saying it aloud is what restores the judgment the call suppressed. If nobody is nearby, call someone.
  3. Send nothing. No transfer, no OTP, no screen share, no app. If a transfer is already in flight, call your bank immediately and ask them to stop or recall it.
  4. Preserve the evidence before you clean anything up. Screenshot the call screen, the number, the chat thread, the documents they sent, any UPI IDs or account numbers, and note the times. Do not delete the thread out of embarrassment — deleted is exactly what an investigator cannot use.
  5. Report it even if you lost nothing. An attempt reported today is what links a number to the case of someone who did lose money last week. It takes ten minutes at cybercrime.gov.in.
  6. Block, and expect a second attempt. They call back, sometimes from a different number and a more senior-sounding "officer". Do not pick up to argue.

If you are the person someone has just run to: do not start with "how did you fall for this". Start with "you did the right thing telling me". You need them talking, and shame is the thing that makes people go quiet for three days while the money moves.

If the money has already gone

Speed is the only lever you have, and it is a real one. Work these in parallel if there are two of you in the room.

  • Call 1930. The national cybercrime helpline, run around the clock by I4C under the Ministry of Home Affairs. Behind it sits the Citizen Financial Cyber Frauds Reporting and Management System, which pushes your complaint to the banks and payment intermediaries in the transaction chain so funds can be held while still in transit between mule accounts.
  • Understand the golden hour honestly. Stolen money is layered onward within minutes — split across accounts, withdrawn, or converted. A hold works only on what has not yet moved, so the first hour is worth more than the next week. But "late" is not "pointless": file regardless of how much time has passed.
  • File at cybercrime.gov.in. The National Cybercrime Reporting Portal takes every category, and gives you an acknowledgement number. Keep that number — it is what every later conversation with a bank, an officer or a court will start from.
  • Call your bank's own fraud line, separately. The 1930 route reaches the bank, but your own dispute has to be raised on your account. Do it in writing as well as by phone, and get a written acknowledgement with a timestamp.
  • Go to the police too. Your local cyber cell or any police station. Under the Zero FIR principle, no station can turn you away for being outside its jurisdiction — the FIR is registered and transferred. What follows from there is described in how cybercrime cases are solved.
  • Secure everything they touched. Change passwords, revoke sessions, remove any app they had you install, and tell the bank if you shared credentials or shared your screen.
  • Look after the person, not only the money. Victims of this fraud carry it badly, and there have been suicides. If someone is in acute distress, stay with them, and know that Tele-MANAS, the national mental health helpline, is reachable on 14416.

On the legal side: impersonating a public servant, cheating, cheating by personation, and extortion are all substantive offences under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, and cheating by personation using a computer resource is an offence under Section 66D of the IT Act. Which sections a charge sheet finally carries depends on the facts of the case and is the investigating officer's call, not yours. The background to those statutes is in India's IT law, explained.

What recovery actually looks like

I am not going to tell you the money comes back, because often it does not, and every article that implies otherwise makes it harder for victims to accept the honest version.

Here is the mechanism. Your complaint triggers holds down the chain of accounts the money passed through. Only the portion still sitting somewhere in that chain can be held — anything already withdrawn in cash, converted, or moved outside the country is beyond the reach of a hold. Recovery rates therefore fall steeply with every hour of delay, which is the whole argument for calling 1930 before you do anything else.

And a hold is not a refund. Money frozen in a mule account is evidence in a live case; getting it released to you generally needs an order from a court, and that takes months, sometimes longer. Partial recovery is the common good outcome. Full recovery happens, but it is not the norm and no one can promise it to you in advance — not the bank, not the police, and not me.

Two things are still worth doing even when the money is gone. Reporting is what builds the case against the network rather than your case alone, and those cases are how numbers and accounts get blocked before they reach the next person. And your acknowledgement number is the anchor for any later claim, insurance question, or restitution order.

One warning specific to the aftermath. If someone contacts you afterwards offering guaranteed recovery for an advance fee — as a "cyber recovery agent", a "portal officer", or a lawyer who approached you rather than the other way round — that is the second scam, run on people already identified as having paid once. Genuine recovery happens through the bank, the police and the courts, and it never starts with a stranger's phone call.

How to talk to your parents before it happens

This is the highest-value section on the page, because the people most often targeted are not the people who read security blogs. The calls land during working hours, at homes where one person is alone, and they land on a generation that was raised to treat a uniform as the end of an argument.

Do it before, not after. And do it as information about other people, not as a test of their judgment — "this is going around, and it has caught doctors and retired officers" opens the conversation; "you would fall for this" closes it and guarantees they will not call you on the day.

  • Give one rule, not a list. Lists do not survive fear. One sentence does: no police, no court and no bank ever video calls you — if someone on a screen says police, hang up and call me. That is the whole curriculum. Everything else here is for you, not for them.
  • Give explicit permission to be rude. This matters more than it sounds. Say out loud that hanging up on a real police officer has no consequence whatsoever, that nobody gets arrested for ending a phone call, and that you would rather they were rude to a hundred genuine officials than polite to one fraudster. Many older victims stayed on the line because they had been raised never to disconnect on authority.
  • Agree a household money rule. No money moves on any call, ever, without one phone call to a named person first — you, a sibling, a trusted neighbour. Make that number a favourite on their phone. The rule has to be absolute, because "unless it seems really urgent" is the exact gap the scam is built to fit through.
  • Rehearse it once, out loud. Have them practice saying "I am going to hang up now and call my family" and then actually doing it. A rehearsed motion survives adrenaline in a way a remembered fact does not. It feels silly for ninety seconds and it is the most useful ninety seconds of the conversation.
  • Remove the shame in advance. Say this sentence and mean it: "if this ever happens, call me, and I will not be angry with you." That single promise is the difference between a call at hour one, when a hold might still catch something, and a confession on day three, when nothing can.
  • Fix the small practical things. Check that bank SMS and email alerts reach a number and inbox they actually read. Ask whether they would like a second person alerted on large transactions. Ask — do not take over their finances on their behalf, which is its own harm and makes people hide problems rather than report them.
  • Repeat it in six months. One conversation fades. The scripts also change: the parcel becomes a SIM, the SIM becomes a court notice. The rule survives all of those variants, which is why the rule is what you rehearse.

If you want a single action from this article, it is this: send this section to a sibling and agree which of you is making the call today.

Questions people actually ask

Is digital arrest a real legal procedure in India?

No. It is not a term in any Indian statute and not a power any agency holds. An arrest under the BNSS is a physical act, with the grounds communicated to the person and production before a magistrate within twenty-four hours. None of that is possible over a video call. Any call telling you that you are under digital arrest is a fraud, without exception.

Can the police or a court ever contact me online at all?

Yes. Section 63 of the BNSS allows a summons to be served in electronic form, and courts increasingly work over video. But a summons asks you to appear at a stated place and time before a named court or officer. It never demands money, never asks you to stay on camera, and never tells you to keep it secret from your family.

The number on my screen looked like a real police station. Does that mean anything?

No. Caller ID is display data, not proof of origin, and it can be made to show almost anything. Treat a familiar-looking number as neutral information. The only way to verify is to end the call and dial a number you looked up yourself from an official source, on a different phone if you have one.

Should I keep the caller talking to collect evidence?

No. Every extra minute is a minute they get to work on you, and the evidence that matters is already on your device — the number, the timestamps, the documents they sent, and the account details they gave you. End the call, then screenshot everything before you touch anything else.

I already transferred money. Is it too late to report?

Report anyway, immediately. Call 1930 and file at cybercrime.gov.in, then call your bank's own fraud line. Holds placed in the first minutes have the best chance of catching money still sitting in the chain, but complaints filed days later still matter — they support the investigation, they link your case to others, and they are what makes any later restitution order possible.

Will I get my money back?

Sometimes partly, often not, and nobody honest can promise otherwise. Only funds still sitting in the chain can be held, a hold is not a refund, and releasing held money usually needs a court order and months of waiting. Anyone who contacts you afterwards promising guaranteed recovery for a fee is running the second scam on the same victim.

They have my Aadhaar number and my photograph. How much trouble am I in?

An Aadhaar number is an identifier, not a password, and knowing yours proves nothing about a case against you. Nothing said on that call is evidence of anything. Report it, change the passwords on any account whose details you shared — the passwords and passkeys piece covers doing that properly — watch your statements, and if you shared banking credentials or installed anything at their request, tell your bank so the account can be secured.

A last thought about where this fraud sits. People often assume something this organized must be run from some hidden corner of the internet, and the mythology around that corner does more harm than good; the honest picture is in surface web, deep web, and dark web. The digital-arrest call needs none of it. It runs on ordinary phone numbers, ordinary messaging apps, ordinary bank accounts, and one person alone in a room being told not to hang up. That is also why the defense is ordinary: a rule, said out loud, before the phone rings.

A necessary disclaimer: this article is an educational orientation, not legal advice, and I am not a lawyer. Section numbers, agency procedures and reported figures change over time and vary between states. If you are dealing with a live incident, call 1930, report at cybercrime.gov.in, and speak to your local cyber cell and a lawyer rather than relying on any article, including this one.
