Automation

What actually happens in the first week of an automation engagement

If you are paying someone to automate a process, the first week is not building. It is reading the process as it actually runs, counting it, and deciding which parts deserve a machine — including the parts that deserve deleting instead. Here is that week in the order it happens, and what goes wrong in it.

Published 31 August 2026 · 9 min read · by Krunalkumar Shah

What week one actually produces

If you hire someone to automate a process, the reasonable expectation is that by Friday something is running. That is not what my first week produces, and I would rather say so before you pay for it than after.

Week one produces two things. The first is a map: a written description of the process as it actually runs, with the systems named, the people named, the volume counted and the failure points marked. The second is a decision — which parts to automate, which to fix by hand first, and which to delete.

An automation is a machine that repeats a decision. If the decision is wrong, or the process is about to change, or the whole thing runs four times a month, nobody has saved anything: you have bought a maintenance liability with a dashboard on it.

The shape of the week, roughly, and it is rarely this tidy:

Roughly whenWhat happensWhat it produces
Day 1Discovery conversation; access requested in writingNamed systems, named people, open questions
Days 1–3Waiting on read-only access, and chasing itUsually the longest queue of the week
Day 2 or 3Watching one real run, start to finishThe process as run, not as described
Days 3–4Counting volume, duration and error rate from exportsBaseline numbers with sources attached
Day 5Writing the map, the exclusions and the quoteA document, and a decision that is yours

The limit of that argument: for small work a week is overkill. One trigger, one system, one person, low consequence if it breaks — then a call and one observed run is the whole of discovery, and I say so rather than selling five days. The threshold I use is soft: more than two systems, or more than two people, and discovery earns its cost. I have not measured that boundary.

Day one: the conversation, and the questions that matter

Day one is a conversation, usually an hour or two, with whoever owns the outcome and — if I can get them — whoever actually does the work. Those are frequently not the same person, and that gap is often the most useful thing I learn all week.

The question I try not to ask is “what would you like automated?” It gets an answer that is already a solution, usually one somebody chose after seeing a demo. These are the questions that pay for themselves:

  • Who does this, and what else do they do? What it really costs, and who will end up owning whatever gets built.
  • What happens if it does not get done today? A step nobody notices when it is skipped is a candidate for deletion, not automation.
  • What did this look like before your current tool? Many odd steps are workarounds for a system that was replaced years ago and never removed with it.
  • Where does the data come from, and where does it end up? Named systems, not categories. “The CRM” is not an answer; the product, the plan and its administrator is.
  • What do you do when it goes wrong? The exception path is the hard half of any automation, and the half no demo shows.
  • Who has to trust the output? If finance or an auditor relies on it, logging and an audit trail are part of the build rather than a nicety, and that changes the price.

I write the answers in the words they were given in. Where two people describe the same step differently I do not reconcile it on the spot. The difference is a finding.

One more question belongs here: what would “done” look like, observably, if a build goes ahead? I have written separately about scoping work before you quote it. Ask it in week one, because the answer often changes the moment somebody says it out loud.

Read-only access, and why it is the first friction

Before that conversation ends I ask for read-only access to every system somebody named in it. Not admin. Not a service account with write scope. Read-only, and only to the named systems.

You cannot count what you cannot see. A view seat in the CRM, an API token scoped to read, a folder shared as viewer, or an export I can take away — any of those work. What does not work is a summary prepared by the person whose process I am there to examine, however honestly it is offered. It will describe the process they believe they run.

This is where the week usually slips. In my experience access is the commonest reason a discovery week becomes a discovery fortnight, and it is almost never obstruction: the person who wants the automation is rarely the person who can grant the permission, so the request joins a queue behind unrelated work. The only defence I have found is to name the exact systems and the exact permission level in writing on day one, with a date.

Nobody should be handing over write access in week one. There is nothing to write yet. If a proposal asks for admin credentials before anything has been designed, that is worth a question. Read-only protects you from me as well: I cannot break a process I can only look at.

Sometimes access is genuinely impossible — regulated data, a vendor contract, a customer’s system that is not yours to open. That changes the method rather than ending it. Discovery moves to a screen share with the operator driving, and the written output says out loud that the counts came from a sample rather than the full record. An honest caveat costs less than a confident number nobody can check.

Watch the process run once instead of being told about it

Everybody can describe their own process. Very few descriptions survive contact with the process. So the centre of the week is an hour spent beside the person who does the work, watching one real instance from start to finish, writing down timestamps and interrupting as little as I can manage.

What tends to appear that was not in the description:

  • A step that only checks another step, added after something went wrong once and never removed.
  • A spreadsheet nobody mentioned, because a spreadsheet is not thought of as a system.
  • Two steps that were described as one, and one that was described as two.
  • A small judgement in the middle — a glance to see whether this one looks odd — that the person does not count as work at all.
  • A step that gets skipped whenever the day is busy, which tells you a great deal about how necessary it is.

None of this is dishonesty. People describe the process they designed and run the process they have adapted, and the adaptations are usually the intelligent part of it.

It is not a specifically human failing either. The deploy script for this site dates each sitemap entry from the newest commit that touched that file. It used to skip any commit touching more than twenty files, so that a footer sweep could not reset every page’s date. Ordinary commits here touch 48, 57 and 146 files, so the filter threw away nearly the whole history and 94 of 100 URLs fell back to the one stale hardcoded date — the exact failure it was written to prevent. The code did what it said. What it said was not what I meant, and I found out by reading its output, not its source. More on that in why your automation broke.

Count the volume and the error rate before anyone claims an improvement

You cannot improve a number you never had. Before anything is designed I want three measurements, each with its source written next to it:

  1. How often it runs. Counted from a real export over a real period — ticket history, sent mail, a sheet’s revision history, log lines — not estimated in a meeting.
  2. How long one run takes. Measured on the run I watched, and ideally a second one, because the first is always the tidy version.
  3. How often it goes wrong, and what wrong costs. Rework, a late invoice, a customer told the wrong thing. The error rate often decides the case, and it is the number nobody has.

The volume is usually smaller than the description of it. That is a pattern in my experience rather than something I have measured across engagements: people remember the painful instances, not the quiet ones, so a task described as daily turns out to run on the days it is needed. Small volume is not a reason to walk away, though. A step that runs four times a month and is wrong once is a different problem from one that runs four hundred times and is never wrong, and only counting tells them apart.

If the evidence is in a log, count it in the log. The log analyser on this site reads an exported log inside your browser and clusters what it finds, with nothing uploaded — which matters when the export belongs to a client. It was built for security triage, so it will not read your CRM export.

Turning counts into money is a separate exercise, and the arithmetic is in the real cost of a manual process rather than here.

The habit came out of research rather than consulting. The one paper I have published is on defending Linux against fork bombs, and its only load-bearing sentence is a measured one: detection in as little as 44 ms at a threshold of 500 forks, on Xubuntu 18.04. Without the threshold and the machine, “44 ms” is marketing. “We saved ten hours a week” is that sentence with the conditions removed.

The steps that should not be automated

The most valuable column in the map is the one headed “leave alone”. Four kinds of step belong in it.

Judgement calls

Any step where a person weighs something that is not in the data: whether a customer sounds annoyed, whether this refund is a goodwill case, whether an invoice looks wrong in a way that is hard to name. Automate everything around such a step — assemble the information, put it in front of the person, record what they chose — and leave the choice where it is. Automating the choice encodes this quarter’s policy and then lets everyone forget it was encoded.

Low volume

Something that runs twice a month and takes four minutes is eight minutes a month. Automating it costs more than that to build and wants attention every time a system it touches changes. The exception is a low-volume step with a high consequence, or one that blocks other work while it waits. Frequency alone is not the test; frequency multiplied by consequence is.

Anything about to change

If the CRM is being replaced next quarter, or the process is already under review, automating against today’s shape buys a few weeks of benefit and a rebuild. “What is changing in the next six months?” is a cheap question.

Steps that should simply be deleted

A report nobody reads. A check the system already performs. An approval that has never once been refused. The question that finds these is “when was the output of this step last used, and by whom?” If nobody can answer, that is the finding, and deleting the step beats automating it.

There is standard advice I disagree with here: automate the most painful thing first. In my experience a process is painful precisely because it is tangled, exception-heavy and poorly understood, which makes it the worst available first build. I would rather start with the boring, high-volume one. What would change my mind is a date: if the painful step holds up something with a deadline on it, urgency beats tidiness.

What lands at the end of the week

The document is the deliverable. It has six parts:

  • The process as observed, step by step, with who does each step and roughly how long it takes.
  • The counts, each carrying the source it came from and the period it covers.
  • The steps proposed for automation — for each, what triggers it, what it does, and what happens when it fails. The last clause is not optional.
  • The steps explicitly left alone, with the reason, so nobody re-opens the argument in month three.
  • What I need from you and by when: access, a decision-maker, sample data.
  • A quote for the build, with a boundary drawn around it.

Then a decision, which is yours rather than mine: build it, fix the process by hand first and revisit, or stop. The document is yours in all three cases, and it still works if you take it in-house or give it to somebody else. A discovery that only makes sense if I do the build is a sales document.

Two honest limits. A week cannot find seasonality: a process that behaves differently at month end or in your busy season shows one face, and the document has to say which week it watched. Where that matters I ask for a longer export. And discovery does not remove surprises from the build; it makes them cheaper, because a surprise met against a written baseline is a change request rather than an argument.

The last thing worth saying is the least commercial. Sometimes the honest recommendation is that this should not be automated at all, and occasionally that there is no build worth quoting. I would rather lose the work than hand over something that entrenches a process that should have been removed. An automated bad process is harder to remove than a manual one: it stops having an operator who complains about it, and other things quietly start depending on its output. Week one is the cheapest week in which to find that out.
