How cybercrime cases are solved: from first alert to the courtroom

A breach or an online fraud starts two clocks at once — the victim's race to contain the damage, and the investigator's race to preserve the evidence. This is the full lifecycle of a cybercrime case: the incident-response track, the law-enforcement track, the digital forensics where they meet, and the courtroom where it all gets tested.

Two clocks start when something breaks

The moment a ransomware note appears on a screen, or money leaves an account it shouldn't have, two clocks start ticking. The first is the containment clock: stop the bleeding, get systems back, keep the business alive. The second is the evidence clock: everything you will ever be able to prove about what happened is decaying — logs are rotating, RAM is volatile, and stolen money is hopping between mule accounts.

These clocks pull in opposite directions, and most of the damage to a cybercrime case — as opposed to the damage from the crime itself — happens in the first few hours, before any forensic examiner or investigating officer arrives. Not through malice; through reflex. The IT team wipes the infected laptop. The embarrassed victim deletes the chat. The company "handles it quietly" and calls the police three weeks later, when the trail is stone cold.

By the time an expert shows up, the case has usually already been strengthened or crippled by people who never realized they were handling evidence.

This article walks the whole lifecycle: the victim's track (incident response), the law-enforcement track (complaint to charge sheet), the digital forensics where the two converge, and the admissibility rules that decide what survives in court. The legal specifics are Indian — the portal, the helpline, the statutes — but the discipline underneath is the same everywhere.

Track one: the victim side of the case

Incident response has a standard shape, and the one most teams borrow is the NIST lifecycle (SP 800-61): preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication and recovery, and post-incident activity. Real incidents loop through these phases rather than marching through them. The phase where cases are won or lost is containment — that is where the two clocks collide head-on.

The classic mistake has a name in every incident-response war story: wiping the compromised box before imaging it. Reimaging feels like decisive action, but it destroys the attacker's tools, persistence mechanisms, timestamps, and every artifact an examiner would have used to answer how they got in and what they took. You have recovered a computer and lost a case. Containment means isolating the threat, not incinerating the crime scene.

You do not need a forensics background to preserve evidence well in the first hours. You need restraint and a notebook:

  • Don't power off a live system as a reflex. RAM is volatile — encryption keys, active network connections, running malware, and logged-in sessions all vanish at power-off. Pulling the plug can still be the right call (say, files are visibly being encrypted), but it should be a deliberate decision, not a reflex.
  • Isolate from the network instead. Unplug the cable, disable Wi-Fi, or shut the switch port. The machine stays alive and full of evidence; the attacker loses their connection.
  • Start a written timeline immediately. Who noticed what, at what time, and every action anyone takes from that moment — kept somewhere other than the compromised system. Months later, this contemporaneous log is the difference between testimony and guesswork.
  • Preserve logs before they rotate. Firewalls, servers, proxies, and CCTV DVRs overwrite themselves on fixed cycles — sometimes days. Export now, ask questions later.
  • Stop touching the machine. "Just checking what happened" opens files, updates timestamps, and overwrites the deleted data an examiner might have recovered. Every click is an edit to the crime scene.

Eradication and recovery come after the evidence is secured — rebuild from clean sources, rotate credentials, close the hole. The post-incident review feeds back into preparation, where an evidence-aware runbook gets written for next time.

Reporting in India: where, and how fast

Reporting is where the victim track hands the baton to the state, and in India the machinery is more capable than most people assume — if you reach it fast.

For financial fraud — UPI scams, card fraud, account takeovers, "digital arrest" extortion — call 1930, the national cybercrime helpline, run 24x7 by the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) under the Ministry of Home Affairs, or file on the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in. Behind both sits the Citizen Financial Cyber Frauds Reporting and Management System, launched in 2021, which pushes your complaint to the banks and payment intermediaries in the transaction chain so funds can be frozen while still in transit between mule accounts. Government figures put the money saved this way in the thousands of crores — but the freeze only works before the cash is layered onward or withdrawn, a window measured in hours, sometimes minutes.

The portal takes every category of cybercrime, not just financial — with a dedicated, anonymous-option track for crimes against women and children. You can also walk into your local cyber cell or any police station: since the new criminal procedure code, FIRs can be registered electronically, and the "Zero FIR" principle means no station can turn you away for being in the wrong jurisdiction — the FIR is registered and transferred.

Companies have an extra, separate obligation: under CERT-In's directions of 28 April 2022 (issued under Section 70B(6) of the IT Act), covered entities must report specified cyber incidents to CERT-In within six hours of noticing them, and maintain ICT system logs for 180 days within India. Note the distinction — CERT-In reporting is a regulatory duty, not a criminal complaint; a company that wants an investigation still needs to file one.

Track two: the law-enforcement side

Once a complaint lands, the police track follows a sequence that has not fundamentally changed since long before computers — information, investigation, evidence, charge — with new tools bolted on at each step.

Complaint to FIR. For cognizable offences — the serious ones, where police can investigate without a magistrate's order, which covers most substantive cybercrime such as cheating by personation, identity theft, and hacking — the information is registered as a First Information Report under Section 173 of the BNSS (the successor to Section 154 CrPC). For non-cognizable matters, a Non-Cognizable Report (NCR) is recorded instead under Section 174, and investigation needs a magistrate's nod. The FIR is the formal ignition of the case; its timing and contents will be scrutinized at trial.

Jurisdiction, and the foreign-server problem. Cybercrime smears itself across the map — the victim in one state, the mule accounts in three others, the server in another country. Domestically, Zero FIR and transfer procedures paper over the turf questions. Across borders it gets genuinely hard: evidence held abroad is formally obtained through Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties or a court-issued letter of request (letters rogatory) under Section 112 of the BNSS (formerly Section 166A CrPC) — a government-to-government channel that is thorough, slow, and often measured in months to years. Practically, investigators send preservation requests to platforms early (so the data survives the wait) and lean on the domestic footprint every scam needs anyway: Indian bank accounts, Indian SIMs, delivery addresses, local money mules.

The investigating officer's toolkit. The workhorse is Section 94 of the BNSS, under which a court — or the officer in charge of a police station — can order any person to produce documents or electronic communications, explicitly including devices likely to contain digital evidence. This is how bank KYC records, call detail records (CDRs), IP detail records (IPDRs), and platform logs are pulled into a case. Higher-powered instruments exist under the IT Act — Section 69 (interception, monitoring, decryption) and Section 69A (blocking) — but these are government-level powers requiring authorization at Home Secretary rank, not everyday police tools; in practice 69A is what removes phishing sites and fraud apps from circulation. For financial fraud, the 1930/NCRP ecosystem does the fast work: layered freezing of amounts as they hop through the mule chain, with courts later ordering restitution from frozen balances.

Seizure, done properly. When devices are seized, procedure is everything, because procedure is what the defence will attack. A seizure memo (panchnama) is drawn up on the spot with independent witnesses; devices are photographed in place, described with make and serial numbers, and sealed with numbered seals. Where the setup allows, a cryptographic hash of storage is computed at or immediately after seizure. Phones go into airplane mode or Faraday bags so they cannot be remotely wiped. Every one of these boring steps exists because its absence has sunk a real case somewhere.

FSL, charge sheet, trial. Sealed exhibits travel to a Forensic Science Laboratory with a forwarding memo; an examiner images and analyzes them and issues a report that enters the case as expert evidence. If the investigation holds together, the officer files a charge sheet before the court, and the trial begins — where the IO is cross-examined on the seizure, the examiner on the method, and the paperwork on its gaps. Everything the next three sections describe is what that cross-examination is really about.

Where the tracks meet: digital forensics

Whether evidence arrives from a company's incident responders or a police seizure, it flows into the same discipline. Digital forensics is where the two tracks converge — and where a case's provability is decided.

Forensics has four canonical phases, and every methodology you will meet is a variation on them: identification (what could hold evidence — devices, accounts, logs, cloud), preservation and acquisition (freeze it in a provable state), examination and analysis (find and interpret what matters), and reporting and presentation (explain it so a court can rely on it). The center of gravity is acquisition, because everything downstream inherits its integrity.

Imaging is not copying. Copying grabs files. A forensic image is a bit-for-bit duplicate of the entire medium — including deleted files, slack space, and unallocated clusters where the interesting things live. The evidence drive is connected through a write blocker, hardware that physically prevents the examiner's workstation from modifying the source, because even mounting a disk normally writes to it. The image lands in a raw format (dd) or a container like E01 (the EnCase evidence format — compression, embedded case metadata, and internal per-block checksums), both long accepted in courts. The original is then sealed and stored; all analysis happens on copies.

Hashing is the integrity proof. At acquisition, the examiner computes a cryptographic hash of the image — today preferably SHA-256, since MD5 and SHA-1 have known collision attacks, though many labs still record MD5 alongside for continuity with older tooling and case records. The property that matters in court is simple: recompute the hash at any later date, and a match demonstrates mathematically that not one bit has changed since acquisition. That single line — "hash values tallied" — is what turns a pile of copied data into evidence with provable integrity. A mismatch is not automatically tampering (failing sectors on a dying drive can cause it), but it is always an explanation the prosecution now owes.

What gets collected first, and why

Not all evidence decays at the same speed, so examiners collect in order of volatility — a principle old enough to be codified in RFC 3227: CPU registers and caches first (practically unobtainable), then RAM, then swap and temporary files, then disk, then backups and archival media. The rule of thumb: capture what dies at power-off before touching what survives it.

This is why live forensics exists. If a production server cannot be taken down, or — increasingly common — the disk is fully encrypted so that powering off locks the evidence forever, the examiner acquires memory from the running system first: encryption keys, running processes, active network connections, and injected code that never touches disk all live only in RAM. Then, and only then, is the power decision made.

Mobile devices are their own specialty. A logical extraction takes what the operating system is willing to hand over — contacts, chats, media, app data; a file-system extraction goes deeper; a physical extraction images the storage bit-for-bit, including deleted data — increasingly difficult on modern encrypted handsets. Often the richest source is not the handset at all but its cloud shadow: backups, synced photos, account tokens, and app data on servers, reachable through legal process. Meanwhile the seized phone sits in a Faraday bag precisely so no one can remote-wipe it while the paperwork moves.

Network forensics supplies the connective tissue: server and application logs, firewall and proxy records, NetFlow summaries, and provider records — the CDRs and IPDRs that map an IP address, port, and timestamp to a subscriber. The practitioner's caveat: with carrier-grade NAT, one public IP is shared by thousands of users, so an IP alone identifies nobody — you need the source port and the exact time. Unrecorded time zones and drifting clocks have genuinely derailed investigations, which is why careful examiners normalize everything to UTC and document the time source for every log they touch.

Artifacts, anti-forensics, and the chain of custody

Cases rarely crack on one dramatic file. They crack on small artifacts the user never knew existed:

  • Browser history and downloads — the searches made before the act, and the tools fetched to commit it.
  • USB registry traces — Windows records the make, model, and serial number of every USB storage device ever connected, with timestamps. Data-theft cases are routinely built on this alone.
  • Prefetch and execution artifacts — proof that a particular program (say, a wiping tool) ran, and when.
  • EXIF metadata — the camera model, timestamp, and sometimes GPS coordinates embedded in photos.
  • Recycle-bin and shell metadata — what was deleted or browsed, and when.
  • Email headers and provider IP logs — where a message really came from, as opposed to where it claims.

Attackers know this, so they attempt anti-forensics — and each technique has a counter. Wiping tools destroy data but leave their own traces: installation records, execution artifacts, and a suspiciously clean gap where activity should be. Timestomping (faking file timestamps) trips over the fact that NTFS keeps duplicate timestamp sets internally, and inconsistencies between them flag manipulation. Encryption is the strongest play, which is exactly why examiners prioritize live acquisition and cloud copies. But the deepest counter is corroboration: one device can be made to lie, but the device, the ISP's logs, the bank's records, and the CCTV footage rarely all lie in the same direction. Examiners build timelines across independent sources precisely so that no single manipulated artifact carries the case.

Binding all of it together is the chain of custody — worth describing concretely, because it sounds abstract until you see one. It is a document trail that rides with each exhibit: exhibit number, description and serial numbers, the hash, who collected it, where and when; then every single handover — seizing officer to property room (the malkhana), to the FSL courier, to the examiner, back to storage — signed and timestamped, with seal numbers recorded at each step. The defence does not have to prove tampering; it only has to point at a gap — an unlogged weekend, a broken seal, a hash nobody recorded — and ask the court to doubt everything after it. Prosecutions have collapsed on exactly that. The paperwork is not bureaucracy around the evidence; it is what makes it evidence.

Will it stand in court? Admissibility in India

Electronic records almost always reach a courtroom as copies — printouts, exported logs, forensic images — and Indian law treats such copies as admissible only with a certificate from someone responsible for the system that produced them, identifying the record and describing how it was made. For decades that meant Section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. Since 1 July 2024, the Evidence Act has been replaced by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, and the electronic-records rule now lives in Section 63 of the BSA — same core framework, with a prescribed certificate format in the Schedule (Part A filled by the party producing the record, Part B by an expert) that expects a hash of the record to be stated.

The controlling judgment is Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (2020) 7 SCC 1, where the Supreme Court settled years of conflicting rulings: the certificate is mandatory for secondary electronic evidence — oral testimony cannot substitute for it — while an original device produced in court does not need one. The practical lesson upstream is blunt: obtain the certificate when the record is extracted, from the person operating the system at that time, rather than hunting for a long-departed administrator on the eve of trial. As of this writing (August 2026), courts are still building Section 63 jurisprudence and older cases continue under the 65B regime, so exact practice varies between courts — but no version of it forgives a missing certificate.

The substantive offences themselves — what the accused is actually charged with — live in the IT Act and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, which deserve their own article: India's IT law, explained.

What to do differently, whoever you are

If you run a company: write an evidence-aware IR runbook before you need it — image before you reimage, name an evidence custodian, keep logs beyond the 180-day regulatory floor, and decide in advance who calls counsel and when (early — privilege and notification duties both reward it). And do not negotiate or settle the evidence away: quietly paying a fraudster or scrubbing systems to avoid embarrassment doesn't just end your case, it can create fresh liability.

If you're an individual victim: preserve first, then report — full-screen screenshots with the URL and timestamp visible, exported chats, saved SMS and emails, transaction IDs. Do not delete anything, even the embarrassing parts; investigators have seen it all, and the deleted message is always the one that mattered. Then report in hours, not days — call 1930 for financial fraud, because the freeze mechanism only works while the money is still moving through the chain. Shame is the scammer's best friend; speed is yours.

If you want to do this work: understand that the discipline is the job. Document everything, contemporaneously — if it isn't written down, it didn't happen. Hash everything, never work on originals, and assume from minute one that a hostile cross-examiner will one day read your notes aloud. The technical skills are learnable; the habit of treating every action as testimony is what makes findings survive court.

That is the honest shape of how cybercrime cases get solved. Not one genius move at a keyboard, but two clocks managed well from the first hour: contain without destroying, report while the trail is warm, image before you touch, hash what you image, and log every hand that touches the evidence. The courtroom at the end of the lifecycle only ever sees what survived the beginning of it.

A necessary disclaimer: this article is an educational orientation, not legal advice. Procedures, section numbers, and timelines vary by jurisdiction, by agency, and by case — and they change over time. If you are dealing with a live incident, speak to a lawyer and your local cyber cell rather than relying on any article, including this one.
