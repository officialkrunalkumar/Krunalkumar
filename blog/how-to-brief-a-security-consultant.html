How to brief a security consultant so the quote is accurate

A vague brief does not get you a cheap quote. It gets you a wide one, because the consultant is pricing the range of things your system might turn out to be — and you pay for that range either way. This is what to put in the brief, and why each item moves the number.

The width of the quote is the guess

Send the same one-line request to three security firms — “we need a security test of our platform” — and the numbers that come back are usually hard to compare. The instinct is to read the spread as one firm being greedy and another being cheap. Sometimes that is exactly what it is. More often all three read the same vague sentence, made different assumptions about what sits behind it, and priced their own assumptions.

Here is the part that is easy to miss as a buyer. When a consultant cannot see the size of the work, they do not price the work. They price the work plus the range of things it might turn out to be. That padding is not dishonest — it is the only rational response to a request for a fixed number covering an unknown quantity. You pay for it either way: if the job turns out small you overpaid, and if it turns out large you get a change request anyway, because the padding was a guess too.

A vague brief does not buy you a cheap quote. It buys you an expensive one with the risk priced in, or a cheap one from somebody who has not understood the job yet.

So the width of a quote is information: it says the consultant cannot see the edges of your system. The useful response is not to negotiate the number down but to remove the uncertainty that produced it. That costs nothing, and you are the only person who can do it.

Four things you might be buying

Before any technical detail, answer one question in a sentence: what has to be true when this is over? There are four common answers, and they are four different engagements. The word “pentest” gets used for all of them, which is how a buyer ends up paying adversarial-test rates for a list of patches, or handing a list of patches to a customer who wanted a signed report.

What you actually want What it is usually called What the deliverable should be
Evidence for a customer or prospect Third-party assessment A dated report from a named firm, with a scope statement their security team can read
An artefact for an auditor Assessment against a named control Findings mapped to the control text, in the format the auditor accepts
To know whether somebody determined gets in Penetration test, or red team if people and process are included A narrative of what was attempted, what worked, and what was never reached
A ranked list of things to fix Security review or assessment Ticket-ready findings with reproduction steps and a severity each

Only the third is a penetration test in the strict sense, and the only one where “we did not find a way in” is a result rather than a disappointment. If what you need is the fourth — which, in my experience, is what most teams need first — say so, and you will get broader coverage for the same money; I wrote separately about what a security review actually finds. If the driver is an audit, the control text decides the scope rather than you, and security compliance, explained covers which frameworks ask for what.

Scope is an inventory, not a sentence

“Our platform” is not a scope. A scope is an inventory with numbers in it, because the person quoting is doing arithmetic on counts. Names help them prepare; counts let them price. If you can only supply one, supply the counts.

  • Domains and subdomains. How many, and whether you believe the list is complete.
  • Public IPs or ranges. Roughly how many live hosts, not how many addresses you own.
  • Web applications. How many, and roughly how many distinct user flows each — sign-up, checkout, admin, reporting. Flows price better than screens.
  • APIs. How many endpoints, whether a specification exists, and whether it is current.
  • Mobile apps. Which platforms, and whether you can supply builds and test accounts.
  • Cloud accounts. How many, and whether you want a configuration review alongside the testing or instead of it.
  • Explicitly out of scope. The office network, staff laptops, the marketing site somebody else hosts, the company you acquired and have not integrated.

That last line is the highest-value sentence in the document, and the one people skip because it feels negative. Without it you will receive findings about systems you did not want tested, and pay for the time that produced them.

If you do not know a count, say so. A brief that says “roughly forty subdomains, and we are not confident that list is complete” beats one implying a precision you do not have. Hidden uncertainty gets priced as the worst case; declared uncertainty gets priced as a short discovery step you can watch happen.

The inventory has a second use. The same list of components, trust boundaries and data flows is what a threat model needs, and building one first tends to expose the parts of your system nobody can describe out loud. The STRIDE tool here runs entirely in your browser.

Environment and access move the number most

Production or staging

Say which. If it is staging, say honestly how representative it is: same code version, same configuration, same filtering in front of it, comparable data volume. Testing a staging environment three months behind production produces a report about staging. That is often a reasonable thing to buy, but write down that it is what was bought, because a customer reading the report a year later will assume it means production.

Credentials and roles

This moves the effort more than almost anything else on the list. There are three broad positions: unauthenticated only, one authenticated role, or every role including administrator and a second tenant. Each role you add is close to another pass over the application, because the interesting authorisation bugs live between roles — can a viewer perform an editor’s action, can tenant A see tenant B. I have not measured this against a large sample, so treat it as a rule of thumb: roles behave like a multiplier on application testing effort, not an addition to it.

Which is why I will say plainly that grey box — you hand over accounts, the subdomain list, the API specification and an architecture sketch — usually gives more findings per rupee than pure black box. In a black box engagement the opening stretch goes on rebuilding a map of your own system at consultant rates, and that map is not the deliverable you wanted.

What would change my mind: if the question you are genuinely asking is “could an outsider with no help get in”, black box measures exactly that, and handing over the map destroys the measurement. That is a legitimate question, just narrower than most buyers think, and a poor first engagement for a team that has never had a credentialed review.

One practical line belongs in the brief too: who provisions the test accounts, whether email verification and any second factor work for them, and whether your own lockout rules will disable them on the first morning. In my experience the most common avoidable way to burn budget is a tester waiting for a login.

Constraints, third parties and the 2am call

Three sets of constraints belong in writing, and only one of them is technical.

What you cannot authorise

You can only authorise testing of systems you control. Your payment processor, your CRM, your hosting provider, the analytics script on your checkout page and the SaaS your product integrates with are not yours to consent for. Most publish a testing policy, and several require notice or their own written approval first.

Warning. Authorising a test that reaches somebody else’s infrastructure is a legal problem, not a paperwork one, and the exposure lands on you as well as on the tester. List every third-party component in the path and name who owns each. Uncertainty about what is yours belongs in the brief, not in the engagement.

Windows, rate limits and the WAF

Say when traffic may be sent and who has been told. If your on-call rota has not been told, somebody will spend a night on an incident that turns out to be the test you paid for. Then decide explicitly about the web application firewall, because leaving it implicit answers a question you did not ask:

  • Filtering on measures what an attacker faces today, including whatever the WAF stops.
  • Filtering off, or the tester allowlisted, measures what your application actually is underneath it.

Both are valid and they answer different questions. I would allowlist the tester so the application itself gets examined, then spend a short block at the end with filtering live. Leaving it on by accident and having the tester blocked on day one measures your blocklist and nothing else.

Denial of service is nearly always out of scope, and it should be. It is destructive, and it is rarely what people mean when they ask whether the system falls over. If capacity is the question, buy a load test.

The 2am finding

Agree in advance what happens when something critical turns up outside office hours. Two questions, answered in writing: does the tester stop, and who do they wake. Name a person, a number and a fallback. Settle one more thing while you are there — what happens if the tester finds evidence of a compromise that predates them. That is not a finding, it is an incident, and the engagement changes shape the moment it appears.

The deliverable is part of the scope

A report has an audience, and the audience is a scoping decision. An executive summary for a prospect’s procurement team is a different document from a finding list your engineers turn straight into tickets. A good consultant can produce both, but only cheaply if you ask before the writing starts; asking afterwards means a rewrite that somebody pays for.

Say who reads it, then ask for these specifics:

  • Reproduction steps and evidence per finding. A finding your engineers cannot reproduce turns into an argument about whether it is real.
  • A stated severity scheme. Ask which one. CVSS is the common answer and a reasonable one, but it scores the vulnerability, not your business: a 9.8 on a service holding nothing can matter less than a 5.3 on the system holding everything. Treat it as an input to prioritisation rather than the prioritisation. If the vector strings mean nothing to you, the CVSS calculator here takes one apart.
  • A coverage statement. What was reached, what was not, and why. A clean report with no coverage statement is not evidence of anything.
  • A machine-readable copy. A CSV or JSON export of findings goes into your tracker. A PDF goes into a folder.

Retest

Ask whether a retest of fixes is included, how many findings it covers, how long you have to use it, and whether it produces an updated report. That last part is the one buyers forget: for a customer or an auditor, a document saying the criticals were remediated and verified on a date is worth more than the original saying they existed. Retest windows are commonly quoted in weeks or months, but I have not surveyed the market, so ask rather than assume.

The questions you should be asked back

You can judge a great deal from the questions that come back at you. A consultant who reads a one-paragraph email and returns a firm number without asking anything is quoting a template. That is not necessarily dishonest — a template price for a template job is a real business model — but the number is not about you, and it will move once the work starts.

The questions I would expect, roughly in this order:

  1. What decision does this report support, and who reads it?
  2. How many domains, applications, endpoints and accounts — and how confident are you in those counts?
  3. What is explicitly out of scope?
  4. Production or staging, and how far apart are they?
  5. Which roles can I have, who provisions the accounts, and by when?
  6. Which components are third-party, and who authorises testing against them?
  7. Has this been tested before, and may I read the previous report?
  8. Who do I call at 2am, and does the work stop while I wait?

The seventh is the most revealing. If you decline to share the previous report, understand what you are buying: rediscovery. Part of your budget goes on finding what somebody already told you.

Common advice says get three quotes and compare. I only half agree. Comparing three numbers produced from three different readings of a vague brief is close to meaningless — you are comparing assumptions, and the cheapest assumption wins for the wrong reason. Send the same detailed brief to all three and the comparison becomes real, and what remains differs on method, seniority and deliverable.

A brief you can copy

I have been on both sides of this document: writing briefs as a buyer, and reading them as the person who has to turn one into a number. The briefs that got the best prices were never the ones that sounded most confident. They were the ones that were specific where they could be and honest where they could not.

Copy this, fill it in, delete what does not apply, and send it with the request:

OBJECTIVE
  What has to be true when this is over:
  Who reads the report:
  Deadline, and what is driving the deadline:

INVENTORY (counts, plus how confident we are in each)
  Domains / subdomains:
  Public IPs or live hosts:
  Web applications, and flows per application:
  APIs, and whether a current spec exists:
  Mobile apps and platforms:
  Cloud accounts or subscriptions:

EXPLICITLY OUT OF SCOPE:

ENVIRONMENT
  Production or staging:
  If staging, how it differs from production:

ACCESS
  Roles available:
  Who provisions accounts, and by when:
  Source code, architecture notes, API spec: yes or no

THIRD PARTIES
  Component, owner, who authorises:

CONSTRAINTS
  Permitted testing window:
  WAF and rate limiting: on, off, or tester allowlisted
  Denial of service: out of scope
  Anything fragile you should not touch, and why:

RULES OF ENGAGEMENT
  Our contact, phone, out-of-hours fallback:
  On a critical finding: stop or continue, and who to call:
  On evidence of an existing compromise:

DELIVERABLE
  Format and audience:
  Severity scheme:
  Coverage statement: required
  Retest: included, how many findings, by when, updated report?

That is one page, and it is the difference between a number you can trust and a number that is mostly padding. A scope honest about its uncertainty gets a better price than one that hides it, because declared uncertainty can be bounded and quoted as a small piece of discovery, while hidden uncertainty gets priced as the worst thing it might be. The same logic runs the other way when you are the one quoting, which I wrote about in scope the work before you quote it.
