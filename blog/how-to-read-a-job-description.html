How to read a job description

A posting looks like a document with an author, and it almost never has one. Some of what is in there is a real requirement, some is a wish somebody added in a meeting, and some is a warning about the job that nobody meant to publish. Reading it literally is the most common expensive mistake I see in the people I mentor. This is how to read one instead.

Who actually writes a posting

Start with the production process, because everything else follows from it. A job posting is assembled rather than written. The hiring manager describes roughly what is needed. A recruiter reshapes it into the format the careers page expects and adds the keywords the applicant tracking system will search on. Somebody in HR pastes in the standard paragraphs. Two people who will work with the hire add a line each, usually about the thing that hurt them last quarter. A senior person adds something aspirational. And whoever is short of time copies half of the last posting for a different role, because that file is the one already open.

The result is a union of everybody's preferences rather than an intersection of what the job requires. Nobody in that chain is being dishonest. It is just that no single person owns the total, and nobody is accountable for the list being shorter than it needs to be.

A second force keeps it inflated. Everyone on the writing side knows the list is negotiable, so adding a line costs nothing and removing one feels like giving something away. Asking for more than you need is free; asking for exactly what you need risks getting less. When both sides understand that convention, the inflation is harmless. The person it hurts is the reader who does not know the convention exists — and that reader is disproportionately the one applying for their first or second job.

A third force is mechanical. Some requirements are there to be filterable rather than because anybody believes them. "Bachelor's degree in Computer Science or a related field" is very often a checkbox with a filter behind it, not a considered position on degrees. So is a years-of-experience number that ends in a zero or a five.

There is an important exception, and it is worth learning to spot because it inverts all the advice below. Some organisations do write a literal list: government roles, regulated roles, anything with a statutory licence, defence, parts of banking and healthcare. In those, the list is the list, because someone will later have to defend the shortlist against it. You can usually tell from the form of the document — formal register, numbered criteria, and an explicit split between essential and desirable. When a posting draws that distinction, believe it. Somebody did the work of sorting, and they are telling you the answer.

The cost of reading it literally

There is a statistic that gets attached to this subject everywhere, usually with the words "research shows" in front of it: that men apply when they meet about sixty per cent of the listed qualifications and women apply only when they meet all of them. I am not going to use it, and it is worth saying why, because the reason is the same discipline this whole article is about.

That number traces back to an internal Hewlett-Packard report, described second-hand in a book. There is no published method behind it, no sample, no measurement. It has been repeated often enough to have acquired the texture of a finding, which is what happens to a number that is convenient and memorable. Repeating it would be the same mistake as taking a requirements list at face value: treating something assembled for one purpose as though it were evidence for another.

What is better supported is smaller and more useful. In 2014 Tara Mohr ran a survey for the Harvard Business Review asking people who had decided not to apply for a job why they had not. The dominant answer was not a lack of confidence in their ability. It was, in effect, a belief about the process: that applying without meeting the stated qualifications would be a waste of time because they would be filtered out anyway. People were not doubting themselves. They were modelling the hiring system, and modelling it wrong.

That distinction is the whole reason this article is worth writing. You cannot argue someone into confidence. You can correct a model.

I see this every cohort. The people who do not apply are usually the careful ones. They have read the posting properly, taken it at its word, found the line they cannot honestly claim, and withdrawn — on a line the hiring manager would have waived in a heartbeat, and in several cases did waive for whoever they hired instead. The people who did apply were not more qualified. They assumed the list was a starting position, and they were right.

One caveat I owe you, because the correction has an obvious failure mode. The answer is not to apply to everything. Applications that are worth anything are tailored, tailoring takes an hour, and eight tailored applications comfortably beat forty generic ones. The point is not to apply more. It is to apply to a different set, chosen by a better rule than "do I match every bullet". The rest of this article is that rule.

Which requirements are load-bearing

The single most reliable test I know takes about two minutes and needs nothing but the posting itself: a requirement that is real usually appears twice.

Almost every posting has two halves. One describes the work — "what you'll do", "day to day", "responsibilities". The other lists requirements. The first half is nearly always written by someone describing an actual job, because it is hard to invent a day. The second half is where the committee left its fingerprints. So read them against each other. If a requirement is used by something in the description of the work, it is load-bearing. If it appears in the bullet list and is never picked up anywhere else, nobody wrote a day of this job that needs it.

Concretely: if the responsibilities say you will own the billing service and its schema migrations, then "three years with Postgres" is real. If the responsibilities never mention infrastructure and the bullets ask for Kubernetes, Terraform, Kafka and Spark, that is an inventory of the environment you will work near, not a list of things you will be doing.

Four secondary signals sharpen the same judgement:

  • Specificity. "Experience with Django's ORM under multi-tenant load" was written by a person with a scar. "Familiarity with modern web frameworks" was written by a person filling in a template. The specific line is nearly always the honest one, and it is also the one worth answering first.
  • Position. The first three bullets are usually the hiring manager's. The bottom third of a long list is where the additions accumulate, and it is where the wishes live.
  • The modal verb. Most postings use "must have", "should have" and "familiarity with" roughly consistently, even when nobody agreed to a convention. It is a free signal and people skim past it.
  • Years of experience. The softest thing on the page. It is a proxy for judgement that nobody has a way to measure directly. If you ask a hiring manager why five years rather than four and there is no answer, it was never a requirement — and you can ask exactly that, politely, on the call.

One more distinction is worth more than all four of those: domain versus tool. Tools are learnable in weeks by anyone who has learned a comparable tool. A domain is not. When a posting asks for both, the domain requirement is almost always the real one. A payments team will forgive you for not knowing their queue technology and will not forgive you for not knowing what a chargeback is, because one of those is a fortnight and the other is a year of being wrong in meetings.

Then count only the load-bearing ones. Meet most of those and you are a candidate, however short you fall on the rest. Meet a dozen bullets while none of the described work resembles anything you have done, and you are a keyword match, which is a slower and more disappointing way of being turned down. That is also why generic keyword-stuffing does so little: it optimises for the half of the document that was never the decision.

The phrases that carry information

A certain set of phrases get treated online as automatic red flags, which is lazy in both directions — it throws away good jobs and it gives no help with the genuinely bad ones. Each of these actually means something. Several of them describe real conditions that suit some people very well. What follows is what each one usually indicates, and the one question that tells you which version you are looking at.

  • "You'll wear many hats." Almost always literally true, and usually said in good faith. It means the company cannot yet afford specialisation. The real question is whether the hats are by design or by attrition. Ask which hats this role has worn in the last three months. An honest early-stage answer is concrete and slightly chaotic — support tickets, a pricing page, an integration. A vague answer means the hats are unbounded, which is the version that eats people. This is an excellent role if you want range quickly and a poor one if you want to get deep at a single craft.
  • "Fast-paced environment." Sometimes it is a plain description of the tempo — agencies, trading, adtech, incident response. Sometimes it is a euphemism for reactive: no roadmap, and priorities set by whoever escalated most recently. The question that separates them is about horizon, not speed. Ask whether they know what this team will be working on in six weeks. Fast and planned is a fine place to work. Fast and unplanned will spend you, and the spending is invisible until it is done.
  • "Rockstar", "ninja", "guru". This tells you less about the culture than people claim and more about the document. It means the posting was assembled from a template that is a decade old and nobody senior read it closely. Downgrade your confidence in the rest of the posting's precision accordingly — but it is weak evidence about the team, and refusing to apply over a word choice made by a recruiter you will never meet is a poor trade.
  • "We're like a family here." The one I trust least, and not because families are bad. It is the metaphor's implications that do the damage. Families do not hold performance conversations, do not have boundaries around hours, and cannot be left without it reading as a betrayal. Most companies that say it mean warmth and mean it sincerely; the risk is that the same metaphor gets used later, to you, to argue against a boundary. The question is deliberately mundane: how does somebody here take two consecutive weeks of leave? A concrete answer with a handover process is warmth without the trap.
  • "Must be comfortable with ambiguity." One of the most honest phrases in recruiting when it is meant. It says: nobody will tell you what to do, the requirements are a conversation you will have to lead, and part of the job is deciding what the job is. Some people are markedly happier there. It is only a warning if you need defined scope to do good work, which is a legitimate way to be and not a deficiency. The test is where authority sits. Ask who decides what this role works on next quarter. "You'll work that out with the founder" is ambiguity with access, and that is workable. A vague answer with no name in it is ambiguity without authority — accountable for outcomes you cannot direct, which is the configuration that burns people out fastest.
  • An unusually wide salary band. A range twice as wide as everyone else's for the same title has three common explanations. It genuinely covers two levels and they would hire either, and a pay-transparency rule obliges them to publish something. Or they have not decided what the role is, and the band is the uncertainty made visible. Or the top of the band exists for one candidate they already have in mind. Ask what distinguishes someone at the bottom of that band from someone at the top. A company with real levels answers in one breath.
  • A role that merges two jobs. "Data analyst / data engineer". "Designer who codes". "DevOps and security". "Marketing and sales". There is an honest version: at their size the work really is one person's, and the combination is a genuine craft that some people are excellent at. There is an unhealthy version: two people left, or a second headcount was refused, and the requisition absorbed both. The question that separates them is about the split. Ask roughly what the ratio is, and which side wins if it turns out to be eighty-twenty. An honest hybrid has an immediate answer. A merged requisition does not, because two stakeholders each believe they are getting a whole person. That is the same failure as quoting work before the deliverable is agreed, and it fails the same way: not at the start, but in month four when both parties discover they meant different things.
  • A stack list naming twelve technologies. Almost never twelve requirements. It is usually inventory, and the only question that matters is which three you will touch in the first month. There is one specifically bad version, though, and it is easy to miss: when the twelve are mutually redundant. Three cloud providers. Two ORMs. Four CI systems. That is not a stack, it is an archaeology site, and it is a reliable prediction about your first year — you will spend it in the seams between migrations that were never finished.

Two more that are worth half a sentence each. "Self-starter" and "hit the ground running" usually mean there is no onboarding, which is survivable if you know it in advance and ask what the first week looks like. And "competitive salary" with no number means the number is either undecided or hard to defend; it is not sinister, but it does move the cost of finding out onto you.

What is missing that should worry you

The absences are better evidence than the phrases. A clumsy phrase can be one person's bad writing day. An absence is a decision, or a gap in what the writer knows — and both of those are about the organisation rather than the document.

  • No salary or range. In markets where publishing is voluntary this is ordinary and means little on its own. Where publishing is required and the range is absent or absurdly wide, the posting is either old or unfinished. What the absence reliably tells you is that you will spend several rounds before discovering whether you are in the same universe, and that cost is yours rather than theirs. That asymmetry, not rudeness, is the reason to raise it early.
  • No team size. The most common absence and the most under-noticed. "You'll join our engineering team" is four different jobs at three people, thirty, three hundred and three thousand. At three you are on call and there is no specialisation. At three hundred there is process, and your work is a slice of something larger. Neither is worse; they need different people, and they reward different instincts. The number is missing because the writer knows it and forgot that you do not.
  • No reporting line. "Reports to" is one line and is nearly always known. When it is missing, the honest possibilities are that it has not been decided — which means the role's mandate has not been decided either — or that it is about to change in a reorganisation, or that the manager is being hired in parallel. None of the three is disqualifying. All three change what you should ask for, and all three are much easier to discover before you accept than after.
  • No sense of what the first ninety days look like. This is the one I weight most heavily, in both directions. If nobody can say what a good first quarter looks like, then either nobody has thought about what this person will actually do, or several people have thought about it and disagree. You would be arriving into a role with no definition of done, and the first performance conversation will be somebody's improvised opinion of a job that was never specified. Conversely, its presence is one of the strongest positive signals a posting can carry: it means somebody sat down and thought about a human being rather than a requisition.

Two smaller absences worth noticing. A posting that never says what the team is currently struggling with is describing a job in the abstract, which usually means the writer was not close to the work. And a posting that has been live for six months without changing is telling you something — most often that the requirements and the budget were never reconciled.

The general rule under all of this. A job posting is a marketing document produced by a process, not a specification produced by a person. Read it the way you would read any document with several authors and no editor: pay attention to what recurs, discount what appears once, and treat the gaps as information rather than as oversights.

Mapping your experience onto their words

Between reading the posting and applying there is a piece of work most people skip, and the two common ways of skipping it fail differently. Mirror their vocabulary mechanically and you get a document full of their phrases with nothing underneath, which reads as hollow to the first human who opens it. Ignore their vocabulary entirely and describe your work in your own words, and you never reach a human at all.

The move is translation, not mirroring: their words for the concept, your evidence underneath it. In practice I ask people to make three columns for a posting they care about.

  1. Their phrase, verbatim. Copy it exactly, including the qualifier, because the qualifier is often where the real requirement hides.
  2. The closest thing you have actually done, in one sentence, with a number or an outcome in it.
  3. The honest distance between the two: same, adjacent, or not yet.

Then treat each row by its distance. Where it is the same, use their phrase — there is no prize for synonyms, and a reader scanning for a concept should find it where they expect it. Where it is adjacent, use their phrase and name the difference in the same sentence: "I have run the on-call rotation for a Django service on ECS; I have not run Kubernetes in production, though I have deployed to it in staging." That is more persuasive than either hiding the gap or apologising for it, because it demonstrates that you can assess your own level accurately — which is a large part of what an interviewer is trying to establish anyway. Where it is not yet, leave it off entirely, and if it was load-bearing, that is a real input into whether to apply.

The rule I will not soften: never put a technology on your CV that you would be embarrassed to be asked about. The interview finds it, and being caught inflating one line does not cost you that line. It makes a reasonable person re-audit every other line on the page, including the true ones.

There is a related translation problem around seniority. Titles are not comparable across companies — a "Software Engineer" at one place does what a "Senior" does at another, and the reverse happens just as often. So translate scope rather than title: how many people depended on your work, what you decided without asking permission, what broke and who fixed it, what you would have designed differently. That is legible everywhere. A title is legible only inside the company that issued it.

A tailored version per posting, without starting over each time. The resume maker on this site builds a structured CV in the browser and lets you keep several variants, which is what tailoring actually requires in practice — one base document and a reordered, reworded version per role. Nothing you type leaves your machine. It will not tell you what is true about your experience; that part is the three-column exercise above, and no tool does it for you.

What to ask on the call

The description is a claim. The call is where you test it. This is not the "do you have any questions for us" formality at the end — although that is where the questions land — it is a deliberate evidence-gathering exercise, and it changes what you should ask.

One principle does most of the work: ask about the last time, not about the general case. "How do you handle competing priorities" gets you a policy, which anyone can produce. "When did two things last collide, and what happened" gets you the actual mechanism, including who decided and how long it took. The general question is answered by the posting. The specific one cannot be.

Beyond the four in the diagram, three more earn their place:

  • "Walk me through the last two weeks of the person who would be my closest colleague." This tests "fast-paced", "wear many hats" and "comfortable with ambiguity" all at once, without asking a leading question that invites a reassuring answer. You are asking for a narrative, and narratives are hard to improvise convincingly.
  • "What has this team tried that did not work?" A team that cannot name a failure has either no institutional memory or no safety in admitting one. Both are things you would want to know before you are the person who has to admit the next one. The quality of this answer also tells you a great deal about how feedback travels there.
  • "What is the band, and what separates the bottom of it from the top?" Ask it plainly, late in the first call. The second half is the informative half.

And watch the meta-signal, which is often worth more than any single answer: how does the person react to being asked a factual question? This is the hour in which they are selling. If a straightforward question about team size, reporting line or the last failure produces defensiveness or fog now, that is data about the two thousand hours a year when nobody is selling anything. I would rather learn that in week zero than in month seven.

It is worth knowing what the traffic looks like in the other direction as well. I have written up the questions I ask when I am the one interviewing and what each of them is actually measuring, which is a reasonable way to see how a posting, a call and a decision connect at the other end of the table.

Is this the role they are hiring for

Three specific mismatches account for most of the cases where a posting and the actual opening have drifted apart. Each has a tell.

  • The posting describes the problem they had, not the one they have. Common wherever the listing has been up for months. Roles are written at a moment and the moment moves. The single most useful question in a first call is often just: has what you need from this role changed since this was written? The answer is frequently yes, sometimes substantially, and you are then discussing the real job while everyone else is still answering the old one.
  • The posting was written to justify a decision already made. An internal candidate, or a specific person in mind. The tells are an oddly precise combination of requirements — a named framework plus a named industry plus an unusual certificate — a very short application window, and prose that reads like a description of a person rather than a job. This is not always cynical; sometimes the need genuinely is that narrow. But if the precision looks arbitrary rather than technical, price your effort accordingly.
  • The title is one level away from the job. Read the responsibilities against the title. If the title says Senior but every responsibility is execution with no mention of design, review or mentoring, the title is doing work that the salary is not. The reverse happens too: a role labelled Engineer whose responsibilities include defining an architecture and mentoring two juniors is a senior job at a junior title. That can be a fine trade to make deliberately, early on, for the scope. It is a bad one to make by accident, because the title is what the next employer reads.

Finally, read the posting against other artefacts rather than on its own. The public profiles of people already on that team. The engineering blog, and how long since it was updated. Older postings for the same team, which show you what has changed. The product itself, used for twenty minutes. A posting is one document produced by a process; a company leaks its actual shape in a dozen other places, and none of those have been through the committee.

Applying anyway when it is a stretch

Here is the rule I would give someone I mentor, in full, because "apply anyway" on its own is advice that costs other people time.

Count only the load-bearing requirements. If you meet most of those, and you can imagine yourself doing the first ninety days as described, apply. If you meet many bullets and none of the load-bearing ones, do not — that is the keyword-match trap, and it wastes more of your effort than theirs.

Two conditions matter more than the count:

  • Can you show one piece of evidence for the hardest load-bearing requirement? One thing you built, shipped, broke and fixed, measured, or wrote up. One is enough. Zero is not, and no amount of framing substitutes for it.
  • Can you say the path from what you have done to what they need in two sentences? If you cannot say it, they cannot imagine it, and imagining it is entirely your job rather than theirs.

What not to do is apologise. "I know I don't meet all the requirements, but…" asks the reader to evaluate you against the list — the very document we just established was assembled by a committee. Lead with the closest real thing you have done. Name the gap once, factually, in one clause. Then move on to what you would do in the role. A covering letter on a stretch application has exactly one job, and it is not enthusiasm and not a summary of the CV: it is to make the leap legible. One paragraph on the adjacent thing you have done and why it transfers.

It is also worth knowing that a stretch application fails differently from a matched one. The matched one fails silently, in a pile. The stretch one occasionally succeeds out of all proportion, because a hiring manager who has read thirty candidates who all match the list is actively looking for a reason to be interested. That is not a strategy and I would not build a job search on it. It is a reason not to decide the answer is no before anybody has said it.

How to frame a gap

Two kinds of gap turn up in this conversation, and they are handled the same way. A gap in employment, and a gap in skills against a load-bearing requirement.

For time out of work, five things, in this order:

  1. Name it first, in one sentence, in your own words. Anything you leave for them to discover grows in the discovering. Anything you state plainly stays the size you stated it.
  2. Say what it was, not why it was, unless the why is short and unremarkable. "I took eighteen months out to care for a parent." "I was laid off in a company-wide reduction in March and have been looking since." Neither of those needs an essay attached, and attaching one signals that you think it needs defending.
  3. Say what you did in it, if you did something — and do not invent something if you did not. A certification is fine. A project is fine. "I rested, and I am ready to work now" is also fine and lands considerably better than a fabricated side project that collapses under one follow-up question.
  4. Say what you are doing now. The question underneath "what happened in 2025" is almost always "is this about to happen again next quarter". Answer that question directly and the gap stops being interesting, usually within thirty seconds.
  5. Do not stretch dates. Rounding a departure forward by four months to close a gap converts a non-event into a credibility problem the moment a reference or a background check disagrees with your CV.

For a skill gap, the structure is three clauses and the third is the one people leave out. "I have not done X. The closest thing I have done is Y, which shares the hard part. I would expect to be useful in about a month, and the evidence that I learn at roughly that rate is Z." That last clause is what makes the estimate believable rather than optimistic, and most people either skip it or replace it with an assurance about being a fast learner, which carries no information at all because everybody says it.

The honest limit: none of this helps against a screen that is purely a keyword filter. Some applications are rejected before a human reads a word, and no amount of good framing changes that. What framing fixes is the human conversation. The way past the filter is a referral, or a smaller company where a person reads every application — and both of those are usually worth more of your effort than another rewrite of the CV.

Questions people ask

Should I apply if I only meet half the requirements?

It depends entirely on which half. Sort the list first: the requirements that also appear in the description of the work are the ones being hired for, and the ones that appear nowhere else are usually preferences. If you meet most of the first group, apply. If you meet half the list but none of the load-bearing half, you are a keyword match rather than a candidate, and the application is a slower way of being turned down. The count on its own tells you nothing, which is why the count is the wrong thing to be looking at.

Is the statistic about men applying at sixty per cent and women at a hundred real?

Not as a study. It comes from an internal Hewlett-Packard report described second-hand in a book, with no published method, sample or measurement behind it, and it has been repeated so often that it now gets cited as research. What is better supported is a survey Tara Mohr ran for the Harvard Business Review in 2014, which asked people who had not applied why not. The most common reason given was not confidence — it was a belief that applying without meeting the stated qualifications would be a waste of effort because they would be screened out. That belief is a model of how hiring works, and models can be corrected.

What does it mean when a posting says competitive salary and gives no number?

Usually one of three things: the number has not been agreed internally, the range is wide enough that publishing it would invite questions, or the company prefers to anchor on whatever you say first. None of those are scandals. What it does mean, reliably, is that the cost of finding out is being moved onto you, in the form of interview rounds that may end at a number nobody could have worked with. That is the reason to ask early rather than a reason to walk away.

Is it rude to ask about salary on the first call?

No, and the recruiter is generally relieved, because they are also trying to avoid four wasted rounds. Ask it plainly and late in the call: what is the band for this role, and what separates someone at the bottom of it from someone at the top. The second half is the useful part, because a team with real levels answers it immediately and a team that invented the band answers it vaguely. If asking is treated as distasteful, you have learned something about how the raise conversation will go in eighteen months.

How do I explain a gap without making it the whole conversation?

Name it first, in one sentence, in your own words, without an apology, and then move to what you are doing now. What it was is usually enough; why it was is only needed when the why is short and unremarkable. Do not stretch dates to hide it, because an inconsistency discovered later converts a non-event into a credibility problem. The question underneath the question is almost always whether the same thing is about to happen again, so answering that directly is what makes the gap stop being interesting.

Is it worth applying to a posting that has been open for six months?

Sometimes, but read it differently. A long-open posting means one of a few things: the requirements are unrealistic for the budget, the role has been deprioritised and the listing left up, the team is collecting a pipeline rather than hiring, or the description no longer matches what they need. The last one is the interesting case and it is worth one question in the first message or call — has what you need from this role changed since this was written. If the answer is yes, you are now talking about the real job, and most applicants are still answering the old one.

Should I tailor my CV for every posting?

Tailor it for the ones you would actually take, and let the rest go. Eight tailored applications beat forty untailored ones, and tailoring does not mean rewriting — it means using their words for the concepts you already have evidence for, and reordering so the load-bearing requirements are answered in the first third of the page. What it must never mean is adding a technology you have not used. The interview finds it, and being caught inflating one line makes a reader re-audit every other line on the page.

None of this makes a posting trustworthy, and that is not the goal. The goal is to stop treating a committee's wish list as a verdict on whether you are allowed to apply. Sort it, count the part that is real, test the rest on the call, and let the company tell you which version of the job it is — rather than deciding on their behalf, from a document none of them would defend line by line.
