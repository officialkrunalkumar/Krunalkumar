How to read a privacy policy in five minutes

A privacy policy is a reference document laid out like an essay, which is why reading it from the top does not work. Here is the order I check things in, what each answer actually tells you, and how to test the document against the page it claims to describe.

Published 31 August 2026 · 10 min read · by Krunalkumar Shah

Search it, do not read it

A privacy policy is a reference document laid out like an essay. Read it from the top and you spend your attention on definitions and reassurance, and reach the paragraph that matters several minutes in, by which point you have stopped caring. That is not bad writing; it is what a document looks like when it must satisfy a regulator, a lawyer and a marketing team at once.

So do not read it. Search it. Open the page, press Ctrl+F — Cmd+F on a Mac — and run these terms in roughly this order. Each lands in the paragraph that answers one question.

  • third part — truncated on purpose, so one search catches “third party”, “third parties” and “third-party”.
  • share and sell — two different verbs for where your data goes. They sit in different paragraphs and do not mean the same thing.
  • affiliate — a company in the same corporate group. Sharing inside the group is often carved out of promises made elsewhere.
  • retain — how long they keep it.
  • delete — how you get out.
  • legal basis — says at once whether this is a GDPR-shaped policy.
  • transfer — catches cross-border transfers and the acquisition clause, the one nearly everybody misses.
  • children — the age line, and a fair proxy for how carefully the document was written.
  • change — how the policy itself can change underneath you.

Nine searches. A search that returns nothing is a finding too: a policy with no occurrence of “retain” has not told you how long it keeps your data.

Read sharing first, because that is the business model

Of the nine, sharing is the one to read properly and to read first. Most of a privacy policy is housekeeping: what a cookie is, that they use encryption, that they care about you. The sharing section is the business. If a company makes money from data rather than from you, this is where it has to say so.

Two different things hide under the same word. A processor — “service provider” in some policies — is a vendor acting on the company’s instructions and for its purposes only: the host, the payment gateway, the support desk. Data going there has not really left the company’s control. A third party is an independent recipient that decides its own purposes. Data going to a third party has left. The word to watch is “partner”, because it sits comfortably in a sentence while meaning either thing.

Which brings us to the sentence near the top of many policies: we do not sell your personal data. It is usually true and it is usually narrow. “Sale” is a defined term in the laws that made the sentence necessary, and the definitions turn on an exchange for consideration; California’s law had to add a separate concept of sharing for behavioural advertising because companies argued their ad deals were not sales. India’s DPDP Act does not use the idea of a sale at all; it turns on notice and consent. So it rules out one transaction and permits a good deal of movement around it: disclosure to processors, sharing for advertising, and the transfer clause further down. I am not a lawyer, but a narrow denial does narrow work.

Count the occurrences of “we may”. It is a permission the company grants itself, not a description of what it does. A policy written almost entirely in “we may” is a list of things allowed to happen to you, not of things that do.

Retention, and what “as long as necessary” means

Search retain and you will land on one of two sentences.

The first is some form of “we retain your personal data for as long as necessary to fulfil the purposes set out in this policy”. Read that as indefinitely. Those purposes are broad by construction, so a period pinned to them is not a period at all. Nothing in it creates a date on which anything is deleted, and nobody had to build the job that does it.

The second is a number. Ninety days after account closure. Server logs for thirty days. A stated period is, in my experience, the most reliable good sign in a privacy policy, and the reason is engineering rather than sincerity: to put a number in the document somebody had to know where the data lives, write the deletion job, and accept being wrong in public if it fails. Very few organisations do that for decoration.

Two things to check around a number: whether it is given per category or as one figure for everything, and whether deleted data survives in backups for a further period. A policy that admits the backup lag is being more careful, not less, even though it means the true number is larger than the stated one.

Legal basis, then deletion and the rights section

If legal basis returns a hit, the policy is built on the GDPR frame and deserves two of your five minutes. European law requires a lawful ground for each purpose, and the grounds are not equally interesting. Consent, contract and legal obligation are narrow. Legitimate interests is the flexible one.

It lets an organisation process data where it has a real interest not overridden by your rights, and it performs that balancing itself. Used properly it covers necessities like fraud prevention and network security. It is also where the surprising processing sits, because it is the ground that does not require asking you. So read the purposes attached to it. If they include profiling, enrichment from outside sources, or anything described as improving relevance, you have found the part that would have needed a consent box under a stricter reading.

Then search delete and read the rights section with one question in mind: what does exercising a right require of me? A policy can grant an unimpeachable set of rights and route every one through a portal that requires an account. Being asked to register in order to be forgotten is a real cost, and a design choice rather than a legal necessity. Look for a named role and a response deadline too: a form with no named recipient and no timeline is a queue, not a right.

In India the shape is different. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is built on notice and consent rather than a menu of lawful grounds, and it requires a grievance officer who answers complaints by name. I have written separately about what consent actually means under DPDP; the implementation detail has been arriving in stages and I am not a lawyer, so treat that as orientation rather than advice.

The two clauses nobody reads

The change clause

Search change. You want two facts. Will they tell you when the policy changes, and how — email, a notice in the product, or nothing but a new date at the top? And does continued use count as acceptance?

“Your continued use constitutes acceptance” is very common, and it quietly moves the work of monitoring onto you. In practice the policy you agreed to is not the policy you are under. The better version exists: notice by email a set number of days before material changes take effect, and a version history so you can see what moved. A changelog on a privacy policy is rare enough that I read it as a signal about the whole organisation, though I have not measured whether that instinct is right.

The acquisition clause

Search transfer or merger. Nearly every policy says that in a merger, acquisition, reorganisation or sale of assets, your personal data may pass to the acquiring entity. It is there because it has to be: personal data is an asset, it is valued in the deal, and in an insolvency it is one of the assets that can be sold.

You cannot negotiate this clause. What it tells you is that every promise in the document has a lifespan bounded by the company’s ownership: a careful policy attached to a company that might be bought next year is a careful policy for now.

Compare the policy against the page

A policy is a claim. The page is the evidence. The last step, and the only one that can catch a policy that is simply wrong, is to check one against the other.

Start with the cookie banner, and open the vendor list rather than the summary. Count the entries, and see how many are on before you touch anything. Banners built on the advertising industry’s consent framework routinely pre-enable vendors under legitimate interest. If the banner names sixty vendors and the policy says “selected partners”, the banner is the more accurate document.

Then open your browser’s developer tools, go to the network tab and reload. You are looking for hostnames that are not the site you are on, and for requests that fire before you have consented to anything. It takes about thirty seconds and is the closest thing to an audit available from outside. It cannot see what happens on their servers — nothing available to you can — but it can show that the policy’s list of third parties is incomplete, which is a specific and checkable failure.

The browser leak test shows the same thing from the other side — what a page can work out about you without asking: your address, your fonts, your canvas, your hardware. It contacts a STUN server as soon as it loads, because that is the only way to show you your own network address, and it says so before it does.

Tells of a policy nobody wrote, and one somebody meant

Tells that nobody wrote it

  • Another company’s name in the text. Search for the company’s own name. If a different one appears anywhere, the document came from a template and was edited by find-and-replace. It is the fastest disqualifier there is.
  • No effective date, or a stale one. A policy with no date cannot be compared to anything, including its previous self. A date from years ago on a product that has obviously changed says the document is not maintained alongside the system.
  • No route to a human. No address, no named role, no email, just a form. Rights exercisable only through an unattributed form are rights on paper.
  • Laws that do not apply. A long section on a foreign statute, and nothing about the law that governs where the service actually operates.
  • A jurisdiction clause that suits only them. Worth noticing, but not damning alone: every organisation writes its dispute clause in its own favour, including ones that behave well.

Tells that somebody meant it

  • Named sub-processors, kept current. A list of actual vendors, with a way to be told when it changes, is the most expensive thing to maintain in a privacy policy, and usually the most informative.
  • Statements of what they do not do, specifically. “We do not use third-party advertising cookies” is checkable. “We respect your privacy” is not.
  • The awkward paragraph. The one admitting something inconvenient: an advertising dependency, what happens on a government request, a limitation they could have left out. A policy that never says anything against its own interest has not been written honestly; every real system has something awkward in it.

Run the method on this site

The method is worth more applied than described, and the fairest target is my own. This site’s privacy policy is a practice run, and I cannot complain about the review.

Search third part and you get names rather than categories: Google Analytics, WhatsApp, Google Calendar and Vercel, plus a second list of services contacted only when you run one of the network labs. Search share and you find the awkward paragraph: the site carries no advertising and sells nothing, and Google Analytics is nevertheless part of Google’s advertising infrastructure, sets an advertising cookie called _gcl_au, and contacts Google’s conversion endpoint. I would rather write that down than have somebody find it in a network log.

Now search retain, and the method turns on me. Correspondence is kept for as long as needed to work together and to meet legal obligations; analytics data for a limited period under Google’s standard retention settings. Both are the soft answer: there is no number, and the number was the thing I called the good sign. The contact and internship forms have no backend at all — pressing send opens WhatsApp with the message pre-filled — so that correspondence sits in Meta’s hands as much as mine. That does not excuse the absence of a number.

The change clause is the weaker form too: a new version, an updated date, no email notice and no changelog. The rights section fares better — a named grievance officer, acknowledgement within twenty-four hours, resolution within fifteen days, and the DPDP nominee provision spelled out.

Five minutes buys a risk read: who receives this, for how long, and how do I get out. It does not buy a legal opinion, and it cannot tell you what runs on somebody else’s servers. A policy can be exemplary and the organisation still lose the data — a different document, covered in how to read a data breach notification. What would change my mind is a regime of standard headings that made policies genuinely comparable: if the sharing section were always in the same place and the same form, reading would beat searching. Until then, searching wins, because the document was not organised for you.
