How to stand out in an internship interview

Between 2021 and 2023, while working at MountBlue and consulting for US firms, I took more than 5,000 interviews. That much time on the other side of the table teaches you what actually separates candidates — and it's rarely what they spent their time preparing. Here's what actually moves the needle, from someone who has done the reviewing at scale.

Published 6 August 2026 · 6 min read · by Krunalkumar Shah

Understand what an internship interview is really for

A senior-hire interview asks "can you already do this job?" An internship interview asks a very different question: "will you be worth teaching?" Nobody expects an intern to know everything. What they are actually screening for is coachability, curiosity, and whether you finish what you start. Once you understand that shift, half of interview anxiety disappears — you stop trying to fake expertise you aren't expected to have, and start showing the traits that actually get you picked.

Attitude first, aptitude second

If those 5,000-plus interviews taught me one thing, it's this: I screen for attitude before aptitude. It sounds backwards — surely skill is what matters? — but the logic is simple. Aptitude can be added. I can teach a framework, a language, a tool; skills are learnable on a timeline. Attitude comes from within, and no amount of training installs it. Curiosity, honesty, the willingness to keep going when something is hard, the instinct to take ownership instead of making excuses — you either bring those to the table or you don't.

So a candidate who is a little behind on skills but clearly hungry, coachable, and honest will beat a more polished one who is arrogant or passive nearly every time. What this means for you is freeing: you do not have to walk in as the finished article. You have to walk in as someone obviously worth investing in. Show the attitude, and the aptitude gap stops being disqualifying — it becomes the exact thing the internship exists to close.

Do the homework almost nobody does

You would be surprised how many candidates can't answer "so, what do you know about my work?" Turning up informed is rare enough that it instantly separates you. Before any interview:

  • Read what the person or company actually does — their projects, their writing, their focus areas — and be able to name one specific thing that interested you and why.
  • Re-read the exact posting and map your own experience to what it asks for, so you can speak to their needs, not just your résumé.
  • Prepare two or three questions that could only come from someone who did their research (more on that below).

This is the cheapest edge available, and most people skip it. Being the candidate who clearly cared enough to prepare is a signal all on its own.

Talk about what you've built, not just what you've learned

"I completed a course in X" tells me you can follow instructions. "I built this small thing, it broke in this way, and here's how I fixed it" tells me you can actually operate. Projects — however small, however unfinished — are the single most convincing thing you can bring to an internship interview, because they are evidence rather than claims.

A candidate who says "I have no idea how this works, but here's how I'd figure it out" beats one who bluffs, every single time. Honesty about a gap plus a plan to close it is exactly the trait I'm hiring for.

When you describe a project, don't just narrate the happy path. The interesting part — the part that proves you can think — is the moment something went wrong and what you did about it.

The mistakes that sink candidates

Most rejections aren't about a lack of skill. They're about avoidable self-inflicted wounds:

  • Memorized answers. Reciting a script makes you sound like everyone else and falls apart the moment a follow-up question goes off-script. Understand your own stories well enough to talk about them naturally.
  • Pretending to know. Bluffing is transparent and it's the fastest way to lose trust. "I don't know that yet" is a complete, respectable answer.
  • No questions at the end. "Nope, I'm good" reads as indifference. Always have questions ready.
  • All ambition, no specifics. "I want to learn everything about cybersecurity" is noise. "I want to get hands-on with how real systems are attacked and defended" is a person with direction.

Ask questions that reveal you've been thinking

The questions you ask are part of your answer. Good ones show curiosity and a bias toward doing real work. A few that consistently land well with me:

  • "What does a great intern in this cohort actually do differently from an average one?"
  • "What's a real problem I could start contributing to early on?"
  • "When you look back at people you've mentored, what separated the ones who grew fastest?"

Notice these all aim at the same thing — you're signalling that you're here to contribute and grow, not just to collect a line on your CV.

The mindset that ties it together

If you remember one thing, make it this: an internship interview is not a test you pass by performing confidence. It's a conversation where the other person is trying to picture working with you. Be prepared, be specific, be honest about what you don't know, and show that you finish what you start. Do that and you won't need tricks — you'll simply be the candidate who is obviously ready to be taught, which is the whole thing they're looking for.
