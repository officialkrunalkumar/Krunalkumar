India's IT law, explained: from the IT Act 2000 to the DPDP era

India's digital economy runs on a legal stack most practitioners have never actually read — an e-commerce law from 2000, criminal teeth bolted on in 2008, a thickening rules layer, and now a full data-protection regime. Here is the whole structure in one pass: what each layer says, the folklore worth unlearning, and a reference index you can bookmark.

The law stack nobody reads

If you build or run anything digital in India, you are already inside this stack: every UPI payment, SaaS security clause, and police complaint quoting Section 66C traces back to a handful of statutes and their rules. Yet most of us meet the law only in fragments, and fragments breed folklore — misquoted sections, invented penalties, a provision struck down a decade ago still cited in complaints.

I am not a lawyer — I am a security practitioner who kept meeting these laws in audits, vendor contracts, and incident calls until I read the primary texts. This is the orientation I wish someone had handed me — every claim checked against its primary source and date-stamped where the ground is moving.

Not legal advice. This is practitioner orientation — a map of the terrain, not counsel about your situation. For a notice, a summons, an investigation, or a compliance decision, consult a lawyer qualified in Indian law. Verified against primary sources in August 2026; where the law is in flux, I say so.

The IT Act 2000: why it exists

In the late 1990s India had a practical problem: contract and evidence law assumed paper. A "document" was something you could hold; a "signature" meant ink. So when the United Nations adopted the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce (1996) and recommended that member states legislate along its lines, India did. The Information Technology Act, 2000 came into force on 17 October 2000 — the country's first comprehensive law for electronic commerce and computer crime.

Its founding achievements sound mundane precisely because they worked: electronic records and digital signatures gained legal validity, so a contract no longer fails merely for being electronic; the Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA) was created to license the authorities issuing digital-signature certificates; and adjudicating officers under Section 46 got power to decide compensation claims up to ₹5 crore, with appeals originally to the Cyber Appellate Tribunal (merged into TDSAT in 2017).

Just as important is what the original Act was not: a commerce statute with a thin chapter of offences and barely a word about privacy. Almost everything practitioners now call "the IT Act" arrived eight years later.

The sections you will actually meet

Here is the practitioner core of the Act, one concrete example each. Penalty figures are from the current statute text.

Section 43 — unauthorised access and damage (civil). Unauthorised access, data downloads, malware, or disruption create liability to compensate the person affected. Civil, not criminal: the departing contractor who deletes a client's database answers here.

Section 43A — negligent handling of sensitive personal data. A company negligent in implementing "reasonable security practices" for sensitive personal data, causing wrongful loss or gain, must pay compensation — with no statutory cap. Note its trajectory: Section 44(2) of the DPDP Act omits it with effect from May 2027; until then, 43A is still the law.

Section 65 — tampering with source documents. Knowingly concealing, destroying, or altering computer source code the law requires to be kept — up to three years and/or ₹2 lakh. Think doctoring billing-software source before an audit.

Section 66 — computer-related offences. The bridge from civil to criminal: any Section 43 act done dishonestly or fraudulently, punishable with up to three years and/or ₹5 lakh. The ex-employee who logs in with never-revoked credentials and copies the customer database faces both a 43 claim and a 66 prosecution.

The 66-family, added in 2008, covers the crimes you read about weekly:

  • 66B — dishonestly receiving a stolen computer resource or communication device (up to three years or ₹1 lakh or both) — buying a phone you know is stolen.
  • 66C — identity theft: fraudulent use of another's password, electronic signature, or unique ID (up to three years plus fine up to ₹1 lakh). Most phishing prosecutions live here.
  • 66D — cheating by personation using a computer resource (same penalties) — the workhorse of India's online-fraud FIRs: fake bank calls, OTP scams, fraudulent "support" numbers.
  • 66E — privacy violation: capturing or publishing images of a person's private areas without consent (up to three years or ₹2 lakh or both).
  • 66F — cyberterrorism: attacks threatening India's sovereignty, integrity, or security, or unauthorised access likely to cause death, injury, or critical-infrastructure damage — up to life imprisonment.

Sections 67, 67A, 67B — content offences. Obscene material (67, up to three years and ₹5 lakh on first conviction), sexually explicit material (67A, up to five years first time), and child sexual abuse material (67B, the strictest — reaching creation, collection, and browsing). The quieter 67C obliges intermediaries to preserve specified records — one hook behind log-retention duties.

Sections 69, 69A, 69B — the state's powers. Interception, monitoring, and decryption (69); blocking public access (69A — the section behind the June 2020 TikTok ban and most content-blocking orders); traffic-data monitoring for cybersecurity (69B). Refusing to assist under 69, or defying a 69A order, carries up to seven years. Who may order what, and with what safeguards, gets its own section below.

Section 70 — protected systems. Systems affecting Critical Information Infrastructure can be declared "protected"; the NCIIPC (s. 70A, created 2014) guards them, and unauthorised access carries up to ten years. Section 70B is CERT-In's statutory basis.

Sections 72 and 72A — confidentiality. Section 72 punishes officials who disclose records they accessed under the Act (up to two years). Section 72A reaches anyone — including an intermediary's staff — who discloses personal information obtained under a services contract, without consent, knowing it will cause wrongful loss or gain (up to three years and/or ₹5 lakh): the section for the support engineer who sells customer data.

Section 66A: the section that won't stay dead

One provision deserves its own chapter, because getting it wrong is the most common error in Indian cyber-law writing. Section 66A, inserted in 2009, criminalised sending "grossly offensive" or "menacing" information — or false information meant to cause "annoyance" or "inconvenience" — through a computer or communication device. Words that elastic criminalise almost anything, and they did: most notoriously, the November 2012 arrest of two young women over a Facebook post questioning Mumbai's shutdown after Bal Thackeray's death — one of them arrested merely for liking it.

A law student, Shreya Singhal, took it to the Supreme Court. In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, decided on 24 March 2015 in a judgment authored by Justice Rohinton Nariman, the Court struck down Section 66A in its entirety: vague, overbroad, chilling to speech, violative of Article 19(1)(a), and not saved by Article 19(2)'s reasonable restrictions. The same judgment upheld Section 69A's blocking power with its safeguards, and read down Section 79 so that an intermediary's takedown duty arises only from a court order or government notification — not from anyone's complaint.

Yet 66A refused to die. FIRs kept citing it for years, and as late as 2022 the Supreme Court was still directing states to stop invoking it and to drop it from pending cases. Treat anything citing 66A as live law the way you would treat code calling a deleted API — a reliable sign the author never checked the source.

The 2008 amendment: the Act grows teeth

The IT (Amendment) Act, 2008 was passed in December 2008 — weeks after the 26/11 Mumbai attacks, with strikingly little debate — and came into force on 27 October 2009. In one bill it added Section 43A and the entire 66- and 67-families; expanded Section 69 and created 69A and 69B; gave CERT-In its statutory role as the national incident-response agency under Section 70B; made the law technology-neutral by recognising "electronic signatures" generally; and rebuilt Section 79's safe harbour into its modern conditional form — intermediaries are shielded from liability for user content only if they observe prescribed due diligence. That conditionality is the hook on which the 2011 and 2021 intermediary rules hang.

In short: the 2000 Act built the marketplace; the 2008 amendment built the police station next door.

The rules layer: where compliance actually lives

Statutes delegate; rules operationalise. Three sets of subordinate legislation carry most day-to-day compliance.

SPDI Rules, 2011. Issued under Section 43A, they defined "sensitive personal data" (passwords, financial, health, and biometric data) and required privacy policies, consent, and "reasonable security practices", with ISO/IEC 27001 named as one acceptable standard. India's old data-protection regime — in force until the DPDP Act repeals it along with 43A in May 2027.

IT Rules, 2021. The Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code Rules, notified in February 2021 under Section 79's due-diligence hook. All intermediaries must publish terms, take down specified content on legal order, and run a grievance mechanism — acknowledge within 24 hours, dispose within 15 days. Significant social media intermediaries (5 million or more registered Indian users) additionally need a Chief Compliance Officer, a nodal contact, a resident grievance officer in India, and monthly compliance reports. The most contested piece is Rule 4(2) traceability: messaging platforms must be able to identify a message's "first originator" on a judicial or competent-authority order. WhatsApp challenged it in the Delhi High Court in 2021, arguing it cannot comply without breaking end-to-end encryption for everyone; as of August 2026 the case remains undecided, with other parts of the Rules under challenge in various High Courts as well. Traceability is not settled law — in either direction.

CERT-In Directions, April 2022. Issued on 28 April 2022 under Section 70B(6), binding service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporates, and government organisations: report specified cyber incidents to CERT-In within six hours of noticing them; keep logs of all ICT systems for 180 days, stored within India; and synchronise clocks to official NTP sources. Data centres and VPS, cloud, and VPN providers must also keep validated customer records — names, contact details, IP addresses — for five years after a subscriber leaves. That requirement is why ExpressVPN, NordVPN, and Surfshark pulled their Indian servers in 2022 rather than log their users.

The DPDP Act 2023: a new data-protection era

The road to a real data-protection law began in the courtroom. In Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (24 August 2017), a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court unanimously held that privacy is a fundamental right under Article 21. Draft bills followed and fell; in August 2023 Parliament passed the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023.

The design is deliberately simpler than Europe's GDPR. It covers digital personal data — any data about an identifiable individual, no separate "sensitive" tier. Data Fiduciaries (who decide why and how data is processed) owe duties to Data Principals (the individuals). Processing needs consent that is free, specific, informed, unambiguous, and as easy to withdraw as to give — or must fit narrow "legitimate uses". Children under 18 need verifiable parental consent, with tracking and behavioural advertising aimed at children prohibited. A Data Protection Board of India adjudicates breaches. The penalty schedule is the headline: up to ₹250 crore per instance for failing to take reasonable security safeguards, up to ₹200 crore for breach-notification or children's-data failures, up to ₹50 crore for most else — per instance, so one incident can stack.

Now the part that changes fastest, so date-stamp it: the Act sat largely un-operational until the DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025, with a phased rollout — the Board first, consent-manager registration around November 2026, and the full substantive obligations (notice, consent artifacts, data-principal rights, breach notification, Significant Data Fiduciary duties) binding from May 2027, when Section 43A and the SPDI Rules also stand repealed. As of mid-2026, India is in the transition window: DPDP deadlines are on the calendar, but SPDI is still the regime you are technically judged under. Plan for both; build for DPDP.

The wider digital-law stack

The IT Act is the spine of India's digital law, but not the whole skeleton. Here is the rest of the stack: what each statute governs, and when you actually meet it.

  • Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (the IPC's successor, in force since 1 July 2024). IT Act charges rarely travel alone: fraud FIRs pair 66C/66D with BNS cheating (s. 318) and personation (s. 319); sextortion adds extortion (s. 308); stalking (s. 78) covers cyberstalking; defamation is s. 356; and BNS forgery expressly reaches electronic records (s. 336).
  • Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 — the procedure side of the same reform; its Section 94 production summons, covered below, is how digital evidence is demanded from companies.
  • Telecommunications Act, 2023 — the Telegraph Act's replacement, in force in phases since June 2024: telecom networks, spectrum, and services, with its own interception power (s. 20) and 2024 rules replacing the colonial-era regime. Carriers answer here; online platforms to the IT Act.
  • DPDP Act, 2023 — covered in depth above; the data-protection pillar of the stack.
  • Consumer Protection Act, 2019 with the E-Commerce Rules, 2020 and the Central Consumer Protection Authority's (CCPA) Dark Patterns Guidelines (30 November 2023). If you sell online: marketplace disclosure and grievance duties, plus a ban on 13 listed dark patterns — false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, subscription traps.
  • Copyright Act, 1957 (as amended in 2012). Software is a literary work, so piracy is infringement; ss. 65A–65B penalise circumventing technological protection measures and tampering with rights-management information.
  • RBI's payments and lending rules. The April 2018 "Storage of Payment System Data" directive keeps end-to-end payment data only in India — every payments fintech lives with this daily — and the digital lending guidelines constrain what lending apps may collect.
  • Aadhaar Act, 2016. Strict limits on Aadhaar identity data, sharply narrowed for private-sector use by the Supreme Court's 2018 Aadhaar judgment.
  • POCSO Act, 2012. Overlaps IT Act s. 67B on child sexual abuse material — prosecutions routinely charge both.
  • The Digital India Act — the announced IT Act successor, chronically over-announced: consultations began in 2023, but as of August 2026 no bill has been enacted; MeitY keeps amending rules under the existing Act instead. Until it passes, the 2000 Act remains the spine.

When law enforcement comes asking

Sooner or later, a company holding user data receives a legal request. These arrive through four distinct doors, and the first skill — for the officer issuing and the company receiving alike — is knowing which door is which:

  • BNSS Section 94 (in force since 1 July 2024, replacing CrPC Section 91): a court, or the officer in charge of a police station, issues a summons or written order — physical or electronic — for production of documents, electronic communication, or devices likely to contain digital evidence. The routine instrument behind most log and record requests; you can comply by producing the material without appearing in person. If you want to see how these production orders play out in a real investigation, that walk-through follows the evidence from first alert to the courtroom.
  • IT Act Section 69: interception, monitoring, or decryption — only on the order of the competent authority under the 2009 Interception Rules: the Union Home Secretary, or a State's Home Secretary, with a review committee examining orders.
  • IT Act Section 69A: blocking of public content, routed through MeitY's Designated Officer (Joint Secretary rank or above) and an inter-ministerial committee under the 2009 Blocking Rules, with an emergency path. Orders are largely kept confidential — much criticised, but the law as it stands.
  • IT Act Sections 69B and 70B(6): traffic-data monitoring for cybersecurity (CERT-In is the notified agency) and CERT-In's power to demand incident information and logs under its 2022 Directions.

If your company receives one of these, the handling pattern is consistent: verify that the issuing authority actually holds the power cited (a police station cannot issue a Section 69 interception order); check the scope — specific accounts, identifiers, and time ranges, not a fishing expedition; respond through counsel, especially where content or encryption is involved; preserve the requested material immediately; and document everything disclosed — what, when, to whom, under which provision. Disclose what the order covers, and only that. None of this is obstruction; it is the diligence a well-run investigation expects from the other side of the table.

If you build software

Three obligations should shape your architecture today. Logging: CERT-In means 180 days of ICT logs retained in India — centralise logs, set retention explicitly, pin the storage region. Clocks: a six-hour reporting window is a detection-and-escalation problem; alerting and an on-call path are compliance controls, not luxuries. Consent UX: DPDP-grade consent is specific, itemised per purpose, unbundled, and as easy to withdraw as to give — if your signup buries consent in the terms checkbox, you are building technical debt with a ₹250 crore interest rate. And add real deletion paths now: erasure rights arrive with the 2027 phase, and retrofitting deletion into a system that never planned for it is miserable work.

If you run a business

If you decide why and how personal data is processed, you are almost certainly a Data Fiduciary. Start with a data map: what you collect, why, where it lives, which vendors touch it. Push obligations into vendor contracts — processors act for you, and liability does not outsource. Remember the overlap years: until May 2027 you are also judged under 43A and SPDI, so an ISO 27001-aligned security program pays twice. Stand up a grievance channel, and if you are notified as a Significant Data Fiduciary, budget for an India-based Data Protection Officer and independent audits. Above all, rehearse the incident play: one breach can simultaneously start CERT-In's six-hour clock, a Board notification, and contractual duties.

If you're an individual

The DPDP era gives you enforceable rights — to know what a fiduciary holds about you, to correction and erasure, to grievance redressal — fully binding from May 2027. Today, your paths are the national cybercrime portal (cybercrime.gov.in) and the 1930 financial-fraud helpline; the platform's grievance officer, obliged to acknowledge you within 24 hours; and compensation claims under Sections 43/43A before an adjudicating officer. One piece of folklore-resistance: if anyone threatens you with Section 66A, it has been void since 2015.

The deeper habit this article advocates: read primary sources. The IT Act and its rules are on India Code and MeitY's site; the DPDP Act is short; Shreya Singhal and Puttaswamy are genuinely readable. India's IT law has a folklore problem because so few practitioners go to the text. Be the person in the room who has.
