Career story

From a classroom to research and AI pipelines

Career pages show the highlights. This is the fuller version — where I started, the parts that didn't go to plan, and how a student in a Gujarat engineering college ended up publishing security research, mentoring people across three continents, and building AI systems for companies. A single $6 session started more than I realized at the time.

The classroom that didn't feel like enough

I earned my bachelor's in engineering from a private state college, then a master's focused on information security. On paper that is a clean line. In reality, the classroom always felt like it was describing the world rather than letting me touch it. Lectures could tell me how a system was supposed to behave; they couldn't show me how it broke when someone poked it the wrong way. That gap — between knowing about something and having actually done it — is the thing that has shaped every decision since.

So I started building and breaking things on my own machine. Most of it went nowhere. But one of those late-night rabbit holes turned into the first real milestone of my career.

The paper I almost didn't write

I got curious about a deceptively simple attack: the fork bomb. A tiny snippet that tells a system to endlessly copy itself until it grinds to a halt. What fascinated me was that even a properly hardened Linux machine could be brought down by it, because creating a process is a completely ordinary thing that needs no special permission. The existing defenses all had a catch — they either blocked legitimate work or punished innocent programs forever.

I thought I had an idea for something better, and I nearly talked myself out of writing it up. Who was I to publish? A student. But I did the work, ran the experiments, and it became a peer-reviewed paper — Security Against Fork Bomb Attack in Linux Based Systems, published in IJRAT. It earned a silver medal from Shantilal Shah Government Engineering College and IPCL, and was nominated for a best-paper award by the International Association of Engineers (IAENG). The lesson wasn't "I'm brilliant." It was that the gap between an idea and a published result is mostly just the willingness to actually finish.

The $6 session that started everything

My first paid mentoring session earned me six dollars an hour. It would be easy to dismiss that as nothing. I saw it as an important milestone — proof that something I knew was worth paying for, however modestly. That single session set the direction for a lot of what came next, because it turned out I loved the work far more than the rate suggested.

What I didn't expect was where mentoring would take me — not up a ladder at one company, but outward, across borders.

Mentoring across three continents

With a US-based organization, Chegg, I guided students in programming, networking, and information security — and while I was teaching, I was also studying, working through Coursera certifications and networking with founders and stakeholders. Teaching and learning were never separate activities for me; they were the same loop.

One of those stakeholders, impressed by the work, offered me a role as a developer and cybersecurity expert with a company based in Guadeloupe. Around the same period I was mentoring people in Taiwan in Python, Linux, cybersecurity, and scripting. A kid from a private college in India, teaching and building for people in the Caribbean and East Asia — I still find that remarkable. Today I work as an independent consultant with a range of tech and EdTech companies, mostly around Python full-stack development and cybersecurity.

A lot of that teaching happened on tutoring platforms, and the numbers there kept me honest about whether any of it was actually landing. On Codementor I worked as a software engineering mentor and developer & cybersecurity expert across roughly 204 sessions, ending with a 5.0 rating from 163 reviews — hundreds of real people, mostly stuck on C, C++, Python, Java, Linux, or shell scripting at some difficult hour, who left feeling unstuck. On Spires I tutored computer science online at university level, as an IBM-certified Cybersecurity Analyst and Data Analyst. Those platforms taught me something the classroom never could: teaching only counts if the other person actually gets it, and the feedback is immediate and unforgiving.

Learning to speak human, not just machine

The hardest skill in all of this wasn't technical. It was talking to people who don't speak your technical language. Early on I would explain things the way I understood them — precisely, and uselessly, for the person in front of me. I learned to lead with the KISS principle: Keep It Simple and Short. Plain language, relatable examples, no jargon for its own sake. A concept a stakeholder actually grasps beats a technically perfect explanation they tune out. That one shift changed how well my work landed more than any framework ever did.

The years I interviewed thousands of people

From 2021 to 2023, working at MountBlue and consulting for US firms, I did something that quietly reshaped how I think about talent: I took more than 5,000 interviews. Sit across from that many people and patterns emerge that no single conversation could ever show you. The biggest one changed my whole approach — I learned to check attitude first, and aptitude second.

It sounds backwards until you've watched it play out thousands of times. Aptitude can be added — I can teach a skill, a tool, a framework, on a timeline. Attitude comes from within, and nothing I do installs it. The people who succeeded were rarely the most polished in the room; they were the curious, honest, take-ownership ones who were obviously worth investing in. That single lens — hire and teach for attitude, because the aptitude will follow — is now the backbone of how I mentor.

The pivot into automation and AI

Security taught me to think about systems as things that can fail in surprising ways. Automation was a natural next step: if you understand how a process behaves, you can also make it run itself. I started small — scripts that turned a spreadsheet update into a task, workflows that replied to routine emails so a human never had to. Unglamorous plumbing, but it saved people real hours, and that feedback was addictive.

That work grew into building genuine AI pipelines in a company setting — systems that read messy company records, meeting notes, and support tickets, then produced something useful on the other side, like a grounded assessment of why a customer might be about to leave. Moving from a personal script to a multi-stage pipeline a business depends on is a big jump, and I learned most of it by shipping something imperfect and then fixing what broke.

What I actually believe about learning

After all of it, my philosophy is simple, and I hold to three Fs: learning should be a combination of fun, freedom, and flexibility. Take those away and even a motivated person burns out. Two more things I tell every mentee. First, shrink the goal — turn a daunting long-term ambition into manageable short-term steps, because most people don't lack ability, they drown in the size of the plan. Second, understand rather than memorize. Tools go out of date; the understanding beneath them compounds. Memorize a framework and you own it until the next version ships. Understand the idea underneath and you can pick up whatever comes next.

The setbacks I don't usually mention

For the honesty this post promised: plenty didn't work. Projects I was proud of got no users. I applied for things I didn't get. I spent weeks on approaches that turned out to be dead ends, and there were long stretches where it felt like everyone else had a clearer path than I did. None of that shows up on a portfolio, but all of it is where the actual learning happened. The dead ends taught me to scope smaller and validate sooner — lessons no success would have taught me as sharply.

Why I mentor now

Mentoring gives back as much as it takes. One moment I come back to often: a mentee in California, working on a master's in economics, came to me for help shaping a research hypothesis — a field I have no formal background in. We worked through it together anyway, and she scored a perfect 300 out of 300 on her presentation, with her professors impressed by the hypothesis. I didn't know economics. What I could do was help her think clearly and simplify the complex — and that turned out to be enough.

That is why I keep doing this. Engaging with different people every day, wrestling with questions that force me to go research something new, keeps me sharper than working alone ever could. If you truly enjoy something, you find a way to make time for it — and I genuinely enjoy watching other people grow.

If there is one thing to take from my story, it is this: you do not need permission to start, and the non-linear route is not a failure of the plan — it is the plan, for almost everyone worth learning from.
