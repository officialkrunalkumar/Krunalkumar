Passwords, passkeys, and what actually protects you

Almost nobody loses an account because somebody guessed their password. They lose it because the same password was used somewhere that got breached, or because they typed it into a page that looked right and was not. Understanding which threat is which is the difference between security advice that helps and security advice that just makes your life harder.

How accounts are actually lost

There are four common routes, and they are not equally likely. Ranked roughly by how often they land:

Reuse after somebody else's breach. A site you signed up to years ago is compromised, and the email-and-password pair from it gets replayed against your bank, your mail, your everything. This is credential stuffing, and it requires no cracking at all — the password is already correct. You can check whether a password of yours appears in known breach data using the breach checker, which does it by k-anonymity so the password never leaves your browser.

Phishing. You are sent somewhere convincing and you type the password in yourself. No technical weakness in your password is involved. A long random one is phished exactly as easily as a short one. Pull a suspicious link apart before you visit it and you will see how much a URL can hide.

Offline cracking after a breach. If the breached site stored passwords badly, attackers grind through them. How long that takes depends almost entirely on the site's choices, not yours — billions of guesses per second against MD5, a few thousand against bcrypt. You can watch that difference happen in the hash cracker.

Actual online guessing. Rare, because every serious service rate-limits it. This is the threat most password advice is written for, and it is the one that matters least.

Three of those four are unaffected by how clever your password is. That is the whole reason the industry is moving on.

What still matters about passwords

Two things, and only two.

Length, not decoration. Substituting 3 for e adds essentially nothing — cracking tools have known that trick for decades. Adding words adds real entropy. Four unrelated words beat P@ssw0rd! by an enormous margin and are far easier to type. Measure it rather than guess: the difference is not subtle.

Never reusing them. This is the one that actually stops the most common attack. And it is only achievable with a password manager, because nobody remembers ninety unique passwords. Any reputable one is fine. The one you will actually use is the correct choice.

Everything else — forced ninety-day rotation, mandatory symbols, security questions about your first pet — ranges from useless to actively harmful. Rotation in particular pushes people toward Summer2026! then Autumn2026!, which is worse than leaving a strong password alone.

What a passkey actually is

A passkey is not a password stored more safely. It is a different mechanism entirely, and the distinction is the point.

When you create one, your device generates a keypair. The public half goes to the website. The private half is never sent to the site — not during sign-up, not during login, not ever. (Synced passkeys do travel between your own devices through iCloud Keychain or Google Password Manager, end-to-end encrypted; a device-bound passkey on a security key never moves at all.) To sign in, the site sends a challenge and your device returns a signature proving it holds the private key, usually after your fingerprint or face or device PIN unlocks it.

Follow what that changes:

  • There is no shared secret to steal. A breach of the website leaks public keys, which are public. There is nothing in the dump to replay.
  • There is nothing to reuse. Each site gets its own keypair, automatically.
  • Phishing stops working. This is the strongest property and it is worth being precise about: the signature is bound to the site's actual domain by the browser. A convincing replica on a lookalike domain cannot receive a valid signature, because the browser will not produce one for the wrong origin. Your own judgement is no longer the thing standing between you and a homograph domain.

What passkeys do not fix

Being honest about the limits is what makes the rest credible.

  • Account recovery is still the soft underbelly. If a service lets you recover by emailing a link, then your email account is still the master key to your life — and it had better be the best-protected thing you own.
  • A compromised device is a compromised passkey. The private key is protected by your device's security, so device lock, encryption and updates all still matter.
  • Coverage is uneven. Plenty of services still do not support them, and some implementations are noticeably worse than others.
  • Losing every device is a real scenario. Platform syncing (Apple, Google, or your password manager) mostly handles this, but it is worth knowing which of those is holding your keys before you need to know.

And session tokens remain what they always were: if someone steals the token your browser holds after login, they do not need your credentials at all. That is why session hijacking is still worth understanding, and why sharing a HAR file from your browser's network tab is far more dangerous than people realise.

A setup worth one hour

In rough order of how much safety each step buys:

  • Protect the email account first. It is the recovery route for everything else. Strongest password, strongest second factor, passkey if offered.
  • Install a password manager and let it generate everything. Do not migrate all ninety accounts today; change them as you sign in over the next month.
  • Turn on passkeys wherever they are offered. Bank, email, cloud storage, anything holding money or identity.
  • Where passkeys are not available, use an app-based or hardware second factor rather than SMS. SIM swapping is a real and unglamorous attack.
  • Check your main addresses against breach data and change anything that appears.

That is the whole list. It is unexciting, it takes about an hour, and it removes the three attacks that actually take accounts away from people.
