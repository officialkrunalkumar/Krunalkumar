Scope the work before you quote it

Nearly every project I have watched go wrong went wrong in the same place, and it was not the price. It was the sentence that said what the work was — because there wasn't one. Scope is not paperwork you do after winning the job. It is the thing you are actually selling, and writing it down is the cheapest insurance either side will ever buy.

Published 27 August 2026 · 7 min read · by Krunalkumar Shah

Under-priced and under-defined are not the same problem

When a project loses money, the reflex is to conclude you charged too little. Sometimes that is true. Far more often the rate was fine and the boundary was missing — so the work kept growing while the number stayed still. Doubling your rate does not fix that. It just means you lose more slowly on a job that has no edges.

You can tell the two apart with one question: if the work had stopped where you both originally imagined, would the price have been fair? If yes, you did not have a pricing problem. You had a definition problem.

A scope is not a list of tasks. It is a description of the edge — what is inside, what is outside, and what has to be true for the inside to be possible.

The five questions to answer before you send a number

None of these are clever. They are simply the five things that, when left unanswered, later turn into an argument. Answer them in writing, in the client's own words where you can, and most of the argument never happens.

1. What does "done" look like, observably? Not "improve the onboarding flow" — that has no end. "A new user can sign up, verify their email, and reach the dashboard without support contact." That has an end, and both of you can see it from across the room.

2. What is explicitly not included? This is the sentence people skip because it feels negative, and it is the single highest-value line in the document. If you are hardening a web application, say plainly that it does not cover the mail server, the staff laptops, or the third-party plugin they bought last year.

3. What do you need from them, and by when? Access, credentials, a decision-maker, sample data, a staging environment. Most delays that get blamed on the contractor were actually a week spent waiting for a login. Name the dependency and name the date.

4. How many rounds of revision? "Until you're happy" is not a scope, it is an open tab. Two rounds is normal and generous. Say two.

5. What happens when something changes? Answered below — but it must be answered before it happens, not during.

Write the boundary down, even for small work

There is a persistent belief that a written scope is for big contracts and that small jobs run on trust. It is backwards. A large engagement has enough margin and enough meetings to absorb a misunderstanding. A two-week job does not — one week of unbudgeted work has already halved it.

The document does not need to be a legal instrument. A page is plenty: what we are doing, what done looks like, what is not included, what I need from you, how long, how much, and what happens if it changes. If a client balks at putting that in writing, you have learned something valuable about the project for the price of an email.

I quote in writing before starting anything, and where the client's work allows it I deliver in the open on GitHub. Both habits come from the same place: ambiguity is expensive, and the cheapest moment to remove it is before anyone has spent money.

Changing scope without either side feeling cheated

Scope will change. It is not a failure — new information appears, priorities move, and a client who learns something halfway through should be able to act on it. Scope creep is not change; it is change that arrives without the timeline or the price moving with it.

The mechanism is boring and it works:

  • Name it the moment you see it. Not at the end. "That's a good idea and it's outside what we agreed — want me to price it?" is a friendly sentence when said in week one and an accusation when said in week six.
  • Price it separately. A small addendum, not a renegotiation of the whole job.
  • Let them say no. The client keeps control of their budget, which is precisely why this does not feel like a squeeze.

Done this way, a change request is a normal business conversation. Done at the end, it is a fight about who remembers what — and you will lose it even when you are right, because you are arguing against something that was never written down.

Learning to say no early

The hardest skill is not writing the scope, it is declining work that cannot have one. Some requests genuinely cannot be bounded yet — the client does not know what they want, or the problem needs investigating before anyone can honestly quote it.

That is not a reason to guess a number. It is a reason to sell a smaller thing first: a short paid discovery that ends in a written recommendation and a real quote. The client gets clarity and something they own either way. You get a defined piece of work and a much better view of whether the larger job is one you want.

A quote you had to guess at is a bet you are placing with your own time. Sell the investigation instead, and price the work when you can actually see it.
