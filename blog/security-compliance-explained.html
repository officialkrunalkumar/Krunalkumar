Security compliance, explained: GDPR, HIPAA, PCI DSS, SOC 2, and more

The alphabet soup of compliance hides one clarifying distinction: some of these frameworks are laws, one is a contract you already signed, some are certifiable standards, and one is an auditor's attestation. Sort the seven that way — with the requirements that matter in practice — and it becomes obvious which are obligations, which are choices, and which are actually yours.

Compliance is trust made auditable

Every security consulting conversation I have eventually lands on the same question. Not "how do we stop attackers" — that one comes with the territory — but some version of: "GDPR, HIPAA, SOC 2, ISO-something… which of these actually apply to us?" It comes from founders, engineering leads, and operations people holding a 300-question vendor questionnaire — always in the same slightly exhausted tone.

It's a fair question, because the alphabet soup hides a real distinction: these frameworks are not the same kind of thing. Some are laws, with regulators and fines behind them. One is a contract you accepted the day you started taking card payments. Some are voluntary standards you can certify against, and one is an accountant's opinion about you. Mix those categories up and you get the classic failure mode I keep meeting: a company proudly chasing a certificate it doesn't need while ignoring a law that already applies to it.

Here is the framing that unlocks all of it: compliance is trust made auditable. Every framework below turns "trust us with your data" into something a third party can verify. And every one of them is a floor, not a ceiling — the minimum a regulator, bank, or customer will accept, not a level attackers find inconvenient. The attack families I mapped in the previous post do not check your certification status before firing.

The lay of the land: seven frameworks in one table

Before the deep dives, orientation. The most useful column is the second one — what kind of thing is this? — because it tells you who enforces the framework and what actually happens when you fall short.

The seven frameworks at a glance — what each one is, who it binds, and what it protects.
Framework What it is Who it applies to What it protects
GDPR EU law (reaches worldwide) Anyone offering services to, or monitoring, people in the EU Personal data of people in the EU
CCPA/CPRA California law For-profit businesses over revenue or data-volume thresholds California residents' personal information
HIPAA US federal law Covered entities and their business associates Protected health information (PHI)
PCI DSS Contractual industry standard Anyone storing, processing, or transmitting card data Payment card data
SOX US federal law Companies listed on US stock exchanges Integrity of financial reporting
ISO/IEC 27001 Certifiable international standard Any organization, voluntarily Information assets, via a managed ISMS
SOC 2 Attestation (AICPA) Service organizations holding customer data Customer data, per the Trust Services Criteria

A necessary disclaimer: this article is practitioner orientation, not legal advice. Applicability turns on details — where entities are incorporated, what contracts say, how data actually flows — so before you rely on any of this for real obligations, talk to qualified counsel. I map the terrain; lawyers draw your property lines.

GDPR: the law with global reach

The EU's General Data Protection Regulation, in force since May 2018, is the law every other privacy law gets compared to. The part people consistently underestimate is its reach: GDPR applies to organizations anywhere in the world that offer goods or services to people in the EU or monitor their behavior. A Bangalore SaaS company with EU signups, or an American site profiling EU visitors with tracking pixels, is in scope without ever opening a European office.

In practice, four obligations dominate. You need a lawful basis for every processing purpose — consent is only one of six, and often the worst choice, because it can be withdrawn. You must honor data-subject rights: access, rectification, erasure, portability, and objection, generally within a month. You must keep records of processing and put proper contracts in front of every processor handling data for you. And you must report qualifying breaches within 72 hours of becoming aware — a deadline that assumes you rehearsed before the incident.

The penalty structure has two tiers: up to €10 million or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover for procedural failures, and up to €20 million or 4% for violations of core principles and rights — whichever is higher, and EU courts have confirmed that "turnover" means the whole corporate group's. Individual fines have already crossed the billion-euro mark. Start here: build the record of what personal data you hold, why, and where it flows. Every other GDPR obligation hangs off that map.

CCPA/CPRA: California's privacy law

The California Consumer Privacy Act (2018), expanded by the CPRA ballot measure with most changes effective 2023, is America's closest thing to GDPR — and it created the CPPA, the first dedicated privacy regulator in the US. It applies to for-profit businesses doing business in California that cross any one threshold: annual gross revenue above $25 million (inflation-adjusted — about $26.6 million now), personal information of 100,000 or more California consumers or households, or half of revenue derived from selling or sharing personal information. That middle threshold counts identifiers like cookies and device IDs, so consumer sites cross it faster than founders expect.

The requirements that matter: honor consumer rights (know, delete, correct, opt out of sale or sharing, limit sensitive data use); recognize that "sharing" includes cross-context behavioral advertising, which pulls in the ad pixels most marketing teams run; and provide the "Do Not Sell or Share" mechanism while honoring Global Privacy Control browser signals.

Enforcement comes from the CPPA and the state Attorney General: penalties of $2,500 per violation, $7,500 when intentional or involving minors (inflation-adjusted to $2,663 and $7,988), counted per consumer — and since 2023 there is no automatic cure period. Consumers can also sue directly over breaches of unencrypted data, with statutory damages per person that make class actions attractive. Start here: check the thresholds honestly, then audit your tags and pixels — most companies that say "we don't sell data" are "sharing" it under the statutory definition.

HIPAA: health data in the US

HIPAA is a 1996 US federal law whose privacy teeth live in three rules. It binds covered entities — healthcare providers, health plans, and clearinghouses — and, crucially, their business associates: any vendor that creates, receives, or maintains protected health information on their behalf. If your SaaS product stores patient data for a clinic, HIPAA applies to you directly, business associate agreement signed or not.

The Privacy Rule governs how PHI may be used and disclosed, anchored by the "minimum necessary" principle. The Security Rule requires administrative, physical, and technical safeguards for electronic PHI, all flowing from a risk analysis — it is deliberately technology-neutral, which means it never goes out of date and never tells you exactly what to buy. The Breach Notification Rule requires notifying affected individuals within 60 days; breaches affecting 500 or more people must also go to HHS and, in the affected states, the media, while smaller ones are reported annually.

Civil penalties run on four culpability tiers, inflation-adjusted every year — the top tier now exceeds $70,000 per violation with annual caps above $2 million per provision — and knowing misuse of PHI can be referred for criminal prosecution. Start here: if you touch US health data, determine whether you're a business associate, then do the risk analysis for real — it is both the first legal requirement and the gap most commonly cited in enforcement actions.

PCI DSS: the standard you signed up for

PCI DSS is the odd one out: not a law at all, but a contractual standard from the PCI Security Standards Council, founded by the card brands. It binds you through your merchant agreement — the day you started accepting cards, you agreed to it, whether anyone mentioned it or not. It applies to every organization that stores, processes, or transmits cardholder data, from a global processor to a bakery with a card terminal.

The current version is v4.0.1 — v4.0 (published March 2022) was superseded by a clarifications-only revision in June 2024 and retired at the end of that year, and the final tranche of future-dated v4 requirements became mandatory on 31 March 2025. The standard organizes 12 requirements under six goals: secure networks, protected account data, vulnerability management, access control, monitoring and testing, and security policy. How you prove compliance scales with volume — the largest merchants undergo on-site assessments by a Qualified Security Assessor; most businesses complete a self-assessment questionnaire.

There is no regulator. Consequences arrive commercially: card brands fine your acquiring bank — figures of $5,000 to $100,000 a month are commonly cited — and the bank passes the cost to you, raises your fees, or terminates your ability to take cards, which for most businesses is the real death penalty. Start here: shrink your scope. Use your processor's hosted payment fields or tokenization so raw card data never touches your systems — it collapses most of the standard into the shortest questionnaire and makes the remainder tractable.

SOX: when security meets financial reporting

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 — the post-Enron law — is not a privacy framework at all: it protects investors by holding US-listed companies accountable for the integrity of their financial reporting. Security people meet it anyway.

Section 302 makes the CEO and CFO personally certify every quarterly and annual report. Section 404 requires management to assess internal control over financial reporting each year (404(a)), with an independent auditor attesting to that assessment for larger filers (404(b) — smaller and newer public companies are exempt from the attestation, not the assessment). Signing a certification you know to be false carries personal criminal exposure — which is precisely why executives suddenly care about controls.

The security angle is IT general controls: access management, change management, and operations controls over every system that feeds the financial statements — the ERP, billing, revenue pipelines, and the integrations between them. The auditor's questions are effectively "who could change a number?" and "would you know?" — which are access control and logging questions wearing a suit. A serious control failure becomes a disclosed material weakness — and markets punish those. Start here: if an IPO is anywhere on your horizon, inventory every system that touches revenue and lock down access and change management a year before you think you need to — retrofitting ITGCs under audit pressure is miserable.

ISO/IEC 27001: the certifiable standard

ISO/IEC 27001 is the international standard you can actually be certified against. What it certifies is an information security management system — not a checklist of tools but a management loop: assess your risks, choose controls proportionate to them, document why in a Statement of Applicability, and keep the loop turning. Two organizations with the same certificate can run very different controls, legitimately, because their risks differ.

The current edition is the 2022 revision, which restructured Annex A from 114 controls in 14 domains down to 93 controls in four themes — organizational, people, physical, technological — and added explicitly modern ones like threat intelligence, cloud security, and data leakage prevention. The transition window from the 2013 edition closed in October 2025, so a certificate referencing the old version is now expired paper. Certification itself comes from an accredited body on a three-year cycle with annual surveillance audits — it is a relationship, not an event.

Who needs it: anyone selling B2B where customers demand evidence, especially outside the US — globally it plays the role SOC 2 plays in America, and enterprise deals in Europe and Asia often simply require it. Start here: define the scope narrowly and honestly, and do the risk assessment yourselves before buying a 200-document template pack. Auditors test whether the system reflects how you actually work; borrowed paperwork fails that test in the first interview.

SOC 2: the report your customers ask for

SOC 2 is not a certification, despite every marketing page saying otherwise — it is an attestation: a licensed CPA firm examines your controls against the AICPA's Trust Services Criteria and issues a formal opinion. Security (the common criteria) is mandatory in every report; Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, and Privacy are added based on what you promise customers. The criteria are the 2017 set, refreshed with revised points of focus in 2022.

The distinction that matters commercially is Type I versus Type II. A Type I says your controls were suitably designed on a given date. A Type II says they actually operated effectively over a review period, typically three to twelve months — and that is the one sophisticated buyers want, because designing a control for a Tuesday is easy and running it for a year is not. There is no public register and no logo: the report is a confidential document shared under NDA — which is exactly what makes it useful to the teams reviewing you.

Who needs it: US-market B2B SaaS, where it has become the de facto ticket to enterprise deals — often the difference between a two-week security review and a two-quarter one. Start here: pick criteria narrowly (Security plus Availability covers most first reports), run a readiness assessment, and treat Type I as the stepping stone while your Type II observation window runs.

The common backbone: build once, map many

Seven frameworks in, you may have noticed the trick: they keep asking for the same things. Strip away the legal wrappers and a common backbone of controls satisfies large chunks of every framework at once:

  • Access control and least privilege — GDPR's security principle, HIPAA's access safeguards, PCI DSS requirements 7 and 8, SOX ITGCs, ISO's technological controls, SOC 2's common criteria. One discipline, six frameworks.
  • Encryption in transit and at rest — named or implied by every single one, and the difference between "incident" and "notifiable breach" under several.
  • Logging and monitoring — you cannot notify within 72 hours (GDPR) or 60 days (HIPAA) about something you never noticed.
  • Incident response — a rehearsed plan is the shared assumption behind every breach-notification clock.
  • Vendor management — GDPR processor contracts, HIPAA business associate agreements, ISO supplier controls, SOC 2 vendor criteria: your compliance is only as good as the companies you hand data to.
  • Data minimization and retention — data you never collected, or already deleted on schedule, is data no framework can punish you for mishandling.

The practical consequence: build the control once, then map it many times. Maintain one internal control set, each control mapped to the frameworks it satisfies — exactly what compliance automation platforms sell, and a play you can run in a spreadsheet. The warning is the mirror image: checkbox theater. Plenty of breached companies held clean audit reports, because an audit samples evidence on a schedule while attackers probe reality continuously. Compliance evidences security; it does not create it.

How to choose what applies to you

When a client asks me "which of these apply to us?", I walk the same four questions every time:

  • Where are your users? People in the EU pull in GDPR (the UK runs a parallel version). California residents at threshold volumes pull in CCPA/CPRA — and a growing list of other US states now have laws that mostly rhyme with California's. Users in India pull in the DPDP Act 2023, whose implementing rules were notified in late 2025 and phase in through 2027 with penalties up to ₹250 crore — a regime I walk through in India's IT law, explained.
  • What data do you touch? Card payments mean PCI DSS already binds you. US health data likely makes you a HIPAA business associate. Special-category data under GDPR raises the bar further.
  • What are you? A US-listed company (or one planning to be) owns SOX. A healthcare provider or insurer is a HIPAA covered entity. Sector determines the non-negotiables.
  • Who is asking? Enterprise customers demanding proof push you to SOC 2 in the US and ISO/IEC 27001 internationally. If nobody is asking yet, you may not need a certificate this year — the laws above apply either way.

Then sequence it: laws first, because they are not optional; contractual obligations next, because PCI DSS is already in force the day you take a card; and certifications when customers make them worth it. Underneath all three layers, build the backbone controls once and let each framework audit its slice. That is what compliance looks like when it works: not paperwork about security, but trust — made auditable.
