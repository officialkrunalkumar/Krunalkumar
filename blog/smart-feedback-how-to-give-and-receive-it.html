Smart feedback: how to give and receive it

Feedback is the engine of growth, and most of it is wasted — too vague to act on, too harsh to hear, or too polite to matter. After years of mentoring, here is how I try to give feedback that actually helps, and how to take it without your defenses swallowing the lesson.

Published 6 August 2026 · 4 min read · by Krunalkumar Shah

Why most feedback does nothing

"Good job." "This could be better." "I didn't love it." These feel like feedback, but they change nothing, because the person receiving them has no idea what to actually do differently. Vague praise is pleasant and useless. Vague criticism is unpleasant and useless. Both fail for the same reason: they describe a feeling instead of pointing at a specific, changeable thing.

Useful feedback is specific, actionable, and kind — and the order matters. Specific so the person knows exactly what you mean. Actionable so they know what to do next. Kind so they can actually hear it instead of bracing against it. Miss any one of those and the feedback simply gets ignored, no matter how right you were.

A simple structure I keep coming back to

When I give feedback, I try to hit three beats in order. It stops me from being vague and keeps the conversation forward-looking:

  1. Observation — the specific thing I noticed, stated as a fact, not a judgment. "This function does four different things" rather than "this is messy."
  2. Impact — why it matters. "That makes it hard to test, and harder for the next person to change safely."
  3. Path forward — a concrete next step, ideally as a question. "What if each responsibility became its own small function — how would you split it?"

The order matters. Lead with the observation so it's grounded, explain the impact so it's motivated, and end on the path forward so the person leaves with momentum instead of a wound.

Praise deserves the same rigor

We obsess over how to deliver criticism and get lazy with praise — but "great work" is just as empty as "needs work." If someone did something really well, tell them exactly what, so they can do it again on purpose. "The way you named these variables made the whole thing readable in one pass" teaches a repeatable skill. "Nice" teaches nothing. Specific praise is one of the most underused tools in mentoring, and it costs you nothing but attention.

The harder half: receiving feedback

Giving good feedback is a skill. Receiving it well is a superpower, and far rarer. The instinct when criticized is to defend — to explain why you did it that way, why the critic is missing context, why it's not really a problem. Every second spent defending is a second not spent learning. The people who grow fastest do the opposite of what feels natural.

Treat every piece of feedback as information first and a verdict never. You don't have to agree with all of it. You do have to actually hear it before you decide.

Two habits make this easier. First, ask clarifying questions before reacting — "can you show me where you saw that?" turns a defensive reflex into curiosity. Second, separate the feedback from your worth. A critique of your code, your essay, or your presentation is not a critique of you. The moment you stop taking it personally, feedback goes from something to survive to something to mine.

Build the loop, not the moment

The best mentoring relationships aren't a series of one-off verdicts; they're a loop. You give feedback, the person acts on it, you both see what happened, and the next round of feedback is sharper because of it. That loop is where the real growth happens. A single brilliant piece of feedback is nice. A trusted, repeating feedback loop is transformative — and building that loop, on both sides, is most of what good mentorship actually is.
