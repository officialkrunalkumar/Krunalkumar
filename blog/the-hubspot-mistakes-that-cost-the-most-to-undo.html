The HubSpot mistakes that cost the most to undo

Some CRM mistakes cost you an afternoon. A few cost you a quarter, and they are not the ones that look worst on the settings page. The expensive ones are expensive because, by the time you notice them, reports, workflows, forecasts and integrations are all standing on top. Here are the ones I would check first in a HubSpot portal, why each resists being undone, and what the cheap prevention would have been.

Two kinds of CRM mistake

Most of what goes wrong in a CRM is cheap. A workflow that fires at the wrong hour, a template with the wrong signature, a deal still owned by someone who left. You notice, you fix it, and by Friday nobody remembers.

The expensive mistakes have a different shape. They are usually not worse decisions at the time, just the obvious decision taken quickly. What makes them expensive is that other things get built on top. By the time the mistake is visible, reports group by it, workflows branch on it, the forecast is derived from it and two integrations write to it. The cost is not the fix. The cost is everything standing on the fix.

There is one diagnostic worth applying to all of them: if this changed, how many other things would have to change with it? If the answer is none, it is a Tuesday afternoon. If the answer is every report you have ever presented, it is structural, and structural mistakes do not get cheaper with time. They get load-bearing.

I have put no money figures on any of what follows. The cost depends entirely on how much has been built on top, and anyone quoting a number for “fixing your CRM” without seeing your portal is guessing.

The object model is the cut you only make once

HubSpot hands you contacts, companies, deals, tickets and, on some tiers, custom objects. Week one is when somebody decides which real-world thing maps to which. It feels like a naming exercise. It is the decision about what the system will ever be able to count.

Three ordinary ways to get it wrong:

  • Companies doing the work of deals. If the sale lives as properties on the company record — amount, close date, status — you can only hold one of it. A second sale to the same account overwrites the first, and you can no longer ask how often you sold to that account.
  • Contacts doing the work of a custom object. Anything with its own lifecycle and a many-to-one relationship to a person — a licence, a course enrolment — does not stay in contact properties. It starts as three fields and ends as a shadow database one person can read.
  • One pipeline for two motions. A self-serve renewal and a six-month enterprise sale share no stages, no cycle length and no exit condition. Forced into one pipeline, every stage-based number is an average of two populations that describes neither.

Why it resists repair: reporting and automation are built on the shape, not on the rows. Re-cutting the objects means rebuilding every report, workflow and integration that read the old shape, then moving history onto records that did not exist when the history happened.

Cheap prevention costs about an hour. Before creating anything, write one sentence naming the unit you sell and the unit you count, then check your three most important questions can be phrased as a query over it. The index visualiser here makes the same point in miniature: how a thing is stored decides which lookups are cheap.

Property sprawl, and the history a delete takes with it

Properties are free to create, which is the whole problem. Nobody sets out to build four fields for lead source. A form needed one, an import brought one, an integration created one with its own prefix, and a report needed a cleaned-up version of all three. Now lead_source, lead_source_new, original_source_detail and utm_source all exist, all partly populated, each right about a different slice of the database.

The damage is not the clutter. It is that reporting and automation split across the duplicates without anyone deciding they should: one dashboard groups by the first, a workflow branches on the second, a rep filters on the third. None of them agrees with the others.

Before you delete a property, export it. Deleting a property does not just remove a column, it removes the ability to ask what that value used to be — the change history hangs off the property itself. Copying today’s values into the survivor keeps the present and discards the past, which is what cohort and attribution reports are made of.

Cheap prevention: one named owner for the property list, a naming convention decided once, and a description that says who writes this property and what a blank means. Then the rule I would defend hardest — a new property needs a report or workflow that will read it.

The expensive repair has limits. Where another system holds the truth you can back-fill. Where it does not, the best outcome available is a documented seam: pick a date, say which property the values came from either side of it, and label that on every report crossing the boundary.

Stages that describe your process, not the buyer

The most common stage list reads like an internal to-do list: Qualifying, Working, Nurturing, Negotiating. Every one describes what somebody on your side is doing; none describes anything the buyer did. You cannot observe “nurturing”. Two reps will put the same deal in two different stages, both sincerely, and neither is wrong because there is nothing to be wrong about.

Stages that measure name a change in the buyer’s state, one somebody outside your company would recognise: a discovery call happened, pricing was sent, their security review passed, the contract reached their legal team. Each either occurred or did not.

Why correcting it is expensive: stage-based forecasting multiplies open pipeline by each stage’s historical conversion rate. If a stage does not correspond to a repeatable event, that rate is noise wearing a percentage sign. Fixing the stage list resets the history, so rates either side of the change are not comparable, and you will not know whether the new numbers mean anything until a full sales cycle has passed. That wait is the real cost, and no tooling shortens it.

The cheap prevention is one sentence per stage, in the stage description, naming the observable event that moves a deal into it. If you cannot name the event, the stage is a feeling.

Write the query before you design the stage. The fastest way to find a stage that cannot be counted is to try to count it. Sketch a deals table, put your stage list in it, and write the GROUP BY your pipeline review is meant to answer. The SQL compiler here runs in your browser, on invented rows.

Values nobody owns: lifecycle drift and competing writes

Lifecycle stage is not an ordinary dropdown. HubSpot date-stamps when a record first enters each stage, and cohort reporting is built on those stamps. Moving a record backwards is therefore not a correction. It is an edit to the past.

It arrives quietly, which is the point. A sync pushes another system’s idea of the stage. A re-import applies a default. A form sets Lead on a record that has been a customer for a year. Nothing errors and the record looks normal afterwards. What changed is every cohort report that counted it, retrospectively, including the ones you already presented.

That failure has a sibling: several workflows writing to the same property. Each was reasonable when it was built. Together they produce a value with no author, and “what set this?” stops having a cheap answer.

A backwards lifecycle move is silent and retroactive. HubSpot has a setting that prevents it; turn it on before you need it. Overwritten stage timestamps cannot be reconstructed from inside HubSpot, only from whatever other system still holds them.

Cheap prevention: for each property reporting depends on, decide exactly one writer, name it in the property description, and restrict edit permission. Give every integration its own staging property and let one workflow decide what the reporting property becomes.

This is the CRM-shaped version of a point I made in why your automation broke: the dangerous failure is never the one that throws an error, it is the one that succeeds quietly with the wrong value.

Identity: duplicates, and imports with no stable key

Every CRM problem becomes an identity problem eventually. Is this the same person as that one? Deduplication is the classic deferred task, because on day one there is nothing to deduplicate.

Deferred long enough it stops being a cleanup and becomes a loss. Two years of emails, calls, meetings and deal history split across two records is not repaired by merging them. HubSpot’s merge combines a great deal, but a merge is a resolution rather than a reconciliation: one record wins each conflicting value, and every report that ran across the split period was already believed.

Imports manufacture duplicates at scale. An import with no stable external identifier has to match on something human — email, company name, domain. Email changes when people change jobs. Company names differ by a comma. Domains fail for any business owning two. Each near-miss creates a record that looks new because, to the matcher, it is.

Cheap prevention: create a unique-identifier property for every system you import from, on day one, and populate it on every import even when nothing needs it yet. Then normalise before you match rather than after — case, whitespace, plus-addressing, the www. prefix. The regex tester here is a reasonable place to work those patterns out.

The expensive repair is a matching exercise against whatever still holds the true key, followed by an admission: some pairs cannot be told apart, and no data inside the CRM will settle it.

Changing production with nowhere to be wrong

The last one is not a modelling mistake. It is the process mistake that turns any of the others into an incident.

Switching on a new automation or association in a live portal surprises people the first time: enrolment is evaluated against the whole database, not the records you had in mind while building. A trigger that matches history will enrol history. If ten thousand records satisfy the condition, ten thousand enrol — and if a step sends an email, it sends ten thousand emails to people who did nothing today.

Why it is hard to undo: outbound actions are not transactions. You can switch the workflow off in seconds. You cannot un-send.

Cheap prevention: build in a sandbox if your subscription includes one; if not, add a hard filter on a test property so only records you chose can qualify; replace the outbound step with something inert for the first run; and read the enrolment count before switching it on. That count is the most useful number on the screen and the one people skip.

The limit of that advice: discipline substitutes for a sandbox imperfectly. It catches the mistakes you thought to filter for, and the interesting ones are those you did not think of.

If you are already in it

Two caveats, because that list reads harsher than I mean it.

First, most of these are only visible in hindsight. Imagine a company with one product and one sale per customer. Putting revenue on the company record is not sloppiness there; it is the correct model for the business that exists, and it became wrong when a second product appeared. No amount of care in week one would have surfaced that.

Second, the small company choosing the simple option is usually right. Custom objects, a second pipeline and a governance process are real costs paid now against a hypothetical saving later, and “model it properly from day one” is advice usually given by someone who will not be paying for it. What I would defend is the simple option plus one written line naming what would have to change for this shape to stop working. That is management rather than design, and in the four M’s framing management is the pillar holding the other three upright.

If several of these describe your portal, fix them in the order of how much depends on them, not the order they annoy you. The property your forecast reads comes before four duplicate lead-source fields.

The seven, the cheap prevention, and the repair once something is built on top.
What goes wrong Cheap prevention Expensive repair
Object model chosen wrong Name the unit you sell and the unit you count first. Re-cut the objects, rebuild everything built on the old shape.
Duplicated properties One owner, one convention, no property without a reader. Back-fill from elsewhere, or label a dated seam on every report.
Stages describing your process One sentence per stage naming an observable buyer event. Remap deals, reset conversion history, wait out a sales cycle.
Lifecycle stage moving backwards Turn on the setting that blocks it; restrict edit rights. Overwritten timestamps are gone; cohort reports stay wrong.
Several workflows writing one property One writer per reporting property, named in its description. Read workflow histories record by record, then rebuild the write path.
Duplicates and keyless imports A unique external id per source system, from the first import. Match on the true key elsewhere, merge with losses, flag the rest.
Automation switched on in production A test-property filter, an inert first run, read the enrolment count. Switch it off in seconds and live with what already went out.

The pattern is the same every time. Something was chosen quickly, other things grew on top, and the growth is what makes the original choice expensive. The cheapest moment to get an object model, a stage list or an identifier right is before anything reads it. The second cheapest is now.
