The life of the Buddha, without the mythology

Almost everything most of us know about the Buddha arrived through a long chain of retellings, and a good deal of it was added centuries after he died. That does not make the story less interesting — it makes it more so, because underneath the gold leaf there is a person who lived, taught for forty-five years, and was unusually insistent that nobody take his word for anything.

What we can actually say about him

He was born Siddhattha Gotama — Siddhārtha Gautama in Sanskrit — into the Śākya people, in or near Lumbini in what is now Nepal. His father, Suddhodana, led the clan. The Śākyas were a small oligarchic republic rather than a kingdom, which already sits awkwardly with the "prince in a palace" image.

Even his dates are unsettled. Traditional reckoning gives roughly 563–483 BCE; a good deal of modern scholarship prefers a later window, closer to 480–400 BCE. A century of uncertainty about someone this significant is a useful reminder of how thin the ground is.

What we have is the Pali Canon, written down in Sri Lanka around the first century BCE after generations of careful oral transmission, along with parallel collections preserved in Chinese and Sanskrit. Where those independent lines agree, we are on reasonably firm ground. Where a story appears only in later commentary, it is later commentary — which is not the same as false, but is worth knowing.

The four sights, and where they come from

The famous version: a sheltered prince, kept from all suffering, ventures out and encounters an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a wandering ascetic — and leaves that night.

It is a superb piece of storytelling, and in that fully-developed form it belongs to later biographical literature, principally the Nidānakathā commentary, composed many centuries after his death.

The earliest texts are quieter and, to my ear, more affecting. In one discourse he simply describes noticing that he was himself subject to ageing, sickness and death, and that this made his own comfort feel hollow. There is no dramatic outing. There is a young man of some privilege who registers a fact everybody knows and is unable to un-notice it.

The legend has him shocked by the discovery of suffering. The early texts have him disturbed by the ordinary fact of it — which is a thing that can happen to anyone, on any afternoon.

Six years of getting it wrong

He left, and he studied. He learned meditative attainments from two teachers, Āḷāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta, mastered what each had to offer, and concluded in both cases that it did not go far enough.

Then he tried severity, and he tried it to the edge of death — extreme fasting, breath restriction, the full ascetic programme, for years, with five companions. It nearly killed him and it did not work.

This period matters more than it usually gets credit for. The central teaching that came later — the middle way between indulgence and self-torture — is not an abstract philosophical position. It is the conclusion of someone who had genuinely tried both ends and found each of them empty. He accepted food, his five companions left in disgust at what they read as failure of nerve, and he continued alone.

Under the tree

At Bodh Gaya he sat beneath a pipal tree and did not get up until something resolved. What he described afterwards was not a vision or a message received but an understanding: of how suffering arises, what feeds it, and that it can stop.

He was, by the texts' own account, initially reluctant to teach at all — doubting that what he had seen could be conveyed. That hesitation is preserved rather than tidied away, which is one of several places where the early record keeps something a purely promotional account would have removed.

The first teaching

He walked to Isipatana, the deer park at what is now Sarnath, and found the five who had abandoned him. The discourse he gave there — the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (SN 56.11) — is the closest thing we have to a founding document.

It contains two things. First, the middle way, framed as the avoidance of two extremes: pursuit of sensual pleasure, and self-mortification. Second, the four noble truths, which read less like doctrine than like a physician's sequence:

  • There is dukkha — usually rendered "suffering", though "unsatisfactoriness" catches it better: the friction of a life in which nothing stays.
  • It has an origin, in craving and clinging.
  • Because it has an origin, it can cease.
  • There is a path that leads to that cessation.

Diagnosis, cause, prognosis, treatment. Whatever else it is, it is a strikingly practical structure.

Forty-five years of walking

He then taught for roughly forty-five years across the Gangetic plain, on foot, in the ordinary language of the people he was talking to rather than in Sanskrit. He built a monastic community that admitted people regardless of caste — genuinely radical in that setting. He accepted women into it, after initial reluctance and under additional rules, which the texts record without smoothing over.

He died at Kusinārā, around eighty, apparently of illness following a meal. The Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (DN 16) gives the account, including the entirely human detail of his last months: back pain, exhaustion, and a body he compares to a worn-out cart held together with straps.

What he said about his own authority

This is the part I find most worth carrying, and it is well attested.

In the discourse to the Kālāmas (AN 3.65), approached by people bewildered at competing teachers each dismissing the others, he does not tell them he is the correct one. He tells them not to go by report, by tradition, by hearsay, by scripture, by logical conjecture, or by respect for a teacher — but to see for themselves what leads to harm and what leads to welfare, and to act on that.

And in DN 16, near the end, he tells his followers to dwell with themselves as their own refuge and the teaching as their refuge, seeking no other. The Pali word there, dīpa, has been translated both as "lamp" and as "island"; scholars generally favour island, and the sense is much the same — the ground you stand on is your own.

Most quotations you will see attributed to the Buddha on the internet are not his. Many are nineteenth- or twentieth-century inventions, some are Theosophy, some are simply invented for the caption. If a line has no sutta reference, treat it as anonymous — the verses on the still page each carry their citation for exactly this reason.

What survives the stripping

Take away the miracles, the prophecies at birth, the palace, the elaborate later cosmology, and something quite solid remains: a man who observed that human beings suffer largely through wanting things to be other than they are, who tested two opposite prescriptions to their limits and rejected both, who found a third approach, and who then spent his remaining decades walking around explaining it to anyone who would listen — while telling them repeatedly not to believe it just because he said it.

You do not have to accept any metaphysics to find that a serious way to have spent a life.
