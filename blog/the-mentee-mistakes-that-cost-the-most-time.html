Mentorship

The mentee mistakes that cost the most time

I have mentored people in the United States, in India and in Taiwan — different universities, different job markets, different times of day. The mistakes do not vary. What follows is the list I keep in my head: what each habit is, why it feels like the responsible choice while you are doing it, what it actually costs, and the specific different action to take instead. Some of these were mine.

Why these are the expensive ones

There is a companion to this post written from the other chair: what running internship cohorts taught me about mentorship is about what the mentor gets wrong. This one is about the mentee, and it is the harder of the two to write, because none of these mistakes look like mistakes from inside. Every one of them is a form of diligence pointed a few degrees off.

That is what makes them expensive. Nobody loses six months to laziness — laziness announces itself, and the person doing it knows. Six months goes to activity that resembles progress closely enough that neither the person nor anyone watching them raises an objection. The cost is not bad code. The cost is calendar time, and calendar time is the one input in a career that cannot be borrowed back.

A caveat on what this list is. It is a pattern description drawn from a lot of people over a lot of years, not a set of case studies, and nobody reading it will recognise themselves in every item. If you recognise two, that is the normal result, and the two you recognise are worth more to you than the seven you do not.

1. The tutorial loop

You finish a course. You start another. The notes get longer, the list of technologies you have "done" gets longer, and every evening ends with something on the screen that works.

Why it feels right. Because it is genuinely effortful, it is genuinely instructive, and it produces a completion every single day. Very little else in learning offers that. It also removes the two most uncomfortable parts of building software — deciding what to make, and sitting in front of a problem with no guarantee that it is solvable by you — while leaving the pleasant part, which is watching a thing come together under your hands.

What it actually costs. Recognition is not recall. Following an argument you can see is a different skill from producing one, and only the first is being trained. Because the loop returns confidence on every pass, the gap between what you believe you can build and what you can build widens quietly the whole time. The bill arrives at the first blank file, and it arrives disguised: it presents as evidence about you rather than as the predictable result of practising the wrong half of the skill. That misreading is the real damage, and I have watched it push capable people out of the field entirely.

The substitute. Not "do fewer tutorials". After any tutorial you finish, close it, open a blank file, and rebuild the same thing with one requirement changed — a different data source, one extra field, output as a file instead of to the screen. You will stall somewhere. That stall is the actual lesson, and it is precise: it tells you the one thing you did not learn, and you can go back for that thing alone. Then invert the order permanently. The tutorial follows the problem; it never precedes it.

2. Starting over instead of finishing

The project is about seventy percent done. The structure you chose in week one looks naive now. You know so much more than you did. A clean start would take a weekend and the result would be something you are not embarrassed by.

Why it feels right. Because the reasoning is sound and the judgement is real. You genuinely do know more, the structure genuinely is worse than what you would choose today, and rewrites genuinely are sometimes correct. It is also the only form of avoidance that produces a commit on the first day.

What it actually costs. Everything you have not done before lives in the last thirty percent. Error handling for input you did not imagine. The deployment. The configuration that differs between your laptop and anywhere else. The moment you have to name things well enough for a stranger to follow. Writing down what it is for. A restart returns you to the opening stretch, which is the part you are already fluent in — so four restarts buy you four beginnings and no endings, and the fluency you gain is fluency at beginning. It also denies you the only thing that compounds: a finished artefact you can point at, extend, and be asked questions about.

The substitute. A rule with no exceptions: you may not restart, you may only refactor, and the project has to run at the end of every session. Keep a file called rewrite.md and put every structural regret in it as you have it — that satisfies the impulse without spending the calendar. If the rewrite is genuinely justified, it will still be justified after you have shipped the ugly version to one person, and by then the file will tell you which parts actually needed changing rather than which parts merely looked old.

3. Asking for a roadmap instead of picking a problem

"What should I learn first?" "Can you give me a roadmap?" "What is the right order?" I get this more than any other question, by a wide margin.

Why it feels right. Sequencing does matter, and asking someone further along seems like the efficient move — better to spend an hour getting the plan right than a month learning something out of order. It is also the polite question, the one that shows you are taking this seriously.

What it actually costs. A roadmap is a shopping list with no forcing function. It cannot tell you when an item is done, so every item stays permanently half-complete and the honest answer to "do I know HTTP yet" is always no. It absorbs weeks in the collecting and comparing of roadmaps, which feels like preparation and is in fact the third pattern on this list doing what it does best. And the answer you get back is generic because the question is generic: the person answering has no idea what you are trying to build, so they hand you a compression of their own path, shaped by constraints you do not have.

The substitute. Bring a problem instead of a request for a plan. "I want this spreadsheet to update itself every morning and I cannot work out how to get the data out" is a question with a five-minute answer that will change your week. The order of learning then falls out of the problem — you learn what an HTTP request is because your thing has to make one, and that ordering is remembered because it arrived attached to a reason. If your uncertainty is genuinely about direction rather than about sequence, that is a different question and worth treating as one; I wrote about it separately in finding the right career.

4. Optimising the tool choice before the first line

Two weeks of comparisons before any code exists. Which framework, which language, which database, which editor, which hosting, which package manager. Long threads of strong opinions, all of them confident and several of them contradictory.

Why it feels right. Because getting it wrong sounds expensive. Everyone has heard about the migration that took a year. Choosing carefully feels like the mature, consequences-aware behaviour, and the reading you do is real reading, not idle time.

What it actually costs. The decision is reversible and the deliberation is not. Two weeks of comparison produce no code, no evidence and no experience of the thing being compared — and the comparison is being made by the person least equipped to make it, which is you before you have built anything with either option. Worse, it is a socially unimpeachable way to not start. Nobody criticises you for researching carefully, so nothing interrupts it. At this stage the difference between two reasonable tools is smaller than the difference between shipping and not shipping, and it is not close.

The substitute. Put a timer on it. One hour, then choose the option with the dullest documentation and the largest number of ordinary answered questions on the internet, because at this stage the thing you are optimising for is the availability of help, not the benchmark. Write one line in the repository saying what you picked, the date, and the reason. Reopen the decision only when you hit a wall you can describe in a sentence. Most of the time you never will, and that line ages into a small, honest record of how you thought.

5. Refusing to read source code

The library does something you did not expect. You reach for the documentation, then a search engine, then a forum, then an assistant. What you do not do is open the file that contains the function you just called.

Why it feels right. A dependency presents itself as a black box, and treating it as one is what the abstraction is for. Reading somebody else's code feels like it requires a seniority you do not have yet, and the first attempt confirms it: you open a file, understand little of it, and reasonably conclude that this is a thing you are not ready for.

What it actually costs. It caps you at the problems the documentation anticipated. Every real bug lives in the gap between what the documentation says and what the code does, and that gap is only ever closed by reading. Debugging without it is guessing with extra steps — and the guesses get slower as systems get larger, so the ceiling arrives later but it does arrive. In my experience this is the single largest difference between someone who is two years in and someone who is two years in and rarely stuck. You also forfeit the only reliable way to see how experienced people structure real code under real constraints, which is not visible in any tutorial, because tutorials are written to be understood rather than to survive.

The substitute. Today, once: open a dependency you already use, find the function you call most, read it to the end of its body, and follow exactly one call it makes. Twenty minutes, and you are allowed to not understand most of it — comprehension is not the goal on the first pass, familiarity with the act is. Then adopt the standing rule: when behaviour surprises you, the first move is to open the source of the thing that surprised you, before the search. It will feel slower for about a fortnight and then it will not.

Practise reading rather than trusting. The git objects lab on this site takes a repository apart and shows you the four object types the commands are built on, so you can see the data structure instead of accepting a description of it. It is a small, safe example of the habit this section is asking for: look at the thing itself. Everything runs in your browser and nothing you open is uploaded.

6. Silence for three days, or a question in three minutes

These look like opposite failings and they are the same one: no calibration of the point at which your own time stops paying.

The silent version. It feels right because independence was praised in you at some point, because asking looks like an admission, and because you do not want to spend a busy person's attention on something you suspect is obvious. What it costs is a fifteen-minute answer bought for three days — and the three days were not spent learning. The first hour was learning. The rest was re-reading the same page with decreasing hope. There is a second cost people miss: what a team finds expensive is not your questions, it is not knowing where you are. A blocked person who says so is a scheduling fact. A blocked person who is quiet is a surprise on Friday.

The immediate version. It feels right because it looks efficient — why burn an hour on something someone can answer in a minute. What it costs is the part that installs the memory. Retrieval is built by the search, not by the answer; solutions you were handed decay in days, and solutions you fought for do not. There is also a quieter effect: you train the person helping you to hand over answers, and once that pattern sets, what they offer you next changes. People stop giving hints to someone who reliably wants the solution.

The substitute. Decide the limit before you begin, because the judgement you need is the one the problem takes away from you. Twenty minutes to an hour is where most things sit. When the limit passes, do not send "I am stuck". Send four lines: what you are trying to do, what you tried in order, what happened exactly with the error pasted in full, and what you currently think is going on. Writing that message answers the question about a third of the time before you send it — that is not a trick, it is the struggle compressed into ten minutes — and when it does not, it converts a twenty-message exchange into one reply.

The mentor's half of this same window is the hardest part of the job, and it is the reason the companion post calls mentorship mostly restraint: let them get stuck, never let them stay stuck alone. Both halves are guesses about the same boundary. Telling your mentor where your limit was set makes their guess much better.

7. Projects nobody has a reason to use

The todo application. The weather dashboard. The clone of a large product, at about four percent of its scope. Built carefully, never used by anyone, listed on a portfolio page.

Why it feels right. Because you were told to have projects, and these are the projects everyone names. They demonstrate a stack. They are also, quietly, the safest thing you can build: the requirements are known, the reference implementation exists, and nobody will complain about the result, because nobody will use the result.

What it actually costs. A project with no user generates no constraints, and constraints are where the learning comes from. Nothing ever arrives in a format you did not plan for. Nothing has to keep working while you are asleep. You never discover that the word you chose for the main button means something else to everyone but you. And it fails at the job you built it for: an interviewer cannot tell your clone from the fifty other clones on the same day, and cannot ask you a question whose answer distinguishes you from someone who followed a video. What makes a project worth discussing is a decision you had to make and can defend. Copies contain no decisions.

The substitute. Build the thing that annoys you or annoys someone sitting near you, every week, in a way you can describe in one sentence. Three requirements and no more: one named person who will use it, one thing it does, and a place they can reach it that is not your laptop. It can be far smaller than you think — a script that renames files the way one team needs them renamed is a better interview subject than a half-built social network, because it has a user, and a user produces stories. That is the practical half of standing out in an internship interview: not a longer list, one real thing with real friction attached.

8. Chasing the newest framework

Something is announced. The timeline says the thing you were learning is finished. You put down what you were doing and start again with the new one, four months after doing the same thing.

Why it feels right. Job posts name the new thing. Being early looks like leverage, and occasionally it is. There is also an uncomfortable truth underneath: for someone without depth anywhere, a new tool is the one arena where nobody has depth, so it briefly feels like an even contest.

What it actually costs. You restart the beginner phase every time, and the beginner phase is the least valuable part of any tool. You never reach the depth where the interesting problems live, because that depth is exactly what you keep discarding. And because each tool is learned at the surface, you accumulate memorised procedures rather than understanding — which is why the next tool feels just as hard as the last one, when it should have felt easier. Frameworks are largely rearrangements of ideas that do not move: a request arrives, work is scheduled, data is stored and looked up, something is cached, something fails and has to be retried. Learn those once and every framework is a dialect.

The honest limit. This is not an argument for ignoring new things — some genuinely change the shape of the work, and refusing to look is its own failure mode, usually arriving about a decade into a career. The test I use is whether you can say, in one sentence and with an example from your own work, what this thing does that your current tool could not. If you cannot, it is news rather than a tool, and news can be read in ten minutes rather than adopted over three months.

The substitute. Split your learning time deliberately and write the split down. Most of it goes to things that do not expire — what a request actually costs, why a query with an index is a different shape of work from one without, how the size of your input changes the time your loop takes, how a process gets memory and what happens when it cannot. Those are all available to practise on this site: HTTP versions, database indexes, and algorithmic cost. The rest goes to whatever you actually ship in. That ratio is the difference between a decade of experience and one year of experience repeated ten times, which is the argument I made at greater length in how to succeed in software development.

9. Treating feedback as a verdict

A review comes back with thirty comments. You read it as thirty counts of an indictment, and you either argue every point or go silent for two days.

Why it feels right. Because feedback arrives in the same register as a grade, and fifteen years of school taught that evaluation is a permanent judgement about you rather than a description of an object. Nobody untrains that on their own. It is also true that some feedback really is a verdict badly disguised, so the instinct is not paranoid, it is over-applied.

What it actually costs. Both responses cost you the same thing, which is the reviewer's attention — the scarcest resource you have access to and the only one nobody sells. Nobody announces this. A reviewer who has had two uncomfortable rounds with you simply starts approving with fewer comments, and it will read as improvement. Arguing spends the attention immediately; silence spends it slowly. Meanwhile the information in the comments, which was the point, goes unread.

The substitute. A mechanical move, done on paper until it becomes a habit. Split every comment into what it observes and what it prescribes. "This function is hard to follow" is an observation, and it is true by construction — the reader is the authority on whether they could follow it, and no amount of explaining will make it retroactively followable. "Split it into three" is a prescription, and you may decline it with a reason. Reply with what you changed and what you did not and why, one line each. Then ask the question that most people never ask: what is one thing I should do differently before the next review. The answer to that is worth more than the review that prompted it. There is a fuller treatment of both directions in smart feedback: how to give and receive it.

The pattern under all nine. Each one removes a specific discomfort: the blank file, the unfamiliar last thirty percent, the choice with no right answer, the code you do not understand, the admission that you are stuck, the person who might not like your thing. They are not laziness. They are avoidance in the shape of diligence, and every one of them defers the same moment — the point at which something you made meets someone who did not make it.

The one that costs the most

If I could remove one habit from everyone I have mentored, it would not be any of the nine. It would be this: never putting anything in front of a real user.

It is the most expensive because it is the one that keeps all the others alive. A thing nobody has used cannot be wrong yet. It is still potential, and potential is comfortable in a way that a small working tool with two complaints against it is not. Every pattern above is, in the end, a way of extending that comfort — one more tutorial before I start, one more restart before I show it, one more comparison before the first line, one more feature before it is ready.

A small tool that five people use teaches more than a large one nobody uses, and in my experience it is not close. Five specific reasons, none of them motivational:

  • A user does things you cannot imagine. Your test data comes out of your own model of the problem, so it can never contradict that model. The fastest way to find out where your model is wrong is to hand the thing to someone whose model differs — which happens within about four minutes, usually with an input you would have called unreasonable.
  • A user forces the parts you would otherwise skip. It has to run somewhere that is not your laptop. It must not lose their data. It has to be explainable in one sentence. It has to survive being used at eleven at night while you are asleep. Every one of those is a skill, none of them appear in a project with no user, and all of them are what the job is mostly made of.
  • A user produces a correctly ordered list of what to do next. Which is the roadmap you were asking for in section three — except this one is about your actual work, arrives in priority order, and is right. Nobody can hand you that. It only comes back from something that exists.
  • Support is where the judgement comes from. The first bug report teaches you about the bug. The second one about the same thing teaches you why defaults matter, why error messages are part of the product, and why the fix that removes the whole class of report is worth more than the fix that removes this one. That lesson is not available anywhere else, and it is the beginning of design sense.
  • Five is genuinely enough. Five people who will reply honestly beat five thousand who will not. Scale teaches real things — contention, cost, the failure that only appears at volume — but it teaches them later, and none of them are the lesson you need first.

What this looks like in practice, this month: pick something you can finish across two weekends, name the one person who will use it before you start, put it somewhere they can reach, and ask them once a week what annoyed them. Fix the top item. That loop is the entire curriculum, and everything on this list is a way of not entering it. It is also, not coincidentally, what I try to hand people in a cohort rather than teach them — real work, small stakes, a real recipient — because the loop teaches more reliably than I do.

The gap between the ninth-best portfolio project and the best one is small and nobody can see it. The gap between a thing five people use and a thing nobody uses is the whole of the difference, and everyone can see it, including you.

The substitutes in one place

None of these substitutes are "try harder" or "be more disciplined", because that advice has never once worked on anyone including me. Each is a different specific action, small enough to start this week.

The nine patterns, what each one actually costs, and the specific action that replaces it.
The pattern What it costs The specific substitute
The tutorial loop Confidence that grows while capability does not, and a bill that arrives at the first blank file. Rebuild the same thing from empty with one requirement changed. Tutorials follow problems, never precede them.
Starting over instead of finishing Repeated practice at the part you are already good at, and no finished artefact to be asked about. Never restart, only refactor, and keep it running at the end of every session. Put regrets in a file, not in a new repository.
Asking for a roadmap Weeks spent collecting plans, and a generic answer because the question was generic. Bring one concrete problem instead. Let the order of learning fall out of what the problem needs next.
Optimising the tool choice Irreversible deliberation spent on a reversible decision, by the person least able to make it. One hour, pick the boring option with the most answered questions, write down the date and the reason, move.
Refusing to read source code A ceiling at whatever the documentation anticipated, and debugging that stays guesswork. Read one function in one dependency today. Then: when behaviour surprises you, open the source before the search.
Staying silent for days A fifteen-minute answer paid for with three days, most of them spent re-reading. Set the limit before you start. At the limit, send what you are trying, what you tried, what happened, what you suspect.
Asking before trying The retrieval that only the search installs, plus a helper who learns to hand over answers. Spend the twenty minutes first. Arrive with your attempts attached so the reply can be a hint rather than a solution.
Projects nobody uses No constraints, therefore no learning, and nothing an interviewer can ask you a distinguishing question about. One named user, one thing it does, one place they can reach it. Smaller than you think, and actually in use.
Chasing the newest framework The beginner phase on repeat, and memorised procedure where understanding should be. Most of your time on what does not expire; the rest on what you ship in. Adopt only what you can justify in one sentence.
Feedback as a verdict The reviewer's attention, spent quickly by arguing or slowly by going quiet. Separate observation from prescription. Reply with what changed and what did not. Ask what to do differently next time.

Which of these were mine

It would be dishonest to present this as a list assembled purely from watching other people. Several of these were mine, and the sections that came out longest are the ones I understand from the inside rather than from the other chair.

The tool deliberation was mine — I could produce a genuinely careful comparison of options I had never used, and it took me a long time to notice that carefulness and progress are not the same measurement. Source-code avoidance was mine too, and it was the more expensive of the two. What changed it was research rather than resolve: you cannot make a claim about how a system behaves under stress by reading a description of how it is supposed to behave, so at some point the reading stopped being optional. The habit turned out to transfer to everything, and it remains the single change to how I work that returned the most.

I am not going to dress that up with a specific story or a number. The useful part is only this: these patterns are not evidence of a defect, they are what the incentives produce, and they are unlearned by changing one action at a time rather than by resolving to be different. What I cannot do from a blog post is tell you which two are yours. That is the first useful thing a mentor does, and it usually takes about one conversation with your actual work open in front of both of you.

Questions people ask

How long should I stay stuck before I ask?

Set the limit before you start, not while you are inside the problem, because the judgement you need is exactly the one the problem has taken away from you. Twenty minutes to an hour is where most things sit. The signal that the window has closed is not the clock alone — it is that you have stopped generating new hypotheses and started re-reading the same page hoping it will say something different. When that happens you are not learning any more, you are only waiting.

Are tutorials always a waste of time?

No, and I would not want that read into this. A tutorial is an efficient way to get the shape of something unfamiliar in an hour. The failure is not the tutorial, it is the loop — finishing one and starting another with nothing in between. The rule worth holding is that the tutorial follows a problem rather than preceding it. You hit something you cannot do, you go and read how it is done, you come back and do it in your own thing. That is a tool. A queue of tutorials with no project attached is a hobby that resembles work.

What should the first thing I ship actually be?

Something you can finish across two weekends, that does one thing, for one person you can name. The best candidates are annoyances that already exist near you: a report someone assembles by hand every Friday, a rename somebody does forty times a week, a reminder that keeps getting missed. Deliberately not a clone of a large product, and deliberately not something that needs accounts, payments and a mobile app before anyone can touch it. Small is not a compromise here — small is what lets it exist at all, and existing is the entire point.

I do not have any users. Where do the first five come from?

From people you already have a reason to talk to. A classmate, a sibling, two people in a group chat about a hobby you share, the person whose spreadsheet you are replacing, a maintainer whose issue tracker you read. They do not have to be strangers and they do not have to be many. What makes a user useful is that they will tell you honestly when it breaks, which is a property of the relationship rather than of the number.

Should I finish a project I have lost interest in?

Usually yes, and I say that knowing how unwelcome it is. The loss of interest very often arrives at exactly the point where the remaining work is unfamiliar rather than boring — deployment, error handling, the parts that only start to matter once someone else touches it. That is the part you have not done before, and therefore the part with the learning in it. There is a real exception: if the premise itself was wrong and nobody wants the thing, stopping is the correct call. The test is whether you can say what changed your mind in one sentence, without using the word boring.

How do I tell useful feedback from noise?

Split every comment into what it observes and what it prescribes. The observation is almost always true and almost always worth having, because it is a report about someone's experience of your work and they are the authority on that. The prescription is one person's suggested fix, and you may take it or not provided you can say why. Noise is the small residue that observes nothing and prescribes nothing — the comment that is only a verdict. Treat that as information about the reviewer's day rather than about your code, and go back to the comments that named something.

I have been in the tutorial loop for a year. Is it too late?

No, and the recovery is faster than people fear, because the knowledge is not absent — it is unpractised. What is missing is retrieval and decision-making, and both come back quickly once something forces them. The way back is unglamorous and takes about two weekends: pick one small problem, build it badly from a blank file, put it in front of one person, and fix the first thing they complain about. The year is not lost. It has simply not been converted into anything yet, and that is the conversion.

If you read this and recognised two of them, the recognition is the whole of the value and the next move is small: pick one substitute from the table and do it this week. None of them require permission, a course, or a better plan than the one you have.
