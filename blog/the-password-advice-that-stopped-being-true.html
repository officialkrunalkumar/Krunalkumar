The password advice that stopped being true

Mix upper case, lower case, a digit and a symbol. Change it every ninety days. Never paste it in. Three of those rules are now formally recommended against by the same bodies that gave them to us, and the man who wrote the original guidance has said publicly that he regrets it. Here is what changed, who changed it, why each rule failed, and what is actually true now.

Published 31 August 2026 · 10 min read · by Krunalkumar Shah

Where the old rules came from

Almost everyone reading this was taught the same four things about passwords. Mix upper case, lower case, a digit and a symbol. Change it every ninety days. Never write it down. Never paste it in.

Three of those are now formally recommended against by the standards bodies that popularised them, and the fourth has been reversed in spirit. Named people changed them, in named documents, for stated reasons — and the reasons are more useful than the rules.

The composition rules came from NIST Special Publication 800-63, published in 2003, and specifically from its Appendix A on estimating password strength. That appendix was written by Bill Burr, then a manager at NIST. It was not careless work. It was work done with almost no real password data — nobody published corpuses of real credentials in 2003 — leaning instead on older research into how guessing worked on mainframes. The advice followed logically from its model of attacker behaviour. The model was what turned out to be wrong.

In 2017 Burr told the Wall Street Journal that he regretted much of it; the line reported was “Much of what I did I now regret.” That is not a gotcha. It is a researcher publicly marking his own work as superseded, which is what you want from the people who write standards, and which rarely gets heard.

What NIST says now

The reversal lives in NIST SP 800-63B, Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication and Lifecycle Management. Its section on memorised secrets — NIST’s term for passwords — is short and unusually blunt. Paraphrasing what changed most:

  • No composition rules. Do not demand particular mixes of character types.
  • No arbitrary expiry. No periodic forced changes — but a forced change is required on evidence of compromise.
  • Allow long secrets. At least sixty-four characters, all printable ASCII and the space, Unicode permitted.
  • Do not truncate. Cutting a password short throws away the strength the user supplied.
  • Allow paste. Stated explicitly, so that password managers work.
  • Check against known-bad lists. Breached corpuses, dictionary words, sequential strings, and context-specific words like the service’s own name.
  • No hints, no security questions. A hint retrievable without authenticating is out, and knowledge-based authentication goes with it.
  • Rate limit, and store properly. Salted and hashed with a deliberately slow key derivation function, not a fast general-purpose hash.

Later revisions continue in the same direction and push the recommended minimum length upward. I am summarising from memory rather than quoting clauses, and NIST renumbers sections between revisions. Cite the current text, not me.

One caution before you change a policy. The standards moved; plenty of compliance questionnaires and internal checklists did not. Dropping expiry can be correct and still raise a finding, because the auditor is reading a template written years ago. Change the policy and its evidence together.

The NCSC arrived at the same place

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre reached the same conclusions on its own timeline, and its phrasing is the one most people have actually met: three random words.

NCSC guidance advises organisations to drop routine password expiry, to stop imposing complexity requirements, to let staff use password managers, to block the most common passwords rather than policing the shape of the rest, and to concentrate effort on the accounts that matter most. Its argument against expiry is a usability argument with a security conclusion: forcing regular changes makes people choose weaker passwords and write them down, so the control degrades the thing it protects.

Two bodies, two countries, different evidence bases, the same answer. That convergence is the strongest part of the case. One standard changing its mind can be fashion; two arriving separately at the same place is closer to a finding.

Why each old rule failed

“The experts changed their minds” convinces nobody. Each rule failed for a mechanical reason.

Composition rules

A rule demanding an upper case letter, a digit and a symbol does not make people choose randomly. It makes them apply the same small set of transformations to the word they were going to use anyway. Capitalise the first letter, digit on the end, symbol is an exclamation mark, a becomes @.

Cracking tools have modelled those transformations for decades: hashcat and John the Ripper both ship rule engines whose whole purpose is to apply human mangling patterns to a wordlist. So the composition rule does not expand the space an attacker must search. It describes that space. Watch it in the hash cracker lab: a wordlist with mangling finds shaped passwords long before brute force gets near them.

Ninety-day rotation

Forced expiry assumes a stolen password stays useful for months and that the scheduled reset ends the exposure. Both halves are shaky. Stolen credentials get used quickly, and if you know a password was taken, waiting for the calendar is not a plan.

What rotation reliably produces is incremental passwords: Autumn2025! becomes Winter2026!. Researchers at UNC Chapel Hill showed that a transform-based attack, given a user’s previous password, recovered a meaningful share of their next one — their result, not mine; I have not reproduced it and will not quote a figure I have not run. The second effect is less measurable and probably larger: a password you must change quarterly is one you write down.

Blocking paste

Fields that reject paste are usually defended as protection against malware or brute force. Neither survives contact: malware on the machine already has the keyboard, and brute force does not use your browser form. What blocking paste does is break password managers, pushing people back to passwords they can retype — short and reused. It prevents nothing and removes the largest improvement available to an ordinary user.

What is actually true now

Four claims, in the order I would defend them.

Length beats character classes. For a secret drawn at random, guessing effort tracks how many equally likely possibilities it came from, and adding characters multiplies that number far faster than adding character types does. This is where articles print a table of crack times. I am not going to: those numbers depend on the hash function, the hardware and the wordlist, and changing any one moves the answer by orders of magnitude. The password lab computes it in your own tab with the hash function as an input you pick. The salt and rainbow table lab makes the same point from the defender’s side.

The caveat matters more than the claim: this is about randomly generated secrets. A long password that is a line from a song is not long in the sense that counts, because the attacker is guessing lyrics, not characters. “Three random words” carries all its weight on random.

Uniqueness beats complexity. The most common way an account is lost is not that somebody guessed the password. It is that they already had it, from somewhere else. A complex password reused on two sites is worth less than two mediocre passwords used once each. The breach check lab tells you whether a password is already in the public corpuses, by k-anonymity: five characters of a hash go out, the comparison happens locally. When a notification lands, reading it properly tells you which accounts need work.

A manager beats memory. Every rule above is impossible to follow by hand at the scale of a modern life. A manager makes uniqueness and length free at once, which is why blocking paste was actively harmful.

Multi-factor beats all of it. A second factor turns a stolen password from a compromise into an inconvenience. Take that one action if you take none of the others — but the factors are not interchangeable.

Test the claim rather than trusting it. The password lab measures entropy instead of scoring shape, then subtracts for the patterns crackers try first. Type something that satisfies every composition rule you were ever given and watch what it scores. Nothing is transmitted.

Second factors, ranked honestly

“Turn on two-factor” hides a wide quality range. Weakest to strongest, with reasons.

SMS codes — weakest, still worth having

A texted code is bound to a phone number, and a phone number is an account at a telecoms company that can be socially engineered, ported or swapped. That is a SIM swap, and it never requires touching your handset. Signalling interception and malware that reads notifications are the other routes. NIST flagged SMS delivery as restricted years ago.

TOTP apps — better, still phishable

An authenticator app deriving six digits from a shared secret and the clock removes the carrier entirely; nobody can port an authenticator app, and the TOTP lab walks through each step. But the code is still a string you can be talked into typing into the wrong window: a relay phishing page asks for it and replays it inside its validity window, in real time. That is the shared weakness of every factor a human retypes. Push approvals remove the typing and with it the relay, but introduce prompt fatigue; number matching largely closes that.

Passkeys and hardware keys — phishing-resistant, and here is why

A passkey is a key pair whose private half never leaves your authenticator, and the property that matters is not the cryptography. It is that the credential is bound to the site’s origin: the browser tells the authenticator which origin is asking, that origin forms part of the signed data, and an authenticator will not sign for a site the credential was not registered to. A lookalike domain gets nothing to replay. The passkey lab registers a real credential against this origin and takes the response apart byte by byte.

The honest limit: phishing-resistant sign-in does not protect a session that has already started. An attacker who steals a session cookie after a legitimate login is past the front door however well it was locked, and that is what a modern phishing kit is built to collect. Session lifetimes and device binding are the controls there, not passkeys.

Do not let the ranking talk you out of the weak option. SMS is the worst second factor and still a large improvement over a password alone. If a service offers nothing better, turn it on and migrate later.

What is still contested

The consensus is not total, and pretending otherwise would repeat the 2003 mistake. Three open questions.

Do hints ever help?

NIST is unambiguous: no hint an unauthenticated visitor can retrieve, and no knowledge-based questions. The counter-argument is operational: hints and security questions reduce the volume of account recovery requests, and recovery is expensive to staff. I side with NIST, because recovery is where accounts are actually taken, and a hint that jogs your memory also helps anyone who has read your public profile. What would change my mind is data showing hints lowered a service’s rate of recovery-based compromise. I have not seen it.

Does forced rotation ever make sense?

Yes, and here I part company with the strongest form of the new advice. Rotation is a crude substitute for revocation. Where a credential is shared — a service account in a config file, an ops login three people use, a Wi-Fi pre-shared key — there is no one person whose access you can revoke, so changing the secret when somebody leaves is the only lever there is. The better answer is to remove the sharing: individual accounts, a secrets manager, short-lived credentials per job. But the better answer and the available answer differ, and telling a small team never to rotate a shared password applies a rule about human-chosen memorised secrets to something that is not one.

Is a password manager a single point of failure?

Yes. That is not a rebuttal, it is a description of the trade. You are exchanging a diffuse risk — one password across two hundred sites, one of which will eventually be breached — for a concentrated one, and exfiltrated vaults are a real category of incident. I take the trade because the concentrated risk is the one I can act on: the vault is encrypted under a key derived from a master password I choose, so that secret and the provider’s key derivation settings are most of the game. The security of two hundred other companies is not. What would change my mind is evidence of stolen vaults being decrypted at scale, rather than merely stolen.

What to change, and what to leave alone

If you own a login form: remove the composition rule, remove the scheduled expiry, allow paste, stop truncating, raise the maximum length, and check candidates against a breached-password list. The blocklist is the item that does real work; the rest is removal of harm.

If you are only looking after your own accounts: get a manager, make every password unique, make the master password long and genuinely random, and put the strongest available second factor on your email first. Email is the reset channel for everything else, which makes it the account that owns all the others. I have written separately about what a passkey replaces and what it does not, because “passwords are dead” is its own piece of advice that is not quite true yet.

And leave one rule where it is. Change a password immediately when there is evidence it was exposed. That is the one forced change every document quoted here still asks for, and the one people skip, because it does not arrive on a schedule. What got deleted was the arbitrary calendar. The rule about evidence got stronger.
