The real cost of a manual process

Almost every automation argument I have watched go badly went badly at the same moment: somebody said the manual way was costing too much time, somebody else said it was fine, and nobody had a number. This is the method I use to get one — three inputs for the visible cost, four costs that never make the estimate, an honest accounting of what the automation costs in return, and a payback line you can run on your own figures this afternoon.

The decision usually stops at a feeling

"This is eating our time" is a feeling. It is a real feeling, and it is usually pointing at something true, but it cannot be argued with and it cannot be compared. Two people can hold it about two different processes and there is no way to tell which one to fix first. Worse, the opposite feeling — "it only takes a minute" — is equally unfalsifiable, and it wins more arguments than it should, because a minute genuinely does sound like nothing.

The fix is not sophistication. It is arithmetic that a person can do on paper and that anybody in the room can check. What follows is deliberately simple: I would rather have a number that is roughly right and openly assumption-marked than a spreadsheet with fourteen tabs that nobody trusts and nobody can audit. Every figure in this article is arithmetic you can redo, and I have shown the working for exactly that reason.

One promise up front, because this post is written by somebody who builds automations for a living and you should read it with that in mind: the method below has told me to walk away from work more often than it has told me to take it. A costing exercise that can only ever say yes is not a costing exercise, it is a sales script.

The visible cost, and its three inputs

Start with the part everybody agrees on. Three inputs, one multiplication:

  • Minutes per run. How long one pass through the process takes, measured rather than remembered.
  • Runs per month. How often it happens. Take this from a system that counts, not from anyone's impression.
  • The loaded hourly rate of whoever does it, which is the input people get wrong, and the subject of the next section.

Multiply the first two, divide by sixty, multiply by the third. That is the visible monthly cost. Multiply by twelve for the year, because a monthly figure looks small and an annual figure looks like a decision.

Two notes on measuring, both of which cost me time to learn. First, do not ask people how long something takes — watch it. Self-reported durations are wrong in both directions at once: people quote the smooth run rather than the median, and they leave out the finding, the chasing and the checking on either side of the bit they think of as the task. Watch five or ten runs on ordinary days, and record the median and the worst case rather than the mean, because the mean of a process with one bad tail is a number that describes nothing that ever happened.

Second, count the runs from a system rather than from a person. Rows added to a sheet, emails in a sent folder, tickets closed, invoices raised. A count somebody can reproduce is worth more in the argument than a better count somebody has to take on trust.

What a loaded hour really costs

This is the single most common error, and it runs in one direction: people take the salary, divide by 2,080 hours, and use that. Both halves of that are wrong, and they compound.

The numerator is not the salary. The business spends more than the salary to keep one person able to do work: statutory contributions and insurance, software licences and seats, the laptop and the desk spread over their life, the cost of recruiting and training spread over the expected tenure, and a share of the time a manager spends supervising. Add those and you get a loaded cost that is typically somewhere between 1.25 and 1.4 times salary for a desk role. It is higher for a role with expensive tooling, and higher again for anyone client-facing whose time carries a travel budget.

The denominator is not 2,080. Fifty-two weeks at forty hours is what you pay for, not what you get. Take out public holidays, annual leave and a realistic allowance for sick days and the hours a person is actually present drop to somewhere near 1,800. You are paying for the absence too, so the cost of the hours you do get has to carry it.

Get both wrong and the undercount is severe. In the worked example below, salary over 2,080 gives ₹288 an hour; salary over 1,800 gives ₹333; the loaded cost over 1,800 gives ₹450. That is the same person, doing the same job, at prices that differ by more than half. Everything downstream inherits the error, which is how a process that plainly costs a quarter of a million rupees a year gets waved through as "a couple of minutes here and there".

If finance will not give you the overhead numbers, multiply salary by 1.3, divide by 1,800, and write "assumed 1.3× loading" next to the figure. An assumption you have labelled is arguable and improvable. An assumption you have hidden inside a rate is the thing that gets the whole model thrown out in the meeting, and it deserves to be.

The four costs that never make the estimate

The visible cost is real, and on the processes I have costed it is usually a little over half of the total. The rest sits in four places. None of them is exotic and none of them requires a consultant to find — they get left out because they land on a different line, in a different month, or on a different person's desk than the one doing the work.

1. Errors and the rework they cause

People doing repetitive transcription make mistakes at a rate that is stable and measurable, and nobody in the business knows what it is, because errors are noticed one at a time and never counted. Wrong email address, wrong owner assigned, a decimal in the wrong place, the same record entered twice.

The cost of an error is not the cost of redoing the step. It is much bigger, because of when it is found. An error caught at the moment of entry costs seconds. The same error found three days later, by somebody else, costs the finding, the working out of what should have happened, the chasing of whoever knows, the fix in two systems instead of one, and often an apology. A useful rule of thumb from my own measurements is that fixing one costs about three times what entering it did, and I would rather you measure yours than borrow mine.

To get the error rate, take a sample of a hundred recent records and check them against the source. It is a dull hour and it is the highest-value hour in this whole exercise, because the rate you find is nearly always higher than the rate anybody guessed, and it is the number the finance side of the room finds most persuasive.

There is a second cost here that I do not put in the arithmetic and do name in the write-up: the errors that reach a customer. A misaddressed invoice or an acknowledgement to the wrong person is not a labour cost, it is a credibility cost, and pretending to price it precisely would undermine the figures that are precise.

2. Context switching

A six-minute task that arrives twelve times a day at unpredictable moments is not seventy-two minutes of work. It is seventy-two minutes of work distributed as twelve interruptions, and each interruption costs a little time either side: putting down what you were doing, finding the tab, working out where you left off, and getting back into whatever you were actually doing afterwards.

I am careful with this number, because it is the one most easily inflated. There are widely quoted figures for how long it takes to recover from an interruption, they are much larger than anything I have watched in an office, and quoting them makes the whole model look like advocacy. So measure it: sit with the person for an hour and time the gap between "stops what they were doing" and "starts the task", and the gap on the way back. Two minutes per pickup is a figure I have seen hold up. If you cannot measure it, use two minutes and mark it as the softest number in the model, or halve it and lose the argument honestly.

The thing worth noticing is that this cost scales with frequency rather than with duration, which is why a two-minute task that happens forty times a day can cost more in switching than it does in work. That asymmetry is also the reason the first automation should usually be a frequent one, which is a section of its own further down.

3. Queue time, which is not a labour cost

Everything above is a cost of doing the work. This one is the cost of the work waiting, and it belongs in a different column because it is not paid in salary at all.

Work that requires a human waits for the human. It waits overnight, it waits through the weekend, it waits while they are in a meeting, and if it is done in batches it waits for the batch. An enquiry that arrives at nine in the evening and is answered at eleven the next morning spent fourteen hours in a queue, and none of those hours cost anybody a rupee in wages. They may still have cost the business the enquiry.

Here is where I stop and refuse to hand you a number. Queue time converts into money through a conversion rate, a response-time sensitivity and a margin, and the honest position is that most businesses have never measured any of the three. What I will give you is the shape:

  • Measure the wait, which you almost certainly can — the arrival timestamp and the handled timestamp are both sitting in a system. Report the median and the ninetieth percentile, because the tail is where the damage is.
  • Ask what changes if the wait goes away. For a sales enquiry it is a conversion rate. For an invoice it is days sales outstanding, which is a financing cost you can actually compute. For an internal approval it is somebody else's idle time, which loops back into the labour arithmetic above.
  • Put the money on it only if you can defend the middle number. If somebody at the table can say what a percentage point of conversion is worth and stand behind it, price it. If nobody can, report the wait in hours, say plainly that you have not priced it, and let it sit next to the figures that are priced.

I hold this line because queue time is where automation business cases go to become fiction. It is the easiest line to inflate — a single made-up conversion assumption can dwarf everything else in the model — and the moment a reader spots one invented number, every real number in the document loses its standing. An unpriced fact stated clearly survives scrutiny. A priced guess does not.

4. Key-person risk

Most manual processes have exactly one person who really knows them. Not by design — by accretion. They built it, they refined it, the exceptions live in their head, and the documentation, if it exists, describes a version from two years ago.

This gets discussed as a risk, which lets it stay hypothetical. It is not hypothetical, and it has a cost you can compute today, because the person is already away about twenty-eight days a year between leave and illness. On those days the work still arrives. Somebody covers it — usually someone more senior, always more slowly, generally with more errors — or it does not get done and lands as a spike the following week. Price the cover honestly and remember to subtract the owner's time it replaces, since you have already counted that in the visible cost. What is left is a real annual number, not a risk register entry.

The part that stays unpriced is the tail: the person leaves. Then you are paying for discovery — what does this actually do, what are the exceptions, who else touches it — during a handover window that is always shorter than you wanted. I do not put a number on that either. I put it in the write-up as a sentence, because a documented process is one of the two things you get from automating that has nothing to do with the software.

The under-rated by-product. To automate a process you must first write it down exactly, including every exception, which almost nobody has ever done. Half the value I have seen delivered arrives during that conversation, before a line of anything is built — because the act of writing it down usually reveals two steps that stopped being necessary years ago. This is also precisely why a process nobody has written down should not be automated yet, which the wrong-call section comes back to.

Opportunity cost does not stack

The fifth cost everybody wants to add is the one that gets the arithmetic wrong most often, so it gets its own heading rather than a place in the stack.

Opportunity cost is what the person is not doing while they do this. If a salesperson spends forty hours a month on data entry, those are hours not spent in front of customers. That is a genuine loss and it is frequently the largest single number in the whole exercise. It is also an alternative to the labour line, not an addition to it. You may count what the hour cost you, or what the hour would have earned you, but counting both charges the same hour twice, and a reader who spots that will be right to discard the rest of your model.

So: use whichever is larger, and say in one sentence which one you used. And attach the condition honestly — the opportunity figure only holds if the freed hour actually goes to the alternative. In practice, freed hours have a strong tendency to be absorbed by whatever else was being neglected, which is usually valuable but is not the thing you promised in the business case. If you are going to claim the selling hours, somebody has to commit to the selling.

A worked example, arithmetic shown

A small services business. Enquiries arrive through a web form and an inbox. One coordinator opens each one, creates the record in the CRM, tags it by service line, assigns an owner, and sends an acknowledgement. Nothing clever, nothing that anybody would call a project. Every number below is invented for the illustration and every one of them is a number you can replace with your own.

The loaded rate

  • Salary ₹6,00,000 a year.
  • Statutory contributions and insurance, ₹78,000. Software seats, ₹36,000. Laptop, desk and connectivity spread over their life, ₹42,000. Recruitment and onboarding spread over the expected tenure, ₹24,000. A share of the manager's time, ₹30,000.
  • Loaded annual cost: ₹8,10,000. That is 1.35 times salary.
  • Hours available: 2,080 paid, less 7 public holidays that fall on working days, 18 days of leave, 5 days of casual leave and 5 sick days — 280 hours — leaves 1,800.
  • Loaded hourly rate: ₹8,10,000 ÷ 1,800 = ₹450 an hour, or ₹7.50 a minute.

The visible cost

  • Measured at 6 minutes per enquiry, median of ten watched runs.
  • 12 enquiries a working day × 21 working days = 252 a month.
  • 6 × 252 = 1,512 minutes = 25.2 hours a month.
  • 25.2 × ₹450 = ₹11,340 a month, or ₹1,36,080 a year.

Note what the same line would have said at the salary-only rate: 25.2 × ₹333 = ₹8,392. Roughly ₹3,000 a month has already gone missing, and we have not left the part everyone agrees on.

The four that get left out

  • Errors and rework, ₹2,700. A sample of a hundred records found 8 with a defect that mattered. 8% of 252 is about 20 a month; each takes 18 minutes to notice, chase and fix. 20 × 18 = 360 minutes = 6 hours × ₹450.
  • Context switching, ₹3,780. 2 minutes of re-orientation per pickup, timed rather than assumed. 2 × 252 = 504 minutes = 8.4 hours × ₹450. The softest number in the model, and flagged as such.
  • Cover when the owner is away, ₹2,660. 28 days a year of leave and sickness. The work still arrives: 28 × 12 = 336 enquiries, handled by a manager at roughly double the time and a loaded ₹700 an hour. 336 × 12 minutes = 67.2 hours × ₹700 = ₹47,040. Subtract the owner's time it replaces — 336 × 6 minutes = 33.6 hours × ₹450 = ₹15,120 — because that is already in the visible cost. Net ₹31,920 a year, or ₹2,660 a month.
  • Queue time, not priced. Median wait from arrival to acknowledgement is 5 hours; the ninetieth percentile is 16 hours, because anything arriving after six in the evening waits for the morning. Reported as hours, deliberately left out of the total.

Total: ₹20,480 a month, ₹2,45,760 a year. The visible line is ₹11,340 of that, which is 55%. The number the business would have quoted is a little over half the number the business is paying.

The other side of the ledger

A cost is only half a decision. The other half is what the automation costs, and this is where business cases written by enthusiasts fall down, because building it is the cheap part and the estimate usually stops there.

Build

For our example: six hours agreeing the rules — which is mostly the exception hunt, and mostly worth it on its own — twenty hours building, eight testing including the cases nobody mentioned, four documenting and handing over. Thirty-eight hours. At a loaded engineering rate of ₹1,400 an hour that is ₹53,200.

Then add contingency, because the exception nobody mentioned always exists and it is never small. I budget fifty hours against a thirty-eight hour estimate — call it ₹70,000 — and I tell the client both numbers rather than quietly padding the rate. That is the same discipline as scoping the work before you quote it: the risk is priced in the open, and the boundary is written down before anybody starts.

Upkeep

A workflow is not a thing you finish. It decays as the systems under it change, and the running cost has three parts:

  • The tool. ₹2,400 a month here. Price the tier you will actually be on at real volume, not the free tier that was enough to prove the idea.
  • The exceptions. Automation handles the ordinary case. Assume something like 15% still needs a person — 38 items a month at 4 minutes to review is 2.5 hours × ₹450 = ₹1,140. An automation that claims a hundred per cent has either an unusually clean process or an unusually optimistic author.
  • Maintenance. Four hours a quarter for connector changes, rule tweaks and the occasional broken run: 1.33 hours a month × ₹1,400 = ₹1,870.

Running total: ₹5,410 a month.

Vendor risk

The maintenance line is not padding. Something in the chain will change within a year — an endpoint deprecated, a field renamed, an OAuth scope split in two — and that is a Tuesday rather than a hypothesis. What differs between platforms is who picks the date: on a hosted tool the vendor's connector update lands in your running workflow without a version you chose, while a self-hosted instance pinned to an image tag changes nothing until you pull it, which also means the fix waits for you. I went through that trade-off properly in n8n, Make, or Zapier: an honest comparison, and the costing consequence is simple: if your maintenance line is zero, you have not finished the model.

Two more lines that belong in the ledger and rarely appear. The first is the cost of the automation failing quietly — none of these tools will page anyone unless you build the paging, so budget the hours for a failure alert into a channel a current employee reads. The second is the switching cost: there is no migration path between these platforms, so choosing one is a two-year commitment whether or not you price it as one.

The payback period, and what it is not

With both sides of the ledger, the decision is one line of arithmetic:

Payback in months = build cost ÷ (monthly cost of doing it by hand − monthly cost of running the automation).

For the example: ₹70,000 ÷ (₹20,480 − ₹5,410) = ₹70,000 ÷ ₹15,070 = 4.6 months. In the first year the manual route costs ₹2,45,760 and the automated route costs ₹70,000 plus twelve months of ₹5,410, which is ₹1,34,920. The difference is ₹1,10,840, and it widens every year after that because the build is not paid again.

Four things that number is not, and each of them has cost somebody credibility:

  • It is not profit. Past month five the saving is capacity, not cash. Unless a headcount actually changes or the freed hours are filled with something that earns, nothing shows up in the accounts, and if you promised that it would, the next project starts with a deficit of trust. Say what the saving is made of before anybody asks.
  • It assumes the volume holds. Run the numbers again at the volume you believe in twelve months, and check the two places a straight line breaks: where the manual process would have needed a second person, and where the tool's pricing tier changes.
  • It ignores the cost of deciding. The measuring, the sampling and the writing-down are real hours. On a small process the analysis can be a meaningful fraction of the build, which is an argument for keeping the analysis to an afternoon.
  • It says nothing about risk. A four-month payback on a process that is about to be replaced by a new system is a worse deal than a ten-month payback on one that has run unchanged for five years. Payback is a number, not a judgement.

As rules of thumb: under twelve months is usually a straightforward yes; twelve to twenty-four is a yes only where the process is stable and somebody has agreed to own the upkeep; past twenty-four the answer is no, or your scope is too big and you should automate the busiest third instead.

When automating is the wrong call

This is the section that keeps the rest of the article honest. Four cases, and I have argued all four out loud with people who were paying me to say the opposite.

The volume is too low. The arithmetic simply says no, and the tell is a payback past two years. Something that happens four times a month at fifteen minutes each costs 1 hour, which is ₹450, which is ₹5,400 a year against a build that will not come in under ₹40,000. It does not matter how annoying it is. Annoyance and cost are different quantities, and a process can be genuinely irritating and genuinely cheap at the same time. Fix the irritation with a checklist and a template.

The process is about to change. A system migration, a new regulation, a reorganisation, a product change that alters the inputs. You would be encoding the shape of something that is being replaced, and the automation will need rebuilding before it has paid back. When somebody tells me a CRM migration is planned for next quarter, the right answer is to wait and use the interval to write the process down properly — which makes the eventual build faster and is free.

Nobody has actually written it down. If two people do it differently and both are confident they are doing it right, you do not have a process, you have a habit with two dialects. Automating that does not standardise it — it silently picks one person's version and makes it policy without anybody deciding to. Write it down first, get the disagreements out where they can be settled, and count the steps that turn out to be unnecessary. Frequently the write-down alone removes enough work that the automation stops being worth building, which is a good outcome that no vendor will ever offer you.

The manual step is a deliberate human check. Sometimes the slowness is the control. An approval, a second pair of eyes on a payment, a judgement call about a customer's situation. The friction is doing a job, and removing it removes the job. The tell is what the step outputs: if the answer is always yes and nobody remembers the last time it was no, it may be theatre and worth examining. If the answer is sometimes no and the no matters, keep the human — and automate everything that leads up to them, so the check is the only thing left for a person to do. That is the shape I argued for in AI driven, human assisted, and it applies well beyond anything involving a model.

There is a fifth case I hold more loosely: where a wrong result is much worse than a slow one and the wrongness would be silent. Automating those is not forbidden, but the cost of the checking you have to build around them belongs in the ledger, and it is usually larger than the build itself.

Why the first one should be the boring one

Once a team has run this exercise on two or three processes, there is a strong pull toward the interesting candidate — the one with judgement in it, the one that would be impressive. Resist it. The first automation should be the dullest high-frequency thing you can find, for five reasons.

  1. The arithmetic favours it. Value is frequency multiplied by savings, and the interesting process is nearly always the rare one. Boring and daily beats fascinating and monthly by an order of magnitude, and the switching cost only exists for the frequent one.
  2. It is already written down, in effect. It is boring precisely because it never varies, which means the rules are knowable and the exception hunt is short. The interesting process is interesting because it needs judgement, which is exactly the thing that is expensive to encode and awkward to get wrong.
  3. It fails cheaply and visibly. A high-frequency automation that breaks tells you within the day, and the blast radius of one bad run is one record. A monthly job that breaks tells you in five weeks, by which time you have forgotten how it works.
  4. The second build is cheaper because of the first. The first one pays for the tool, the credentials, the error channel, the logging and the team's understanding of how any of it works. All of that is fixed cost the second project inherits free, which is why the payback on automation number two is routinely half that of number one.
  5. Trust is the real constraint. Automation projects rarely die of technical failure. They die when a visible one goes wrong early, and the organisation quietly decides this is not for us. A dull one that has worked every day for three months is what buys you permission to attempt the interesting one.

This is the same argument as the one about capacity, from a different direction. A business runs on people, money, know-how and management, and automation is a way of moving work between those four — the framing I set out in the four M's every business runs on. Spending your first attempt on the impressive process spends the management attention, which is the scarcest of the four, on the case most likely to disappoint.

Running this on your own numbers

An afternoon, in order:

  1. Pick one process and write down what it starts with and what it ends with. If you cannot state the boundary in one sentence, you have picked two processes.
  2. Count the runs from a system, for one real month.
  3. Time five to ten runs with a stopwatch, on ordinary days. Record the median and the worst.
  4. Work out the loaded rate for whoever does it. Salary plus overheads, divided by the hours they are actually there. Write the loading factor next to it.
  5. Sample a hundred records against the source and count the defects that mattered. Multiply by the time it takes to fix one when it is found late.
  6. Add switching at two minutes a pickup, marked as an assumption, or measured if you can.
  7. Price the cover for the days the owner is away, less the time it replaces.
  8. Report the queue in hours, median and ninetieth percentile, and do not convert it to money unless somebody can defend the conversion.
  9. Estimate the build, add contingency of about a third, and add the tool, the exceptions and the maintenance as a monthly figure.
  10. Divide. Build ÷ monthly saving = payback in months. Then read the four caveats above before you present it.

Put it on one page, with the arithmetic visible and every assumption named. The reason for one page is not brevity for its own sake: a costing that fits on a page can be challenged line by line in a meeting, and a costing that can be challenged is one that survives being challenged. Attach a date to look again, because the answer is a function of the volume and the volume moves.

The point of this exercise is not to justify automating. It is to make the decision arguable. Half the value shows up the moment the process is written down, and a good share of the answers are "leave it alone, and look again in six months" — which is a result worth an afternoon, and a great deal cheaper than finding out after the build.

Questions people ask

What is a loaded hourly rate, and how do I work out mine?

Take the annual salary, add everything the business spends to keep that person able to work — statutory contributions, insurance, software licences, the desk and the laptop spread over their life, recruitment and training spread over the expected tenure, and a share of the manager's time — then divide by the hours that person is actually available in a year, which is not 2,080. In the example in this article that is ₹8,10,000 divided by 1,800 hours, or ₹450 an hour, against a salary-only figure of ₹333. If you cannot get the real overhead numbers out of finance, multiply salary by 1.3 and label it an assumption rather than a fact.

Our people are salaried, so the time is already paid for. Is it really a cost?

It is a real cost and it is not a cash saving, and confusing those two is how automation business cases lose their credibility. The hour is bought whether or not it is spent well, so removing the work does not reduce the payroll — it converts spent hours into available hours. That is worth something only if the available hours get used for something that earns, or for something that was not getting done at all. Say that plainly in the business case. A claim of cash savings that never shows up in the accounts costs you the argument for the next three projects.

How do I measure how long the process actually takes?

Not by asking. Self-reported durations are wrong in both directions: people quote the good run rather than the median, and they leave out the finding, the chasing and the checking either side of the part they think of as the task. Watch it end to end five or ten times with a timer, on ordinary days rather than a quiet Friday, and record the median and the worst case rather than the average. If you cannot watch it, take the count from whatever system records the work — rows added, emails sent, tickets closed — and divide the observed elapsed time by it.

What if the volume is going to grow?

Run the arithmetic twice, at today's volume and at the volume you actually believe in twelve months, and present both. Growth shortens the payback, because the manual cost scales with volume and the running cost of an automation mostly does not. The discipline is to use a growth number somebody is willing to be held to rather than the one on the plan, and to check the two thresholds that break a straight line: the point where the manual process needs a second person, and the point where the tool's pricing tier changes. Both are step changes, and both land outside the model.

Should I count opportunity cost as well as the salary cost?

No — opportunity cost replaces the labour line, it does not stack on top of it. If a salesperson spends an hour on data entry you can count the ₹450 the hour cost you, or the margin that hour would have produced in front of a customer, but counting both charges the same hour twice. Use whichever is larger, state which one you used, and be honest that the opportunity figure only holds if the freed hour genuinely goes to the alternative. Most freed hours do not, at least not in the first month.

What payback period is short enough?

Under twelve months is usually an easy yes, because neither the volume nor the process is likely to change beyond recognition inside a year. Twelve to twenty-four months is a yes only where the process is genuinely stable and somebody has agreed to maintain the thing. Beyond twenty-four months the answer is no, or the scope is wrong and you should automate the busiest third of the process instead of all of it. Those are rules of thumb rather than finance, and if your business already has a required rate of return, use that instead of mine.

What if the honest answer is that we should not automate it?

Then you have bought a good outcome for the price of an afternoon, and you should write the decision down with the numbers attached and a date to look again. The write-up is the valuable part: it stops the same conversation being had from scratch every quarter, and it names exactly which number would have to change for the answer to flip, which is usually the volume. I have talked people out of automations more often than into them, and the ones that go ahead afterwards tend to survive, because the reason for them is written down rather than felt.

None of this is difficult, and that is rather the point. The reason manual processes persist is not that anybody has weighed them and decided they are worth keeping. It is that nobody has weighed them at all, so the decision defaults to whichever feeling is loudest in the room. An afternoon with a stopwatch, a sample of a hundred records and one honest hourly rate is enough to replace the feeling with a figure — and once there is a figure, the argument is short.
