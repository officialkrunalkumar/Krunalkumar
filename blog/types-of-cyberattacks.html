Types of cyberattacks, mapped: causes, defenses, and the CIA triad

Every attack you have ever read about — phishing, ransomware, DDoS, SQL injection — breaks one of just three promises a system makes. This is the map I wish someone had drawn for me: six attack families, why each one keeps working, and where prevention ends and mitigation begins.

The three promises every attack breaks

Security has a reputation for being endless — new attack names every week, each sounding like its own specialty. But underneath the naming noise, every attack is trying to break one of exactly three promises. Security professionals call them the CIA triad:

  • Confidentiality — only the right people can see the data. Broken when secrets leak: stolen customer records, read email, exposed credentials.
  • Integrity — the data and the system are what they claim to be. Broken when something is altered: a tampered invoice, a poisoned software update, a defaced site.
  • Availability — the system works when legitimate users need it. Broken when service stops: a flooded server, an encrypted disk, a frozen machine.

This lens is the whole trick. Ask "which promise does this attack break?" and an unfamiliar attack stops being scary vocabulary and becomes a variation of something you already understand. Ransomware is primarily an availability attack that uses malware. Phishing is usually a confidentiality attack that goes through a person instead of a computer. My own research subject — the humble fork bomb — is a pure availability attack made of nothing but permitted actions.

Attackers don't respect category boundaries, and real incidents chain several types together. The categories aren't for the attacker's benefit — they're for yours, because each family falls to the same kinds of defense.

The map: six attack families

Here is the landscape in one picture. Six families, grouped by how the attack works — through people, code, networks, load, applications, or logins — with the promise each one usually breaks.

Social engineering: attacks on people

The most reliable way into a system has never been code — it's a person with a plausible story. Phishing casts a wide net with fake emails and login pages; spear phishing aims at one person with details pulled from their real life; pretexting builds a scenario ("IT here, we need your password to fix your mailbox"); vishing and smishing do the same over calls and texts.

Why it works: it exploits helpfulness, urgency, and authority — instincts that make people good colleagues make them good targets. No software flaw is required, which is why patching alone has never stopped it.

Prevention is about removing single points of human failure: multi-factor authentication (a stolen password alone gets nowhere), password managers (which refuse to autofill a look-alike domain), and training that treats mistakes as reports to encourage, not failures to hide. Prefer phishing-resistant MFA — passkeys or hardware security keys — where you can: one-time codes and push prompts can be relayed in real time by modern phishing proxies, while origin-bound credentials cannot. Mitigation is about assuming someone will eventually click: alert on unusual logins, limit what any single account can reach, and make "I think I clicked something" a blameless five-minute report instead of a shameful secret — the sooner you know, the smaller the damage.

Malware: attacks that run code

Malware is any software built to do harm from inside: ransomware encrypts your data and sells you back the key; worms spread machine-to-machine on their own; trojans hide inside software you chose to install; spyware watches quietly; botnets enroll your machines into someone else's army; rootkits bury themselves below where you'd think to look.

Why it works: malware needs a door — an unpatched vulnerability, a user tricked into running it, or a legitimate software update poisoned upstream. It is rarely the first step of an incident; it's what arrives after a phish or an exploit succeeds, which is why it breaks all three CIA promises depending on the payload.

Prevention: patch quickly (most malware exploits vulnerabilities that had fixes available for months), restrict what users can install, and let modern endpoint protection watch behavior rather than just signatures. Mitigation is where ransomware is actually beaten: offline, tested backups — tested meaning you have rehearsed a restore, because backup systems fail silently and attackers deliberately hunt for online backups first. One honest caveat: backups beat the encryption half. Modern ransomware usually steals a copy of the data before encrypting it and threatens publication — double extortion — and only keeping the intruder out beats that half. Segmented networks turn "everything is encrypted" into "one department lost an afternoon."

Network attacks: attacks in transit

Data is at its most exposed while moving. A man-in-the-middle attack quietly relays traffic between you and a real service, reading or rewriting it as it passes. Spoofing forges an identity — an IP address, an ARP reply, a Wi-Fi network with a trustworthy name. Sniffing passively records whatever crosses the wire. DNS poisoning corrupts the address book itself, so the right name delivers you to the wrong server.

Why it works: the internet's core protocols were designed for a network of trusted peers, and identity was bolted on later. Anywhere trust is assumed instead of proven — open Wi-Fi, plain HTTP, unauthenticated DNS — an impostor can stand in the middle.

Prevention: encrypt in transit, everywhere — TLS/HTTPS with HSTS so browsers refuse to downgrade, VPNs or encrypted tunnels on untrusted networks, DNSSEC where you control zones. (This site sends Strict-Transport-Security on every response for exactly this reason.) Mitigation: treat certificate warnings as incidents rather than annoyances, and monitor for the tell-tales — duplicate ARP replies, unexpected certificate changes, DNS answers that differ by resolver.

Denial of service: attacks on availability

This family doesn't steal or corrupt anything — it makes systems unusable, and it's the one I know most personally. A DDoS floods a service with traffic from thousands of machines. Amplification attacks bounce small forged requests off public servers that reply with large answers, multiplying the flood. Slowloris starves a web server by holding connections open with agonizing slowness. And resource exhaustion attacks like the fork bomb need no network at all — one line of Bash, made entirely of permitted actions, freezes a machine from the inside.

Why it works: every system has finite capacity — bandwidth, connections, memory, process slots — and usually no distinction between heavy legitimate demand and hostile demand. The attack is just "more" until the limit breaks.

Prevention: rate limits, quotas, and for anything public-facing, a CDN or DDoS protection service that can absorb floods bigger than any single origin could. Mitigation is where the interesting trade-off lives: crude limits punish legitimate users too — the exact problem my fork bomb research tackled. Its answer: detect an offender within tens of milliseconds, then quarantine repeat suspects instead of permanently banning them — so a falsely accused process can prove itself and go free. The principle generalizes to every rate limiter you will ever design: protect the system without becoming your own denial of service.

Web and application attacks

These attacks exploit flaws in software you wrote or depend on. SQL injection smuggles database commands through input fields — decades old and still breaching companies today. Cross-site scripting (XSS) plants attacker JavaScript in pages other users trust. CSRF rides a logged-in user's session to perform actions they never intended. Zero-days are flaws the vendor doesn't know about yet, so no patch exists. And supply chain attacks poison a dependency or update upstream, turning your own trust in your tools against you.

Why it works: software trusts input more than it should. Nearly every attack in this family is some form of data being treated as instructions — user input becoming SQL, becoming script, becoming commands.

Prevention: parameterized queries (which make injection structurally impossible, not just unlikely), output encoding and a strict Content-Security-Policy for XSS, anti-CSRF tokens and SameSite cookies, dependency scanning and lockfiles for the supply chain. Frameworks do most of this correctly by default — the vulnerabilities usually arrive when someone works around the framework. Mitigation: least-privilege database accounts (an injected query that can't DROP anything is an incident, not a catastrophe), a web application firewall to buy patching time, and logs detailed enough to answer "what did they actually reach?"

Credential and access attacks

Sometimes nobody breaks in — they log in. Brute force tries passwords until one works. Credential stuffing replays username-password pairs leaked from one site against every other site, betting on reuse — and the bet pays constantly. Password spraying inverts brute force: one common password tried across thousands of accounts, slow enough to dodge lockouts. And insider misuse needs no attack at all — just access that outlived its purpose.

Why it works: passwords get reused, access accumulates, and login endpoints are reachable from anywhere by design. One leaked database on the other side of the world becomes your breach because someone used the same password twice.

Prevention: multi-factor authentication — the single highest-value control in this entire article; it defeats all three password attacks at once. Add password managers (which make unique passwords effortless), breached-password checks at signup, and rate limiting on login endpoints. Mitigation: review access on a schedule and when roles change, alert on impossible logins (new country, new device, odd hours), and make revoking access part of every offboarding — the account nobody remembers is the account nobody watches.

Why attacks actually succeed

Six families, but if you read the "why it works" lines again, the same handful of root causes repeats. Incidents almost never require a genius adversary — they require one of these being true:

  • Unpatched software — the fix existed; it just wasn't applied.
  • Weak or reused credentials — one leak anywhere becomes access everywhere.
  • Misconfiguration — the storage bucket was public, the default password stayed, the port was open.
  • Misplaced trust — in a sender, a network, a dependency, or an input.
  • Excessive access — accounts and services that could do far more than their job required.
  • Nobody watching — the breach lasted months because no one was looking at the logs.

This list is quietly optimistic. Every cause on it is boring, known, and fixable — which means most security wins come from discipline, not brilliance.

Prevention vs. mitigation: before and after

The distinction I've used throughout deserves to be explicit, because budgets routinely get it wrong. Prevention reduces the chance an attack succeeds. Mitigation reduces the damage when one succeeds anyway — and some eventually will. Mature security spends deliberately on both; that's what "defense in depth" actually means.

The highest-value prevention and mitigation for each family — starting points, not complete programs.
Family Breaks Prevention (before) Mitigation (after)
Social engineering C Phishing-resistant MFA, password managers, blameless training Login alerts, least privilege, fast blameless reporting
Malware C · I · A Prompt patching, install restrictions, endpoint protection Offline tested backups, network segmentation
Network C · I TLS everywhere + HSTS, VPN on untrusted networks Certificate monitoring, treating TLS warnings as incidents
Denial of service A Rate limits, quotas, CDN / DDoS absorption Graceful degradation, quarantine over permanent bans
Web & application C · I Parameterized queries, CSP, framework defaults, dependency scanning Least-privilege DB accounts, WAF, forensic-quality logs
Credential & access C MFA, unique passwords, login rate limits Access reviews, anomaly alerts, disciplined offboarding

How to use this map

Three takeaways worth keeping after you close this tab. First, name the promise: when you meet a new attack, ask whether it steals (confidentiality), alters (integrity), or stops (availability) — you'll understand it faster than any glossary can teach you. Second, fix the causes, not the headlines: patching, MFA, least privilege, backups, and monitoring quietly defend against most of every family at once. Third, budget for "when," not just "if" — the organizations that recover well are the ones that rehearsed recovery before they needed it.
