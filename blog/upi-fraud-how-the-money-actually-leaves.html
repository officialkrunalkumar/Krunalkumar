UPI fraud: how the money actually leaves

A request for money and an offer of money look almost the same on a phone screen, and one of them takes while the other gives. Nearly every UPI fraud in India lives inside that resemblance. This is the mechanism, written to be forwarded to a parent, and the one sentence in it is worth more than the rest of the article put together.

The one sentence worth forwarding

If you send one line from this page to somebody, send this one:

The UPI PIN authorises money leaving your account. Always. No refund, no cashback, no prize, no salary arrear, no insurance payout and no returned wrong transfer has ever required you to enter it.

That is the whole defence, and it is short enough to say down a phone line. Everything else in this article is the reasoning behind it, the shapes the lie arrives in, and what to do when it has already worked.

I have volunteered as a registered cyber-expert supporting law enforcement since 2020, and the cases that reach me are almost never technical. They converge on a single moment: somebody who genuinely believed money was about to arrive typed six digits that sent it instead. Nothing was broken into. No password was guessed. The person authorised their own debit, because the screen in front of them was describing a different transaction from the one it was about to perform.

Two screens that are not the same

Open any UPI app and look at what a collect request actually renders as. There is a name at the top, a UPI ID under it, a note, an amount, and a bar across the bottom. Now look at a payment you are about to make. Name, ID, note, amount, bar across the bottom. They are the same card.

The difference is which text on that card the other party controls, and it is nearly all of it. The display name is whatever their account is registered as, which can read CUSTOMER CARE — REFUND DESK. The note is free text they typed, which can read approve to receive your refund. The amount is chosen by them. None of it is verified as a description of what the screen will do, because none of it is a field the payment system inspects — it is a label being carried along.

There is exactly one line on the screen the other party cannot write, and it is the bar at the bottom. Your app writes that line, and it says pay. It says pay on the refund screen, it says pay on the winnings screen, and it says pay on the screen where the note claims the money has already been credited and is merely awaiting your approval. That contradiction — credited and awaiting your approval — is the most common frame in Indian payment fraud, and it cannot be true. A real credit is never pending anything, because it has already happened.

So the reliable question is not "is this genuine". It is: which phone is being asked for a PIN? That phone is the one the money leaves.

What the PIN actually signs

UPI is run by the National Payments Corporation of India, and the PIN is not a password for the app. You set it against a specific bank account on a specific device, usually by entering debit card details that only your bank can check. What it produces, every time you type it, is an authorisation for a debit from that account — generated on your device, verified by your bank. That is its only function. There is no other thing it does.

Which means receiving is passive, structurally. There is no step in the protocol where a payee approves an incoming credit, because nothing about a credit needs the payee's consent. Money arrives and you are informed. A genuine credit is news, not a decision.

Once that is clear, a long list of sentences becomes impossible rather than merely suspicious:

  • "Enter your PIN to receive the refund." There is no receive screen with a PIN on it.
  • "Approve this request and the cashback will credit." Approving a request debits you.
  • "Just verify with one rupee and we will release the amount." A one-rupee verification is a one-rupee debit, and its real purpose is to teach you the motion so the larger one feels familiar.
  • "Scan this code to receive the payment." A QR code is an address to pay to. Scanning one has never pulled money in.
  • "Enter your PIN to confirm you are the account holder." Your bank already knows. It issued the PIN.

The uncomfortable part is that nothing here is a flaw. A UPI debit requires the payer's PIN, entered by the payer, inside the payer's own app on the payer's own device. That is a sound design, and it is precisely because the technical route is closed that the fraud has to go through the person. This is the social engineering family from the cyberattacks map, and it is the one family that patching has never touched.

Why the collect request is the whole problem

UPI lets anybody who knows your address raise a request against your account. The amount is theirs to choose and the reason line is theirs to type. It arrives on your phone as a prompt, and it dies there unless you approve it. Nothing about that is broken: it is how a shop bills you at a counter, and how a friend asks for their share of a dinner.

But look at what it gives an attacker. A push notification they trigger, on a stranger's phone, in the middle of a phone call they are also running, carrying a headline and a body of text they wrote, landing in an app the victim trusts. They do not need to build a fake page or register a lookalike domain. The payment app renders the lie for them, in its own typeface, with its own logo above it.

Person-to-person collect has been tightened repeatedly for exactly this reason — the limits on it are far lower than on an ordinary payment, and several apps have moved it behind an extra step or withdrawn it for individuals. What is available differs by app and keeps changing, so check the settings in the app in front of you rather than trusting any number printed in an article, including this one.

The mandate is the same trap with a longer tail. An autopay mandate is one PIN entry that authorises a series of debits. The screen shows a small amount today and a recurring amount underneath it, and after you approve it, nothing asks you again until you cancel. Read the then line before the today line, and check the mandate list on a parent's phone the same way you would check a bank statement.

Reading about this is weaker than deciding it. There is a lab on this site that puts seven stylised payment screens in front of you, one at a time, and asks a single question: does this take money or give it? The tell is named afterwards on each one. Every screen is drawn as a labelled diagram rather than a convincing mock, there are no real handles or bank names anywhere on it, and it makes no network request of any kind.

The scripts that actually reach people

Six recurring shapes. For each one: the hook that gets attention, the pressure that removes the pause in which you would have checked, and the exact moment the money moves. The fourth line — the question that ends it — is the only part that is any use while it is happening, so it is the part to carry away.

1. The fake refund

The hook. It opens with them giving rather than asking. An order that failed, a train ticket cancelled, a double-charged electricity bill, a deposit the landlord is releasing. Being owed money switches off the part of you that checks, because on the face of it nothing is being requested.

The pressure. A closing window, delivered politely. The refund reference expires at six. The gateway batch closes tonight. They stay courteous throughout, because courtesy is what stops you hanging up and calling the company on a number you already have.

Where the money moves. They raise a collect request, send a QR code, or walk you into a payment screen, and call it the refund. What you approve is a debit. Often the first one is small and framed as verifying the account — that one exists to rehearse the motion, not to take the money.

The question that ends it. Which of us is about to type a PIN? If it is you, this is not a refund. A refund needs nothing from you at all.

2. Sorry, wrong transfer — please send it back

The hook. A small amount lands in your account, often genuinely, followed by a distressed message. Sent to the wrong number. Please return it. Sometimes a second voice calls, presenting as a bank officer, to confirm the story.

The pressure. Decency and embarrassment, which are stronger levers than fear. You are being asked to be a good person about somebody else's mistake, and refusing feels like keeping what is not yours.

Where the money moves. The return goes out through their link, their QR or their request, to an account that is not the one that credited you. Two common endings: the amount you send back is quietly larger than what arrived, or the original credit is reversed later as a disputed transaction while your return has already left. There is a third and worse one — if the money that reached you was itself stolen, passing it on by hand has made your account a link in the chain, and it is your account that gets frozen.

The question that ends it. Why is a bank not doing this? A genuine misdirected transfer is reversed by the sender's bank, on the sender's written request, with a reference number. It is never returned by a stranger with a link.

3. The QR code sent "to receive"

The hook. You have listed a sofa, a phone, a bike. A buyer appears quickly, agrees the price without haggling, and wants to settle immediately — which is unusual enough on its own to be worth noticing.

The pressure. They are travelling, their driver is downstairs, they are being posted out tomorrow. The urgency is structural rather than emotional: it explains why they cannot meet, why they will not inspect the item, and why this has to finish in ten minutes.

Where the money moves. They send a QR code "to receive the payment with" and ask you to scan it and enter your PIN. A UPI QR code carries a payee and sometimes an amount, and scanning it can only start a payment out of your account.

The question that ends it. Show me the receive screen that asks for a PIN. There is not one, in any app.

A QR code is not a mystery, and it is worth seeing that. The QR lab on this site decodes a code in your browser and shows you the plain text inside it — the payee, the amount, the link — before anything acts on it. Once you have watched a code turn into an address, the sentence "scan this to receive money" stops being plausible.

4. The seller with posting orders

The hook. A car, a bike, furniture or a flat, well under the going rate, listed by somebody who says they are armed forces or a government officer being posted out at short notice and must sell this week. A photograph in uniform and a picture of an identity card do most of the work.

The pressure. Rank and hurry. The story is built so that checking feels like an insult, and a unit transport or a depot will deliver so you never have to see the item or the person. Somebody senior may call to vouch for them.

Where the money moves. Never in one payment. A token to hold it, then transport, then insurance, then a refundable security deposit, then a clearance charge. Each is small against what you have already put in, which is the design — by the third one you are not deciding to pay, you are protecting what you paid.

The question that ends it. Am I paying for something I have not seen, to somebody I have not met? No canteen, depot or unit ships goods against an advance transfer to a personal account, and no posting order has ever required one.

5. The helpline you found yourself

The hook. Something went wrong with a delivery, a booking, a payment app, an account. You searched for the customer care number and rang what came up. Nobody called you — which is precisely why it works, because every warning you have ever heard is about incoming calls.

The pressure. None, at first. The number sat on a page that looked official, in an advertisement above the results, or in a business listing anybody can suggest an edit to. The person answers in the company's name, has a script, and is unhurried. Patience is the tell you will not notice.

Where the money moves. A verification payment of one or ten rupees to confirm the account is yours, or a push towards a screen-sharing app to process the refund. Either way you approve something. The small amount is not the point; the approval is, and so is what they watch you type next.

The question that ends it. Where did this number actually come from? Support numbers live inside the app you already have installed, on the back of your card, or on your statement. A search result is not a source. If you want to see how much a plausible-looking link can hide, pull one apart in the URL inspector.

6. The screen share

The hook. Something has to be fixed and they need to look. A stuck refund, a KYC that will not go through, an app that keeps failing. They send a link to a genuine remote-support application from the real app store, which makes the request feel routine rather than strange.

The pressure. Helpfulness, plus a little technical embarrassment. You do not want to be the person who cannot follow simple instructions, and the app installs without a single prompt that reads like a warning.

Where the money moves. With your screen visible they read the one-time password out of your notification shade, your balance, and your PIN as your thumb lands on it. With remote control they operate the banking app themselves while you watch. The debit does not feel like a debit, because you did not perform it.

The question that ends it. Would my bank ask to watch me type my PIN? No.

Why the screen share is the worst step

Of everything above, one request deserves to be treated as an absolute rather than as a judgment call, and it is the request to install a screen-sharing or remote-access app.

Every other script still needs you to make a decision. The screen share removes the decision. With view-only access, the attacker sees the one-time password the moment it appears in your notification shade, so the second factor stops being a second factor; they see your balance, so the amount they ask for is the amount you have; and they see the PIN pad as your thumb travels across it. With remote control, they simply operate the banking app themselves. Every habit this article is trying to give you — read the confirm bar, ask which phone is being asked for a PIN, pause before approving — assumes you are the person driving the phone.

Two details make it worse than it sounds. The app is genuine: it comes from the real store, it is signed, and no scanner will flag it, because there is nothing wrong with it. And on Android the permission it asks for is usually an accessibility service or a screen-capture grant, which does not end when the call does. Somebody who hung up an hour ago can still be watching, and often the victim has no idea the session is live.

The rule, stated without exceptions. No bank, no payment app, no telecom operator, no delivery company and no government office ever needs to see your screen. Not to verify, not to help, not once, not for a moment. If a support call moves towards "install this so I can see", the call is over — and it does not matter who called whom. Workplace IT support does legitimately use these tools, which is why the rule is scoped to money and to strangers: nobody handling your bank account is ever on the other end of one.

The first hour, in order

If it has already happened, speed is the only lever you have, and it is a real one. If there are two of you in the room, work the first four in parallel rather than in sequence.

  1. Stop paying. There is no unlock fee, no clearance charge and no refundable deposit that ends this. If somebody is still on the line, hang up without explaining that you have worked it out.
  2. Cut the access before anything else if a screen was shared. End the session, uninstall the app, and revoke the accessibility or screen-capture permission it was granted. Until that is done, everything else you do is being watched.
  3. Call 1930. The national cybercrime helpline, staffed around the clock under the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre at the Ministry of Home Affairs. Behind it sits the reporting system that pushes your complaint out to the banks and payment intermediaries in the transaction chain, so funds still in transit between accounts can be held.
  4. File at cybercrime.gov.in. The National Cybercrime Reporting Portal takes every category and gives you an acknowledgement number. Keep it. Every later conversation with a bank, an officer or a court starts from that number.
  5. Call your bank's own fraud line, separately. The 1930 route reaches the bank, but the dispute still has to be raised on your account. Do it by phone and in writing, and get an acknowledgement with a timestamp.
  6. Close what they can still reach. Change the UPI PIN and the app passcode, cancel any autopay mandate that appeared, and ask the bank to block the UPI facility or the account if credentials were shared.
  7. Write everything down while it is fresh. Numbers, names, times, amounts, the reference of every debit, the beneficiary UPI ID or account number if you can see it, and screenshots of the conversation before anyone deletes it.
  8. Go to the police as well. Your local cyber cell, or any police station. Under the Zero FIR principle no station can refuse you for being outside its jurisdiction — the FIR is registered and transferred. What happens to the complaint after that is described in how cybercrime cases are solved.
  9. Look after the person, not only the money. Shame is what keeps people quiet for three days while the money moves. Tele-MANAS, the national mental health helpline, is on 14416.

Why the hour matters. Stolen money is layered onward within minutes — split across accounts, withdrawn in cash, converted. A complaint filed now can put a hold on whatever has not moved yet; a complaint filed tomorrow reaches accounts that are already empty.

And an honest word about liability, because it is where people are most often misled. UPI has no chargeback in the card sense. What exists is a dispute raised in the app and with your bank, escalated to the Reserve Bank's ombudsman scheme if the bank's answer is unsatisfactory or does not arrive within the time the scheme allows. The limited-liability protection people have heard about turns on a transaction being unauthorised — and a debit you approved with your own PIN is, on its face, authorised. That is the argument you will be met with. Raise the dispute anyway, because the facts differ case by case and a remote-control debit somebody else performed is a genuinely different argument from a collect request you approved yourself. But do not go in expecting a rule to rescue you automatically, and do not let anyone tell you it will. I am not a lawyer, and none of this is advice on your particular case; the statutory background is in India's IT law, explained.

What actually improves the odds, in order: minutes rather than hours; the exact reference number of every debit; the beneficiary details if you have them; both channels used, 1930 and the portal, not one; and everything repeated in writing so it exists somewhere other than in a call log.

What recovery honestly looks like

I am not going to tell you the money comes back, because often it does not, and every page that implies otherwise makes the honest version harder to accept when it arrives.

Here is the mechanism. Your complaint triggers holds down the chain of accounts the money passed through, and only the portion still sitting somewhere in that chain can be held. Anything already withdrawn in cash, converted, or moved out of the country is beyond reach — which is why recovery falls away so steeply with every hour of delay.

A hold is also not a refund. Money frozen in a mule account is evidence in a live case, and getting it released to you generally needs a court order, which takes months. Partial recovery is the common good outcome. Full recovery happens and is not the norm, and nobody can promise it to you in advance — not the bank, not the police, and not me.

Two things stay worth doing even when the money is gone. Reporting builds the case against the network rather than only your own case, and that is how numbers and accounts get blocked before they reach the next person. And the acknowledgement number is the anchor for any later claim, insurance question or restitution order.

One warning specific to the aftermath: anybody who contacts you afterwards offering guaranteed recovery for an advance fee is running the follow-up, on a list of people known to have paid once. Real recovery goes through the bank, the police and the courts, and it never begins with a stranger's call.

Setting up an older parent's phone

This is the section that actually reduces losses, because the people these scripts are aimed at are not the people who read security articles. The work is an afternoon, and most of it is about capping what a bad ten minutes can cost rather than about preventing the bad ten minutes.

  • Separate the money from the app. Link UPI to an account that carries a working balance, and keep the savings in an account with no UPI on it. This one change is worth more than everything below it, because it turns the worst possible day into a bounded number.
  • Set the limits down to what a week actually costs. Apps and banks both let you cap per-transaction and daily amounts, and the defaults are set for the whole country rather than for one retired person's grocery run. The ceiling that matters is the lower of what the app allows and what the bank allows, so set both.
  • Turn collect requests off where the app offers it. Several apps now let you refuse requests from anyone who is not a verified merchant, or have narrowed the feature considerably. Where it cannot be switched off, replace it with a motion: an unexpected request is declined first and asked about afterwards. Declining costs nothing and is reversible; approving is neither.
  • Audit the autopay mandates. Open the list, read the recurring amounts, and cancel anything nobody recognises. Then put it in the calendar to do again in six months.
  • Close the install route. Turn off installing apps from unknown sources, leave the store's own scanning on, and remove any remote-access or screen-sharing app that is already there. If one is present and nobody can explain it, treat that as an incident rather than as clutter.
  • Put a second person on anything large. The version that always works is a family rule agreed out loud: above an agreed figure, nothing moves until a named person has been called. The version built into the system is UPI Circle, the delegation feature, where a primary account holder lets a family member spend from their account within a cap and, under partial delegation, approves each payment individually. Availability differs by app, and it only helps if the parent is willing to spend from the delegated account rather than their own — but it is the only mechanism I know of that puts a second person in the loop by design rather than by agreement.
  • Save 1930 as a contact, plainly named. Nobody searches well while panicking, and a number that is already in the phone is a number that gets dialled.

Then have the conversation, and have it before rather than after. Frame it as information about other people — "this is going around, and it has caught doctors and retired officers" — rather than as a test of their judgment, because a conversation that sounds like a test guarantees they will not call you on the day. Give one sentence, not a list. Lists do not survive fear; a single line does, and the line is at the top of this article.

Be honest with yourself about what all this buys. None of it stops a determined script, and somebody patient enough will still get a person to approve a debit. What it does is lower the ceiling, add friction where friction is cheap, and put a second human being between a frightened person and an irreversible transfer. That is a real improvement, and it is not the same thing as safety.

Questions people ask

Do I ever need to enter my UPI PIN to receive money?

No. Not in any app, not for any amount, not to verify an account, not to claim a refund, not once. The PIN is a signature on a debit and it does nothing else. Receiving needs no approval because there is nothing to approve — the credit simply happens and you are told afterwards. If a screen is asking for your PIN, money is leaving your account, whatever the text above the prompt says.

Can somebody empty my account just by knowing my UPI ID or my phone number?

No. A UPI ID is an address, like an email address. Somebody who has it can send you money, or raise a collect request asking you for money. A request cannot debit you on its own — it arrives as a prompt on your phone and dies there unless you approve it with your PIN. Almost every account that was supposedly emptied by a stranger who knew the ID was emptied by an approval the owner was talked into giving.

Somebody transferred money to me by mistake and wants it back. What should I do?

Do not send it back through their link, their QR code or their request. A genuine misdirected transfer is reversed by the sender's own bank, on the sender's written request, with a reference number — so tell them to raise it there. Leave the amount untouched, tell your own bank what happened, and keep the messages. If the money that reached you was itself stolen, handing it onward makes your account a link in the chain, and it is your account that gets frozen.

A buyer sent me a screenshot showing the payment. Is that proof?

No. A screenshot is an image, and images are edited in seconds. The only thing that settles it is your own side of the transaction: the alert from your bank, or the entry in your own statement or passbook. Wait for that before you hand anything over. The awkwardness of making somebody wait is exactly what the script is built on, which is why the wait is the defence.

Can I turn collect requests off completely?

Sometimes, and it depends on the app in front of you. Person-to-person collect has been tightened repeatedly — the limits on it are far lower than on an ordinary payment, and some apps have put it behind an extra step or withdrawn it for individuals altogether. Because that keeps changing, look in the app's own payment settings rather than trusting a figure in an article. Where it cannot be switched off, the workable substitute is a habit: an unexpected request is declined first and asked about afterwards.

How much of the money usually comes back?

Often none of it, and I would rather say that plainly than imply otherwise. A complaint triggers holds down the chain of accounts the money passed through, and only the portion still sitting somewhere in that chain can be held — anything withdrawn in cash, converted or moved abroad is beyond reach. A hold is also not a refund: frozen money is evidence in a live case, and releasing it to you generally needs a court order and months of waiting. Partial recovery is the common good outcome, and nobody can promise more in advance.

A parent already installed a screen-sharing app during a call. What now?

Treat it as access rather than as an app. End the session, uninstall it, and then revoke the accessibility or screen-recording permission it was granted, because on Android that permission is what outlives the call. Change the UPI PIN and the app passcode from a different device if you can, review and cancel any autopay mandate that appeared, and call the bank to say the screen was shared during a call — that fact changes the conversation, so do not leave it out. Then report it even if nothing moved yet.

The system did not fail in any of these stories. A debit needed the payer's PIN, on the payer's device, in the payer's own app, and it got one. That is the uncomfortable thing to sit with, and it is also the reason the defence is a sentence rather than a setting. Six digits authorise money leaving. They have never once caused money to arrive. If somebody in your family remembers only that, the afternoon you spent on this was worth it.
