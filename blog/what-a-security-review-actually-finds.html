What a security review actually finds

People want to know what a review will turn up before they agree to one, which is fair. The honest answer is that it is usually the same short list, in roughly the same order, for reasons that are almost never carelessness. Here is that list, the difference between a review and the two things it gets confused with, and the part that does not belong on a sales page: a clean review does not mean you are secure.

What a review actually is

A security review is a structured read of a system by somebody who did not build it, with access to everything: the source, the configuration, the cloud console, the deployment pipeline, and an hour with the engineers. It is time-boxed. Its job is to enumerate the ways the system can be made to do something it should not, work out which of those an attacker would actually reach, and hand back an ordered list of changes.

The access is the whole point. On a small product, reading is enormously more efficient than guessing. An outsider probing a login form from the internet has to infer what the code does; a reviewer with the repository open can see the handler that forgot a check, and can see it in ten minutes rather than two days. The trade-off is that a reviewer with full access is not proving the attacker's path — that is a different exercise, and it is the next section.

A review is also not a scan. Automated tooling is one input among several, and on the systems I see it produces two useful findings and four hundred lines of noise. The value a person adds is deciding which of those four hundred lines is reachable from the request path, and noticing the thing no scanner has a rule for, which is nearly always the authorisation logic.

Review, penetration test, red team

Clients routinely ask for one of these and need another. The words are used loosely across the industry, including by people selling them, so it is worth being exact.

  • A review is white box and broad. You hand over everything and I read it. The output is a ranked list of weaknesses with the smallest change that closes each one. It is the right first engagement for a product nobody outside the team has ever looked at, because it finds the most per unit of money and it finds things that are invisible from the outside.
  • A penetration test is a bounded attack simulation, usually with far less information, against an agreed target and within an agreed window. The output is depth: proof that a specific path works, with the request and response attached. When a customer's contract says they require an annual test by a third party, this is the thing they mean, and a review will not satisfy that clause.
  • A red team is goal-based adversary emulation — get to this data, by any route, without being caught. It tests your detection and response as much as your software, which is why it is wasted money until you have detection and response to test. If nobody is watching the logs, a red team will simply tell you that nobody is watching the logs, which a five-minute conversation would also have told you.

The mistake I see most often runs in one direction: a company's first enterprise customer asks a security question, somebody buys a penetration test, and the test finds the same eight things below at three times the cost, because paying an attacker to rediscover from outside what a reader could have been told from inside is an expensive way to buy a list. The mistake in the other direction is rarer but worse: buying a review when the contract you are trying to close specifically names a third-party test report. Read the clause before you buy. That is the same discipline as scoping the work before you quote it — agree what the deliverable is, in writing, and the noun stops mattering.

The eight things that keep coming back

What follows is ordered by a rough product of two things: how often it appears, and how badly it ends when it is exploited. It is not a checklist I work through — it is what the work keeps returning. Every one of these has a reason behind it, and the reason is almost never that somebody was careless. It is usually a decision that was correct when it was made and stopped being correct when the product grew past the conditions that made it correct.

That distinction matters commercially as well as technically. A report that reads as a list of mistakes gets defended against. A report that says this was right in 2023 and the thing that changed is that you added a customer portal gets fixed.

1. Broken object-level authorisation

The single most common serious finding, by a distance. There is an identifier somewhere the caller controls — /api/invoices/1042, a ?account=88 in a query string, a document id in a JSON body — and the handler checks that you are logged in but never checks that the thing you asked for is yours.

Finding it is unglamorous. I take two accounts in different tenants, list every identifier the first account can see, and ask for each of them as the second account. It takes an afternoon and it is dull, which is precisely why it survives: it is not the sort of thing that shows up in a unit test, because the test was written from the perspective of the user who owns the row.

Why it happens. The first version of the endpoint was written when the check was true by construction. There was one tenant, or one internal team, or the service was only reachable inside the office network, so "the only person who can call this is the person it belongs to" was a fact about the deployment. Then a customer portal arrived, or the API went public, or a second tenant was onboarded, and the fact stopped being true. Nobody rewrote the handler, because nothing about it looked wrong.

Why it ends badly. There is no exploit to write, no tooling to buy and no skill barrier. A curious customer finds it by changing a number in the address bar. And because it is a data-access flaw rather than a code-execution flaw, one loop over a range of integers is the entire breach — which converts an engineering ticket into a notification obligation and a regulator's question, the territory covered in security compliance, explained.

The fix that actually holds. Not a check added to the handler, because the next handler somebody writes will forget it too. Scope the query itself: every read and every write carries the caller's account as a condition, enforced in the data-access layer rather than remembered by each endpoint. Return 404 rather than 403 for objects that are not yours, so the response does not confirm that the record exists. And write the test with two accounts, because a test with one account can never fail this way.

2. No rate limit on the front door

Login, password reset, OTP verification, invite acceptance, email-change confirmation. On most small products at least one of these will accept as many attempts as you care to send.

Why it happens. Rate limiting is infrastructure, and the authentication flow is usually the first thing anyone builds — before there was infrastructure to put it behind. It then ships behind a load balancer or a CDN that probably does something, and nobody ever confirms what, because confirming it looks like attacking your own service and feels like something you need permission for.

What it costs. Three separate things, only one of which is the obvious one. Credential stuffing replays somebody else's breach against your login at machine speed. A six-digit code with no attempt cap is a space of one million guesses, and a million requests over an unthrottled endpoint is minutes, not years — the length of the code was never the control, the attempt limit was. And the one that is not a security incident at all: an unlimited password-reset endpoint is a free email cannon pointed at anyone, which is how a young company gets its sending domain blocked for a fortnight.

The fix. Limits keyed on the account as well as the source address, so rotating proxies does not defeat them. An attempt counter bound to the code itself, which invalidates the code rather than merely slowing the caller. Exponential backoff instead of a hard lockout, so the control does not become a way to lock legitimate users out on purpose. That trade-off — protecting a system without becoming your own denial of service — is the same one I spent a research project on, and it applies to every limiter you will ever design.

3. Secrets in the repo or the build

A live key in the repository, in the git history after being "removed", in a Docker layer, in a CI log, or shipped to the browser inside a frontend bundle.

Why it happens. The first deploy needed a key and there was no secret store yet, so it went in a file. Later a secret store arrived and the application was migrated to it — but the old value was never rotated, so the key in the history is still a working key. And the two mechanics that surprise people are both about how build systems store things. Deleting a secret in a later commit does not remove it: git keeps every version of every file as a content-addressed object, and anyone with the repository can still read the old blob. You can watch that happen in the git objects lab. The same is true of container images — COPY .env in one instruction and RUN rm .env in the next leaves the file sitting in the earlier layer, and the layer ships. The Docker layers lab takes an image apart so you can see it.

What it costs. Entirely dependent on what the key opens. A read-only analytics token is a shrug. A cloud access key attached to the role in the next finding is the whole account, which is why these two are so dangerous together and so unremarkable apart.

The fix, in this order. Rotate first — it is the only step that changes anything today. Then scrub, accepting that scrubbing history on a repository with forks is best-effort. Then prevent: a pre-commit scanner and your host's push protection, so the next one is caught at the door rather than at the review.

4. Cloud roles that only ever grew

The service account with a wildcard on the bucket. The CI role that can deploy anything to anywhere. The administrator policy attached at two in the morning during an outage and never taken off.

Why it happens. This one deserves particular sympathy, because least privilege is easy at design time and expensive at debug time. Every over-broad role I have found was created to unblock somebody, correctly, at a moment when the alternative was leaving the service down while a permission was worked out by trial and error. That was the right call. It stopped being the right call on the following Tuesday, when nobody narrowed it, and there is no alarm for a permission that is merely wider than it needs to be.

What it costs. On its own, usually nothing. It is a multiplier. It converts a leaked build token, or a server-side request forgery in one small service, from an incident into an account-wide one. This is the finding that most often moves another finding two rows up the list.

The fix. Do not guess at a narrower policy — read the access records. Every major cloud will tell you which permissions a principal actually used over the last ninety days. Narrow to that plus a written exception for the things that run quarterly, then check again in a month. It is unglamorous, it is mostly reading, and the reading is the single highest-leverage afternoon in most cloud accounts — the rollout afterwards, done safely across a live account, is longer than that, which is why the plan below has it waiting behind a stopgap rather than shipping this week.

5. The staging box with real data

An environment that was going to be temporary. It usually has no multi-factor authentication, a password shared in a chat thread, a build from months ago with the vulnerabilities that have since been patched in production, no logging anybody reads — and a copy of the production database, because realistic data was needed to test something.

Why it happens. Because realistic data genuinely is useful and anonymising it genuinely is hard. Masking a database properly means understanding every field, every foreign key and every free-text column somebody has been pasting phone numbers into. Copying production was the cheap way to get a working environment before a deadline, and the deadline was real.

What it costs. The same data with none of the controls. It is also outside most teams' mental model of "the system", which means it is outside the patch cycle, outside the monitoring, and outside the diagram somebody drew of the architecture. In a review, this is the asset most likely to be one nobody mentioned to me, which is why the first thing I do is go and look rather than ask: subdomain records in the DNS lab, and issued certificates in the certificate transparency lab, will usually surface a hostname the team had forgotten they owned.

The fix, in a deliberate order. Find every environment first. Put each one behind authentication and off the public internet second — that is an afternoon. Argue about data masking third, because it is a project, and a project is not a reason to leave the door open for the quarter it takes.

The pattern under all five so far. None of these are ignorance. Each is a decision that was correct under conditions that later changed — one tenant, no infrastructure, no secret store, an outage, a deadline. Security debt behaves exactly like technical debt: it accrues from reasonable choices and it is repaid deliberately or not at all. A reviewer's real job is to find where the conditions moved and the code did not.

6. Dependency drift

Not "your scanner reports four hundred vulnerable packages", which is a fact of no use to anybody. Two things in that report matter: which of those packages is reachable from code you actually run, and which ones are now so far behind that upgrading has quietly become a project instead of a patch.

Why it happens. The lockfile was pinned to make builds reproducible, which was right. Then the upgrade was deferred because a release was close, which was also right. Repeat that four times and a minor version behind has become a major version behind, and the upgrade now requires a code change, so it gets deferred again — this time for a reason that is real.

What it costs. Occasionally it is the way in. More often it is the reason a fix for something else takes three weeks, because the patched release requires a framework version you cannot yet run. That second cost is the one worth pricing, and almost nobody does.

The fix. Split the list. Anything reachable from a request path gets fixed now. Everything else gets an upgrade cadence with a named owner and a slot in the sprint, because a dependency policy that lives in a document and not in a calendar is a document. And rank by reachability rather than by score: a 9.8 in a parser your code never calls sits below a 6.5 in your login path, every time.

7. Errors that say too much

Stack traces rendered to the browser. SQL fragments in a 500 page. Internal hostnames, absolute file paths, framework and version banners. And the quieter one: a login that says "no such user" for one address and "wrong password" for another.

Why it happens. Verbose errors were correct in development, where they are the fastest debugging tool available. The production configuration inherited from the development one, or the flag was flipped during an incident to see what was happening and never flipped back — which is the same shape as the cloud role above, and for the same human reason.

What it costs. On its own, very little, and it is often argued down for exactly that reason. As an accelerator it is expensive: it names the framework, the version, the ORM and the directory layout, and sometimes hands over the query. It turns an afternoon of an attacker guessing into ten minutes of reading. The account-enumeration variant is worse than it looks, because knowing which addresses are registered is what makes a credential-stuffing run efficient.

The fix. A generic error page carrying a correlation id; the detail goes to the log, and the user quotes the id to support. Identical responses and comparable timings on every authentication path, whether or not the account exists.

8. Sessions that never end

A session that survives a password change. A token that outlives the employee who was issued it. A permission that was revoked in the database while the credential that already carries it keeps working until it expires next year.

Why it happens. Stateless sessions were chosen for good reasons — they scale, they remove a database round trip on every request, and they make horizontal scaling simple. The trade-off is that you gave up the ability to take one back. That trade-off is usually made correctly and recorded nowhere, so the person who later asks "can we log everyone out?" is told yes, in a meeting, by somebody who has not read the token code. The JWT lab decodes a token in the browser and shows you exactly what a bearer of it is claiming, which is the fastest way to see why revocation is a separate problem.

What it costs. It undermines every other fix on the list. You rotate a leaked credential and the attacker's existing session keeps working. You demote an account and the old token keeps its old claims. Revocation is what converts "we found it" into "we stopped it", and without it your incident response has no off switch.

The fix. Short-lived access tokens with a refresh token you can actually revoke. Invalidate every session on password change, on role change and on offboarding. And make "log out everywhere, now" an action an administrator can take at three in the morning without waiting for a deploy — that button is worth more than most of the controls people ask about first.

How findings get ranked

A list of eight problems with no order attached is not a plan, it is an anxiety. The ordering is most of the deliverable, and it is not done by CVSS alone.

CVSS describes a vulnerability in the abstract, as though it existed on its own. Your instance of it is concrete: it sits behind your authentication, in your network, reachable by your users, held by an account with your permissions. Two systems with the identical 7.5 can deserve entirely different urgency. So the score is one input to three questions:

  1. Is it reachable from where an attacker actually stands — anonymously, as any signed-up user, or only as an administrator who is already inside?
  2. What does it get them from there — one record, every record, or the ability to run code and reach the next thing?
  3. What does the fix cost — an afternoon, a sprint, or a migration?

The output of that is a plan rather than a ranking: fix the cheap and reachable things this week, schedule the expensive and reachable ones with a stopgap running in the meantime, batch the cheap and unreachable ones into the next sprint, and write down — in a sentence, with a name against it — the ones you are choosing to accept. That last category is not a failure. An accepted risk that somebody decided is safer than a high-severity finding nobody read.

See what a score is and is not. The CVSS lab on this site lets you build a vector metric by metric and watch the number move, so you can see how much of a score comes from assumptions about your environment rather than from the flaw. It is a good way to arrive at the point this section is making: a score is an input to a decision, never the decision. Nothing you type leaves the browser.

What you actually get

Concretely, at the end of a review:

  • A findings list. Each entry says where it is — file, endpoint, resource — how I confirmed it, what an attacker gets from it, and the smallest change that closes it. If I could not confirm something and am reporting a suspicion, it says that too, in those words.
  • A remediation plan ordered by risk against effort, in the shape of the diagram above, with a stopgap named for anything that cannot realistically be fixed this month.
  • An architecture note. The handful of things that are not findings today but will generate findings later — the second permission model nobody has reconciled with the first, the queue that trusts its own messages, the service that has to be an administrator because of one call.
  • A re-test. Once you have made the changes, I check them, within a stated window and at no extra cost. A review without a re-test is half a service, because the fix is where new bugs get introduced.
  • A conversation with your engineers. A document nobody reads is not a deliverable. An hour walking the list with the people who will do the work is what converts it into merged pull requests.

What you do not get is a certificate. A review is not an audit, and I cannot attest to anything. If a customer contract requires an audited attestation or a third-party penetration test report, that is a different instrument with a different price, and the time to discover it is before the engagement rather than during it. The compliance post lays out which framework wants which artefact.

What you have to bring

A review is a collaboration, and it is worth saying plainly what makes it worth doing, because without these the engagement gets more expensive and less useful at the same time.

  • Read access to the code and the configuration. The repository, the infrastructure definitions, the cloud console in a read-only role. Without it I am inferring from the outside, which is slower, less complete, and costs more for less.
  • Two test accounts, in different tenants, in a non-production environment. Half the findings above cannot be demonstrated with one account, and a finding I cannot demonstrate is a finding your team is entitled to disbelieve.
  • An hour with someone who can answer "why is it like this". This is the single most valuable input, and the most often skipped. Nearly every finding has a history, and the history changes the fix — a permission that exists for one nightly job is a different problem from a permission nobody remembers granting.
  • Written authorisation and a defined scope. What I may touch, what is out of bounds, which hours, and who to call if something falls over. Testing without that in writing is not something I will do, for your protection and mine.
  • Somebody who can accept a risk. Not every finding gets fixed, and that is fine — but a finding nobody has the authority to decide about stays open forever and quietly rots the credibility of the whole list.

If you want to think through what you are protecting and from whom before any of this, the threat modelling lab walks through assets, adversaries and trust boundaries in the browser. Turning up to the first call with a rough answer to "what would hurt most if it leaked" makes the whole engagement sharper.

A clean review does not mean secure

This is the part that costs me work to say, and it is the reason to say it.

A review is a sample: one person's attention, over a bounded number of days, on the system as it stood that week. Absence of findings is evidence, and evidence is worth paying for, but it is not proof. Three specific limits, none of which go away with a bigger budget:

  • The code changes on Tuesday. Every deploy after the review is unreviewed. The report describes a system that no longer exists in exactly that form, and the gap widens daily.
  • I read what I was given. An environment nobody mentioned, a repository nobody listed, a vendor integration described as "just a webhook" — I look for these, and I still only find the ones that leave traces.
  • The classes I am good at are not all the classes there are. A reviewer strong on authorisation, cloud configuration and application logic is not the same person as a cryptography specialist or someone who reverse-engineers firmware. Where a system needs that, I will say so rather than produce a paragraph that sounds confident.

Anyone who tells you their review makes you secure is selling something. What it honestly does is remove the things an attacker would find first, and replace a vague unease with a short written list of things you now know you are choosing to live with.

That second half is the part people underrate. Most teams I meet are not carrying an unknown risk so much as an undiscussed one — everybody suspects the staging box is a problem, nobody has said it in a room where a decision could be made. A review is, among other things, a device for making that conversation happen with a document in front of it. It is cheaper to be told than to be shown.

Questions people ask

Is a security review the same as a penetration test?

No. A review is a structured read of the system with full access — code, configuration, cloud, and the people who built it — and its output is breadth: the list of ways the system can be made to misbehave, ranked. A penetration test is a bounded attack simulation, usually with much less information, and its output is depth: proof that a specific path is exploitable, with evidence attached. Reviews find more per rupee on a system nobody has looked at before. Penetration tests are what a customer's contract usually means when it asks for one. They answer different questions, and the industry uses both words loosely, so agree the deliverable rather than the noun.

How long does a review take?

For a small SaaS product or an internal tool, usually one to two weeks of calendar time for a few days of actual work, plus a re-test later once the fixes land. The variable is not the size of the codebase — it is how many separate authorisation models are in it. One product with one permission model reads quickly. The same product after three years of bolted-on roles, an admin panel, a partner API and a mobile client is four systems, and each one has to be walked separately.

Will the review break anything?

It should not, because most of the work is reading rather than sending traffic, and what testing there is happens against a non-production environment by default. Where something has to be tried against production — because staging does not reproduce it — that gets agreed in writing first, scheduled, and done with someone from your side on a call. The honest caveat is that rate-limit testing is the one activity that can visibly load a system, so it is always scheduled rather than sprung.

We already have SOC 2. Do we still need this?

They are different instruments. A SOC 2 report attests that you operate the controls you said you operate, over a period, in the judgement of an auditor. It does not involve anyone reading your authorisation logic. I have seen a system with a clean report and an object identifier in a URL that nobody checked. Compliance describes the process around the software; a review looks at the software. Neither substitutes for the other, and a company that has done one is usually well organised enough to get good value from the other.

What if you find nothing serious?

Then you get a short report saying so, the architecture notes, and the same fee, because you paid for the looking rather than for the finding. It is uncommon on a system nobody has reviewed before, and it is not the same as a guarantee — it means the checks I ran on the code I was given, in the time I was given, did not surface a serious issue. If I ever return nothing and cannot tell you specifically what I looked at and what I could not reach, you should not accept the report.

Can you give us a certificate or a letter for our customer?

I can write a factual summary letter stating what was reviewed, when, by whom, and what was fixed on re-test. I cannot certify anything, because a certificate implies a standard and an accreditation body behind it, and a review has neither. If a customer contract specifically requires a third-party penetration test report or an audited attestation, a review will not satisfy it, and you should know that before you buy rather than after.

How often should we do this?

Tie it to change, not to the calendar. The useful triggers are a new authorisation model, a new class of user such as a partner or a reseller, a migration, an acquisition of somebody else's code, and the first enterprise customer whose questionnaire will ask. Once a year is a reasonable default for a product under steady development, and once a year is far less useful than one review timed for the month before you ship a multi-tenant portal.

The eight findings above are not a secret, and publishing them costs me nothing, because knowing the list is not the same as having walked your own system against it. If you read this and recognised three of them, that recognition is already worth more than most reports — and the next step is either an afternoon with your own code and two test accounts, or a conversation.
