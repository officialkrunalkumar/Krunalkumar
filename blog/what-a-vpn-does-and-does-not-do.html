What a VPN does and does not do

A VPN is an encrypted tunnel to a server somebody else runs, which then makes requests on your behalf. That is genuinely useful in specific situations and useless in most of the ones it gets sold for. The single most important sentence is that it changes who you have to trust, rather than removing the need to trust anyone.

I have no VPN to sell you and I am not going to name one. This is what the technology does, described accurately enough that you can decide whether your situation is one of the ones it helps with.

What it actually is

Without a VPN, your traffic goes from your device to your router, to your internet provider, and out to whatever server you asked for. Each of those can see something. The provider sees which servers you connect to and when. The destination sees your address.

With one, your device opens an encrypted tunnel to the VPN provider's server first, and everything goes through that. Your own network and your ISP now see one encrypted connection to one address, and nothing about what is inside it. The destination sees a request arriving from the VPN's server rather than from you.

Three real consequences follow, and they are the honest case for using one: your traffic is hidden from the local network, your address is hidden from the destination, and your apparent location moves.

It moves the trust rather than removing it

This is the spine of the whole subject, so it gets its own section.

Your ISP could see which sites you visit. Now they cannot — and the VPN provider can. You have not eliminated the party who can watch your traffic. You have chosen a different one.

Whether that is an improvement depends entirely on the comparison. A provider in a jurisdiction with strong privacy protections, with a published and audited logging policy, whose business is selling privacy, may well be a better custodian than an ISP with a legal obligation to retain records. Or it may be a free app whose actual business is the data. The swap can be strongly positive or strongly negative, and the technology is identical in both cases — which is why “use a VPN” on its own is not advice.

On logging policies specifically: a no-logs claim is unverifiable from outside by construction. An independent audit helps and is much better than nothing, but it is a snapshot of a configuration on a particular day, not a continuing guarantee. Treat it as evidence of seriousness rather than proof.

What HTTPS already did to the sales pitch

The classic advertisement is the coffee shop: someone on the same wifi reading your passwords and your bank details. That threat was real, and it was substantially closed by something else entirely — near-universal HTTPS.

When a connection is encrypted end to end, the person next to you on the network sees ciphertext. They cannot read your messages or steal your session by watching, whether or not you have a VPN. That part of the pitch is describing a web that mostly stopped existing.

What does still leak on a hostile network is metadata: which sites you are visiting, via DNS lookups and via the server name sent in the clear during connection setup. So the honest pitch for a VPN on public wifi is not “they can read your banking” — it is “they can see where you go”. That is a real privacy concern and a much smaller one than the advertising implies.

It is also worth knowing that there are narrower tools for exactly that leak: encrypted DNS closes the lookup, and Encrypted Client Hello closes the server name, where both ends support it. If metadata on a specific network is your actual problem, those address it more directly and without routing everything through a third party.

The list of things it does not do

This is the part most worth reading carefully, because almost all of the disappointment around VPNs comes from expecting one of these.

  • It does not make you anonymous to a site you log into. If you sign in, you have told them who you are. The tunnel is irrelevant from that moment on.
  • It does not stop cookies. Tracking that follows an identifier in your browser is unaffected by which network path the request took.
  • It does not stop fingerprinting. Your screen size, fonts, timezone, graphics stack and dozens of other properties are read from the browser, not the network. A new address with the same fingerprint is still recognisably the same visitor — and a timezone that disagrees with your apparent country is itself a signal.
  • It does not stop malware or phishing. A tunnel carries a malicious page exactly as faithfully as a legitimate one. Some providers bundle blocklists; that is a separate product sharing an app.
  • It does not protect data after the tunnel ends. Whatever you send arrives at the destination in full. Their breach is still your breach.
  • It does not encrypt anything that was not already encrypted on the final hop from the VPN server onwards. If a site is unencrypted, it is unencrypted between the VPN and that site.
  • It does not defeat a serious adversary by itself. Traffic timing and volume still correlate, and a party who can watch both ends of the tunnel does not need to decrypt it.

The fingerprinting point is easier to believe when you see it. There is a page here that shows what your browser hands over before any script asks — none of which a VPN changes.

The ways a tunnel leaks anyway

Even where a VPN is the right tool, the protection is not automatic. Three failure modes are common enough to be worth knowing by name, because each one quietly returns you to the situation you were trying to leave.

The connection drops. Tunnels fail — on a network change, waking from sleep, a server restart. Unless something stops traffic while it is down, your device simply carries on over the ordinary connection, usually without telling you. A kill switch is the feature that blocks traffic rather than letting it fall back, and it is the single setting most worth checking.

DNS goes around it. If lookups are still being sent to your ISP’s resolver while everything else goes through the tunnel, the list of sites you visit is leaking to exactly the party you were hiding it from. Well-configured clients route DNS through the tunnel too; it is worth verifying rather than assuming.

Something bypasses the tunnel entirely. Split tunnelling is a deliberate version of this and useful when you understand it. The accidental versions are worse: traffic over a protocol the client does not capture, or an application with its own network stack.

The general lesson is the same one as everywhere else in security. A control you have not verified is a control you are assuming. If the reason you are using a VPN matters, check that it is actually carrying your traffic rather than trusting the icon.

When it is genuinely the right tool

Having been unkind about the marketing, here are the cases where I think it is a sound choice:

  • A network you actively distrust — one that injects content, blocks things arbitrarily, or is run by someone with an interest in your traffic.
  • An ISP you distrust more than the alternative, particularly where retention or resale of browsing records is routine.
  • Hiding your address from a specific destination, which is a genuine and frequently overlooked use: the site you are looking at does not learn your rough location or your provider.
  • Reaching your own network remotely, which is what the technology was built for in the first place and where it is unambiguously correct.
  • Geographic restriction, which works, though it is a terms-of-service question rather than a privacy one.

And the case where a VPN is the wrong tool and people reach for it anyway: when the threat is serious and identification would be harmful. A journalist, a source, or someone at genuine risk needs a design where no single party can link both ends of the connection. Tor is built for that, at a real cost in speed and convenience, and its protection comes from a very different property — not one trusted provider, but no single party holding both halves.

On free VPNs, briefly: running servers and bandwidth costs money, so if you are not paying, the arrangement is being funded some other way. That is not automatically sinister, but it is a question with only a few possible answers, and most of them involve the traffic you were trying to protect.

How to decide, in one question

Ask who you are hiding from, and then check whether a VPN sits between you and them.

  • The wifi owner, or your ISP? Yes, a VPN is directly the right shape.
  • The site itself? Only your address, and only until you log in.
  • Advertisers and trackers? Mostly no. That is a browser problem, not a network one.
  • A government, seriously? A VPN alone is not the right answer.
  • Nobody in particular, just uneasy? Then you are being sold a feeling, and your money buys more safety in a password manager and two-factor authentication.

One honest note on my own confidence: this is a description of what the technology can and cannot do, and I have deliberately made no claim about any specific provider's conduct, because that is not verifiable from outside and I would only be repeating marketing or rumour. Whether a given company is trustworthy is exactly the part you have to decide without proof — which is, in the end, the same conclusion as the second section.
