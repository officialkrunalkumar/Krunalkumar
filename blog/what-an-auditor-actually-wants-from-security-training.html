What an auditor actually wants from security training

Every corporate training conversation that reaches procurement arrives at the same question, usually phrased as "is your training certified?" What the person means is: when our assessor asks how we satisfy the awareness requirement, will this hold up? Those are two different questions, and only one of them is about the trainer. This is what the standards actually say, what an assessor asks to see, and what nobody can promise you.

The question behind the question

"Is your training certified?" is a question about the vendor. "Will this satisfy our auditor?" is a question about your file. They get asked in the same breath and they have almost nothing to do with each other, which is why the conversation so often ends with both sides slightly disappointed.

No logo on a certificate satisfies an awareness control. What satisfies it is a record showing that the right people were trained on the right things at the right time, inside a programme somebody planned. Most of that record is produced by you, not by whoever stands at the front of the room. The trainer contributes four artefacts and a date. You contribute the plan, the personnel list, the follow-up for the people who missed it, and the discipline to keep all of it in one place.

So the useful version of this article is not "book me." It is: here is what each of the three regimes you are most likely to be facing actually asks for, here is the pack that answers it, and here is the line past which no training vendor — including this one — can help you.

What this is and is not. This is practitioner orientation from someone who builds and delivers the material, not legal advice and not an assurance opinion. Standards get revised, rules get notified, and your own assessor's judgement governs your engagement. Read the clause text yourself, and where the obligations are real, talk to counsel or to your assessor before relying on anything here.

What Annex A 6.3 actually says

The 2022 revision of ISO/IEC 27001 restructured Annex A into 93 controls across four themes — organizational, people, physical, technological. Awareness lives in the people theme as control 6.3, "Information security awareness, education and training." Its substance is that personnel of the organization, and relevant interested parties, shall receive appropriate information security awareness, education and training, together with regular updates of the organization's information security policy, topic-specific policies and procedures, as relevant for their job function.

That sentence is doing four things, and each one is worth reading slowly:

  • "Personnel and relevant interested parties." Not only employees. Contractors, and often the vendor staff working inside your systems, are in scope if your own risk assessment put them there. This is the most commonly missed half of the control.
  • "Appropriate." Proportionate to the role. A developer, a finance clerk and a support agent do not need identical sessions, and an assessor who sees one generic deck given to everybody will ask how you decided it was appropriate for each function.
  • "Regular updates." A cadence, not an event. The control anticipates that your policies change and that people need to hear about it more than once.
  • Tied to your own policies. The content is anchored to your information security policy and your topic-specific policies — not to generic industry material that never mentions how your company actually works.

Now the four things the control does not say, because this is where vendors overstate. It does not name a frequency. It does not name a duration. It does not say who must deliver it. And it does not require that a certificate exist at all. If someone tells you the standard mandates annual training, they are quoting a widespread convention as though it were the text.

The record requirement is somewhere else, and that is the part most people miss. Annex A is a reference set of controls you select from and justify in your Statement of Applicability. The management-system requirements you are audited against are clauses 4 to 10, and two of them do the work here. Clause 7.2, Competence, requires you to determine the competence necessary for people whose work affects information security performance, ensure it on the basis of education, training or experience, take action where there is a gap, and — the operative phrase — retain documented information as evidence of competence. Clause 7.3, Awareness, requires that people doing work under your control are aware of the policy, of their contribution to its effectiveness, and of the implications of not conforming. Clause 7.5 governs how documented information is created, controlled and retained.

Annex A 6.3 is why you run the session. Clause 7.2 is why you keep the register. If you take one sentence from this article into your next vendor call, make it that one.

What an assessor asks to see

A certification audit is a sample, not a census. Nobody reads every record you hold. The auditor takes your personnel list, picks a handful of names across different roles and joining dates, and asks you to produce the evidence for each of those people. Almost everything below follows from that one mechanic.

In practice the questions are four:

  1. Who attended? Named individuals, matched against the personnel list they came from. "The engineering team" is not an answer to a question about one specific person.
  2. What was covered? A topic list, or the material itself, close enough to your policy that an assessor can see the connection without you narrating it.
  3. When, and for how long? A date, and hours. The hours matter more than people expect, because they are the difference between a briefing and training.
  4. Where is the plan? Evidence that this was programmed rather than improvised.

The fourth is the hardest, and the one that separates a file that reads well from one that does not. A single dated session sitting in a folder reads as a reaction to the audit. A one-page programme — the topics, the cadence, who owns it, how new joiners are covered, when it gets reviewed — turns that same session into an instance of a system. Assessors look for the system, because a system is what a management standard certifies.

The sample that catches people out is almost always new joiners. The original session is documented beautifully; the person who started four months later has nothing. If you fix one thing after reading this, make it induction: a documented route by which somebody joining in March receives, and is recorded as receiving, what the January cohort got.

Two more things a thorough assessor looks for. First, whether the loop closes — did you measure anything, and act on what you measured? Simulation results, a short assessment, a management review minute noting coverage and gaps. Second, and this one is self-inflicted: whether you did what your own policy says. If your policy commits you to quarterly refreshers and your records show one session a year, you have written your own nonconformity. Those are the easiest findings in the world to raise, because the auditor only has to read two of your documents.

How SOC 2 maps the same evidence

SOC 2 works differently enough that the same evidence gets treated differently, and the difference is worth understanding before you buy anything. It is not a certification: it is an attestation, in which a licensed CPA firm examines your controls against the AICPA's Trust Services Criteria and signs an opinion. How it compares with the other frameworks is the longer subject of the compliance explainer.

Here is the mechanic that matters for training. In SOC 2, you write the control. The criteria are general; your description of the system is where you state what you actually do. A sentence like "all personnel complete security awareness training within thirty days of hire and at least annually thereafter" is your sentence, not the framework's. The service auditor then tests whether that sentence was true for every instance in the observation period. You do not fail because a standard demanded a particular class. You fail because your own stated control did not operate.

Where does the evidence usually land? Typically against the common criteria covering the control environment and internal communication — the criterion about demonstrating a commitment to attract, develop and retain competent individuals, and the one about internally communicating the information and responsibilities that internal control needs in order to function. Some firms tie the code-of-conduct acknowledgement in alongside it.

But state the limit plainly, because this is where training vendors reliably oversell: which criterion a given piece of evidence supports is the service auditor's determination, not the trainer's. There is no accreditation that makes a course "SOC 2 compliant" — the phrase describes something that does not exist. A vendor cannot map your evidence for you, cannot bind your auditor to a mapping, and cannot tell you in advance that a session will be accepted against a criterion. What a vendor can do is hand you a pack complete enough that the mapping is easy for the people whose call it is.

Two practical consequences. A Type II report punishes a missing register far harder than a Type I does, because it tests operation across a period rather than design on a single date — a good session that fell outside the observation window buys you nothing in that report. And exceptions in SOC 2 are counted per instance: three people out of forty with no completion record is a noted exception with a described cause, not an automatic failure. That is a more forgiving structure than most people expect, and a far less forgiving one if you have no records at all.

Where India's DPDP Act sits

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is the regime most likely to matter if your people or your data sit here, and it treats this subject differently again. Section 8(5) requires a Data Fiduciary to protect personal data in its possession or under its control, including data processed on its behalf by a Data Processor, by taking reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach.

The word "training" does not appear in that obligation. That is not an oversight — outcome-based drafting is deliberate, because naming specific controls in a statute freezes them at the moment of drafting. The consequence for you is precise, and worth being blunt about: training is never a box the DPDP Act asks you to tick. It is one of the things you point to when you have to argue, after something has gone wrong, that your safeguards were reasonable.

That argument is worth preparing for, because the stakes here are the highest in the Act. Its schedule of penalties puts failure to take reasonable security safeguards at the top — up to ₹250 crore, more than for any other breach of obligation the Act lists. The implementing rules notified in 2025 phase in through 2027 and set out in more detail what those safeguards are expected to include; the IT law explainer covers how the Act and the older statutes fit together.

The practical consequence for someone planning a programme is that DPDP tells you nothing about what to teach, so the useful question becomes: what would a breach investigation reveal about your people? If your incidents start with a convincing email, awareness training on phishing is a defensible safeguard, and you should be able to show it was delivered and repeated. If your real exposure is one shared administrator credential, training is not the control — access management is — and no amount of awareness content will make that argument for you. Match the training to your threat model rather than to a checklist, and the reasonableness argument mostly writes itself.

The evidence pack that survives a review

Four artefacts. Three of them are obvious once stated, and the fourth is the one most vendors skip.

  1. Attendance with dates and hours. The names of the people who were actually present, the date, and the start and end time. Not the invite list, not the calendar entry, not the headcount. For a remote session the platform's attendance export is better evidence than a typed list, because it records join and leave times per person.
  2. The material as delivered. The deck, exercises and handouts as they stood on the day, dated and kept. Version matters more than people expect: an assessor comparing coverage against your policy needs the version that was taught, not the improved one you shipped a month later.
  3. A completion record per attendee. One document per person, carrying their name, the session, the date, the hours and an identifier. Per person, because the sample is per person. A single certificate issued to the company cannot answer "show me the evidence for this employee," which is the only question that actually gets asked.
  4. A way for the reviewer to check a certificate without going through the trainer. If your auditor's only route to confirming a certificate is emailing the person who issued it, they have one source wearing two hats. Every completion certificate I issue carries a unique ID that anybody can look up on the verification page — your auditor included, without contacting me, and without any company name being published there.

The honest caveat on that fourth item, because it is the one I could most easily oversell: a verification page I host is still a record I control. What it removes is the delay and the awkwardness of a verification email landing in the middle of fieldwork, and the reasonable suspicion an auditor has about a document whose only corroboration is the person who wrote it. What it does not do is make me a certification body, or turn a lookup into a notarised fact. It is a better second source than an email. It is not a trust anchor.

There is a fifth item, and it comes from your side rather than mine: the programme document. One page naming the topics, the cadence, the owner, how new joiners are covered and when the whole thing gets reviewed. Without it, four perfect records still read as one event that happened to be well minuted.

Keep them together. One folder per session, named by date, holding the four artefacts and referenced from the programme document. That sounds like filing advice because it is filing advice, and it is worth more at audit time than a better session whose records are spread across an inbox, a shared drive, and a training platform nobody has admin access to any more.

What does not count

The failures are consistent enough to list, and none of them are about the quality of the teaching.

  • A video everyone clicked through. This is not an argument against e-learning — a well-built module that records per-user completion with a timestamp is decent evidence, often better than a live session nobody wrote down. It is an argument about what the platform logs. If your system records "opened" rather than "completed," and cannot say who reached the end, you have proof of distribution rather than participation, and an assessor will establish that with one question.
  • A policy nobody read. An acknowledgement checkbox is evidence that a person clicked a box on a date. That is a real record and worth keeping, but on its own it shows neither what they were made aware of nor that awareness followed. Clause 7.3 asks about awareness; a click is a signature, not an outcome.
  • An annual slide deck with no record of who attended. The most common failure I hear described, and the most frustrating, because the session usually happened and was usually fine. Six months later there is nothing to sample.
  • A screenshot of the video call grid. Not a register, not durable, and it names only whoever had their camera on.
  • A vendor certificate naming the company. It evidences a purchase, not attendance.
  • Training that does not match your own policy's scope. The contractor population is the usual gap, because Annex A 6.3 reaches relevant interested parties and your contractors are exactly that.

It is entirely possible to run genuinely good training and have nothing to show for it, and equally possible to hold an unimpeachable file behind a session nobody remembers. An audit can only see one of those two things. That is a limitation of audits rather than a reason to stop caring about the other one — but it is a very good reason to keep the register.

The limits no trainer can cross

No trainer can promise you an audit outcome. ISO/IEC 27001 certification is granted by an accredited certification body after auditing your management system. A SOC 2 opinion is signed by a licensed CPA firm after testing your controls. A trainer supplies one input to one control among dozens. Any vendor selling "audit-ready" or "guaranteed to pass" is making a promise they have no standing to make, and the rest of their claims deserve to be read in that light.

There is also a distinction that gets deliberately blurred in this market, and naming it is what decides which vendor you should be calling.

  • Being an accredited training partner of a certification body means a scheme has approved you to deliver its courses, so that the certificate you hand out is recognised inside that scheme. This matters enormously when the credential itself is the product — a Lead Implementer or Lead Auditor course, where somebody needs a qualification the scheme will acknowledge. If that is your requirement, an accredited provider is the only correct answer.
  • Being competent to teach the material is about whether the session changes what people do on Monday. It is a different property entirely. Accredited partners can be dull and forgettable; people who have done the work can be excellent and hold no partnership at all. Neither status implies the other, and a vendor who lets you believe it does is telling you something about how the rest of the engagement will go.

Which of the two applies here, plainly: I am not an accredited training partner of any certification body, and I hold no instructor certification. None of the certificates I list is a teaching credential. If what your team needs is a scheme-recognised qualification printed on the certificate, I am the wrong supplier, and you will hear that on the intro call rather than after the invoice.

What I bring is the second kind of standing: published Linux security research (IJRAT, 2019), an M.E. in Information Security, six months as an Assistant Professor teaching operating systems and information security, teaching engineers since 2021 through MountBlue, Springboard, Coding Ninjas, Correlation One and Codementor, and volunteering as a registered cyber-expert supporting law enforcement since 2020. It is built on that teaching, not on two hundred previous corporate sessions. If you need a vendor who has run this exact day many times over, that is a fair reason to hire someone else — which is what the services page says as well.

What to ask any training vendor

Eight questions, in the order I would ask them. None of them are gotchas: a vendor who has thought about your file answers all eight in ten minutes, and one who has not will struggle on the second.

  1. What do I receive afterwards, in what format, and how quickly? A good answer names artefacts and a number of days. A vague one means you will be chasing paperwork in the week your fieldwork starts.
  2. Is the completion record issued per attendee? If it is one certificate for the company, you cannot answer a per-person sample.
  3. Can my auditor check that record without contacting you? "We will confirm by email" is workable but slow. A lookup they can run themselves is better. Either way, settle it before you book rather than during the audit.
  4. Do I get the material as delivered, dated, and may I keep it? Some vendors licence rather than hand over, which is a legitimate model. Your file still needs a copy, so agree it in the contract.
  5. Will you map your outline to the controls we have to evidence — and will you say where the mapping is our assessor's call? This is the diagnostic question. A vendor who maps everything with total confidence has not understood what an assessor does.
  6. Who is actually in the room? A named person, or a pool from which somebody gets assigned. Both models exist. Only one of them lets you evaluate the trainer in advance.
  7. How are absentees and new joiners covered? A recording with completion tracking, a repeat session, or nothing. If the answer is nothing, your induction gap is now the vendor's gap too.
  8. Have you run this before, and for how many teams? A small number is not disqualifying — everybody starts somewhere — but you should hear it before you sign rather than infer it afterwards.

One negative test is worth keeping alongside those eight: if any answer sounds like a guaranteed audit outcome, treat it as a reason to walk rather than as reassurance. The instinct that makes you suspicious of a security product promising to stop every attack applies here too. People who are careful about what they promise are usually the ones who understand the thing they are promising about, which is the argument scoping work before you quote it makes at greater length.

Questions people ask

Does ISO/IEC 27001 require annual security awareness training?

No. Neither Annex A 6.3 nor clause 7.3 names a frequency. The control asks for appropriate awareness, education and training together with regular updates of your policies, as relevant to a person's job function. Annual is a widespread convention, not a requirement. What actually binds you is your own policy: if it commits you to quarterly refreshers and your records show one session a year, the auditor raises a nonconformity against your document rather than against the standard.

Will a training certificate make us ISO 27001 certified or SOC 2 compliant?

No. ISO/IEC 27001 certification is issued by an accredited certification body after auditing your management system, and a SOC 2 report is an opinion signed by a licensed CPA firm after testing your controls. A training record is evidence for one control inside those audits, and a useful piece of evidence, but it is not a shortcut to either outcome. Any vendor implying otherwise is describing something that does not exist.

Does training have to be in-house, or can an external trainer count?

Either counts. Nothing in the standard names who delivers it. What gets tested is whether the right people received training appropriate to their job function, whether the content lined up with your policies, and whether you retained the record. An external session with a clean register is stronger evidence than an internal one nobody wrote down.

What will the auditor actually sample?

People. Expect them to take your personnel list, pick a handful of names across different roles and joining dates, and ask you to produce the evidence for each of them. New joiners are the usual weak point, because the original session is documented well and the person who started three months later is not.

Is a click-through video enough?

It depends entirely on what the platform records. A module that logs per-user completion with a timestamp is decent evidence. A video that logs only that a link was opened proves distribution rather than participation, and an assessor can tell the difference with one question about what the system stores.

Does India's DPDP Act 2023 require security training?

Not by name. Section 8(5) requires a Data Fiduciary to protect personal data by taking reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach, and leaves what counts as reasonable to be argued on the facts. Training is one of the things you point to in that argument, which is a different job from ticking a box. This is orientation rather than legal advice, and the penalty for getting safeguards wrong is the largest in the Act.

Are you an accredited training partner of a certification body?

No, and I would rather say it here than let you find out after the invoice. I hold no instructor certification either, and none of the certificates I list is a teaching credential. If your requirement is a scheme-recognised qualification printed on the certificate, book an accredited provider. What I offer instead is material built for your team after an intro call, delivered by the person you booked, with the attendance record, the material as delivered, and per-attendee certificates carrying an ID your auditor can check on the verification page without going through me.

The honest answer to "will this satisfy our auditor" is a shape rather than a yes. The session is the smaller half of it, and the more interesting half to run. The file is the half that gets read.
