What consent actually means under the DPDP Act

India's data protection law hands an ordinary business two ways to make processing lawful, and one of them is a short closed list that probably does not cover you. Everything else runs on consent — and the Act is specific about what consent is, who has to prove it, and what happens when someone takes it back. This is that law read as an engineering problem, by somebody who has to make systems satisfy it rather than argue about it in court.

What this is and is not. I am a security practitioner, not a lawyer, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is the reading I use when I have to build or review a system that will be judged against this law: what the statute says, which parts depend on Rules that are still phasing in, and where the engineering work actually is. If you hold a lot of personal data, operate in a regulated sector, or have already had an incident, take advice from someone who can be on the record for it. Dates and figures below are as at 31 August 2026, and the parts that live in the Rules are the parts most likely to have moved since.

Why the Act hangs on consent

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is a deliberately short statute. You can read the whole thing in an evening, which is not something you can say about the European regulation it is compared to, and the brevity comes from a specific design decision: it does not offer a menu of lawful bases.

Europe gives a controller six grounds to choose from, and in practice most commercial processing there runs on two of them — necessity for a contract, and legitimate interests — with consent used sparingly for the awkward cases. India inverted that. Under DPDP, processing is lawful if you have consent, or if it falls inside a closed list the Act calls legitimate uses. There is no legitimate-interests ground. There is no contract-necessity ground. The closed list is genuinely closed, and for a private company running an ordinary business, most of it does not apply.

That single structural fact is what makes this post necessary. Consent stops being the fallback and becomes the load-bearing wall. If your consent is defective, the processing built on it is not merely badly documented — it is unlawful, along with everything downstream that depended on it. And the burden of showing you had valid consent sits on you, not on the person complaining.

The good news, and I mean this genuinely, is that the work this forces is work worth doing anyway. Knowing what personal data you hold, why you hold it, who else touches it and how you would delete it is the same inventory that makes an incident survivable. It is also the inventory that most small companies discover they do not have the first time somebody asks.

Data Principal and Data Fiduciary

Two words carry the whole Act, and both are worth learning properly because the rest of the text is written in them.

  • Data Principal is the individual the personal data is about. Where that individual is a child, the term includes the parents or lawful guardian; where the individual is a person with a disability who has a lawful guardian, it includes that guardian. So the rights below are not always exercised by the person the data describes.
  • Data Fiduciary is whoever determines the purpose and means of processing — alone or with others. Elsewhere this role is called a controller. The change of word is not decoration: a fiduciary owes duties to a person, not paperwork to a regulator, and the Act reads that way throughout.
  • Data Processor is anyone who processes personal data on a Fiduciary's behalf. Under DPDP the statutory duties sit almost entirely with the Fiduciary; a Processor's obligations arrive through the contract, and the Fiduciary remains answerable for what the Processor does. That is a meaningful difference from Europe, where processors carry direct duties of their own, and it means you cannot push liability down your supply chain by choosing a vendor carefully. You can only reduce the chance you need to.

Almost every company I talk to is both at once, on different datasets. You are a Fiduciary for your own customers and your own staff. If you build software for a client and their users' data passes through your systems, you are a Processor for that. The single most useful hour at the start of this work is a table with one row per dataset and one column saying which role you are playing, because the obligations differ and teams routinely apply the wrong set.

What counts as personal data

The definition is broad and short: any data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to such data. Digital personal data is what the Act governs, including data that started on paper and was later digitised.

Three consequences that catch people out.

There is no sensitive tier. The old SPDI Rules had a special category for passwords, financial details, health, biometrics and so on; Europe has its own special categories. DPDP has none. Health records and a mailing list are governed by the same words. That simplifies the law and complicates the engineering conversation, because you can no longer argue that a dataset is ordinary enough to deserve less care — and equally, you cannot pretend the marketing list is outside scope because it is only names and emails. It is not.

"Identifiable in relation to" is wider than a name. A device identifier tied to a profile, an order number that resolves to a person in your own system, a photograph, a location trail — all personal data. The test is whether the individual can be identified using it, including in combination with what you already hold. Genuinely anonymised data falls outside, but genuine anonymisation is much harder than dropping the name column, and a dataset that can be re-joined to an identity was never anonymised in the first place. Personally identifiable information as most engineers use the phrase is narrower than what this Act reaches.

It follows the person, not the server. The Act applies to processing of digital personal data within India, and to processing outside India where it is connected with offering goods or services to Data Principals in India. A company hosted anywhere selling to customers here is in scope. Meanwhile a narrow set of things sit outside: processing for a purely personal or domestic purpose, and personal data the individual herself made publicly available, or that someone was under a legal obligation to publish. That second exclusion is narrower than the scraping industry would like it to be, and I would not build a product on the assumption that anything visible on a public profile is fair game.

The six things consent has to be

Here is the operative sentence, and it repays reading slowly. Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, with a clear affirmative action, and it signifies agreement to processing for the specified purpose, limited to the personal data necessary for that purpose.

Six requirements, and each one kills a common pattern.

  • Free. Not extracted. If the service is withheld unless the person agrees to something the service does not need, the agreement is not freely given. The everyday version of this is the app that will not open until you accept analytics it does not require to function.
  • Specific. Tied to a named purpose. One tick cannot stand for marketing, analytics and sharing with a partner, because the person cannot agree to one and refuse the others. Bundling is the failure mode this word exists to prevent.
  • Informed. The notice comes first. Consent obtained before the person could have known what they were agreeing to is not informed, whatever they clicked.
  • Unconditional. You cannot attach riders. In practice this overlaps with "free" and reinforces it: consent to an optional purpose cannot be made the price of a service that works fine without it.
  • Unambiguous, with a clear affirmative action. An act, not the absence of one. A pre-ticked box is not an act. Silence is not an act. Continuing to scroll is not an act. The person has to do something whose only meaning is agreement.
  • Limited to what is necessary. Even a perfect tick only covers the data the stated purpose needs. The Act carries its own illustration of an app for one thing demanding access to something unrelated, and the excess is invalid regardless of what the person tapped.

Two further requirements sit alongside those six and are just as operative. Consent must be as easy to withdraw as it was to give — if it took one tap, withdrawal cannot take an email to support and a five-day wait. And once withdrawn, you stop processing, you cause your processors to stop, and the data is erased unless some other law requires you to keep it.

Then the requirement that turns all of this from a policy question into a database question: if there is a dispute, the burden of proving that notice was given and consent obtained is yours. Not the individual's. Operationally, no record means no consent. I have watched teams design an elegant consent screen and store the outcome as a single boolean column that gets overwritten every time it changes, which is a system that can tell you what a user thinks today and can prove nothing at all about last March.

Why "by using this site, you agree" is not consent

This sentence is on a very large number of Indian websites, and it does not do what its authors think. Test it against the six requirements and it fails at least three of them simultaneously.

It has no affirmative action — browsing is not an act whose meaning is agreement, it is an act whose meaning is browsing. It is not specific, because it points at a policy that covers every purpose the company has ever contemplated. And it is not informed in the sense the Act means, because a link is not a notice presented before the request. Then ask the evidential question: if the Board asks you to produce the consent record for a particular individual, what row do you show?

Two related patterns are better and still insufficient. The first is the single checkbox at sign-up reading "I accept the Privacy Policy and Terms of Service" — an affirmative action, at least, but one tick standing in for every purpose in a long document, which is precisely the bundling that "specific" excludes. The second is the cookie banner with a large accept button and a small, slow, three-click path to refusing. That one has a separate problem: India's consumer regulator has published guidelines naming a list of prohibited dark patterns, and an interface engineered so that refusing is materially harder than accepting is exactly the shape they describe. Two regimes, one banner.

The instinct is to make the consent wall bigger. The correct move is to make the purpose list shorter. Every purpose you drop is a notice line you never have to write, a tick you never have to collect, a retention rule you never have to enforce and an access request you never have to answer. Compliance cost scales with what you decided to do with the data, not with the size of the banner you put in front of it.

The notice, and its languages

The notice is a separate obligation from the consent itself, and it has to come before or with the request. At minimum it must tell the person three things: what personal data you want and the purpose you want it for; how to exercise the right to withdraw consent and the right to grievance redressal; and how to make a complaint to the Data Protection Board.

The Rules put shape on that. The notice has to stand on its own rather than being buried inside other text, it has to be in clear and plain language, and the data and the purposes both have to be itemised rather than described in the abstract. "We collect information about you to improve our services" satisfies none of that. "We collect your name, email address and mobile number, in order to create your account, send you order updates, and — if you tick the third box — send you offers" satisfies most of it.

Then the requirement that reliably surprises people: the individual must have the option of that notice in English or in any language specified in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution. That is twenty-two languages — Assamese, Bengali, Bodo, Dogri, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Konkani, Maithili, Malayalam, Manipuri, Marathi, Nepali, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Santali, Sindhi, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu — plus English.

Read as an engineering requirement rather than a legal one, that changes the artefact. Your notice is no longer a paragraph in your terms; it is a piece of content with twenty-three versions, a version history, and a translation workflow. Which leads to the most useful practical consequence in this entire article: the language requirement is a brevity requirement. A 200-word notice can be translated accurately and affordably. A 4,000-word privacy policy cannot, and machine-translating a legal notice into a language nobody on your team can read back is a risk you are taking silently. Write the short notice first. Keep the long policy as the reference document it always was.

One more thing that falls out of this: your consent record needs to store which version of the notice, in which language, the person was shown. That is the notice you actually gave. The privacy notice on this site is deliberately short for the same reason — a notice nobody finishes reading has failed at being informative, whatever it satisfies on paper.

Legitimate uses: where consent is not needed

The Act sets out a closed list of processing that does not need consent. Here are the ones a private company might actually touch, in plain terms.

  • Data the individual voluntarily provided for that specified purpose, having not indicated that she does not consent to its use for it. This is the everyday ground and it is narrower than it first looks. It covers the address someone typed into your delivery field being used to deliver the thing. It does not cover using that address for anything else afterwards.
  • Employment purposes, and processing to safeguard the employer from loss or liability — the Act names prevention of corporate espionage, protecting trade secrets, intellectual property and classified information, and providing a service or benefit the employee has asked for. Payroll and benefits live here. Open-ended employee monitoring is a much poorer fit, and I would not stretch this ground over it.
  • Medical emergency involving a threat to life or an immediate threat to health; measures during an epidemic or other threat to public health; and measures to ensure safety or provide assistance during a disaster or a breakdown of public order.
  • Legal compulsion — complying with an obligation to disclose information to the State, and complying with a judgment, decree or order.
  • The State's own processing for subsidies, benefits, services, certificates, licences and permits, which is not a route available to a private business at all.

Now the absences, because they matter more than the list. There is no legitimate-interests ground. There is no contract-performance ground. There is no general fraud-prevention or security-of-the-service ground for a private company. If you find yourself reasoning "we obviously need this data, so there must be a basis for it", stop — that reasoning works in Europe and does not work here. The honest summary for a small private company is that you have roughly two usable grounds, both narrow, and consent for everything else.

What a Consent Manager is

This is the piece of the regime with no European equivalent, and the one most people have not heard of. A Consent Manager is a registered entity through which a Data Principal can give, manage, review and withdraw her consent — a single place a person can visit to see who holds what about her and switch things off, with an interoperable platform behind it. The Act is explicit that the Consent Manager is accountable to the Data Principal and acts on her behalf, not on yours.

The conditions for becoming one sit in the Rules and they are substantial: an Indian company, a stated minimum net worth, a certified interoperable platform, obligations about maintaining consent records, and constraints on what the Consent Manager may itself see. This is infrastructure, and it is intended to be built by a small number of registered entities rather than by everyone.

So what does it mean for a small business? Three honest things.

  1. You almost certainly will not be one. Reading the conditions and concluding they are not for you takes ten minutes and is the correct outcome for nearly everyone.
  2. You may have to work with one. Consent that arrives through a Consent Manager is consent, and a withdrawal that arrives through one is a withdrawal. That is an integration point, and integrations are enormously cheaper designed than retrofitted — which is the strongest practical argument for keeping consent in one service inside your own system, with an interface, rather than scattered across a flag in the users table, a tag in your mailing tool and a field in the CRM.
  3. This is the part most likely to change before it binds. Consent Manager registration sits early in the published rollout, ahead of the substantive obligations, so the ecosystem may well exist before your own duties commence. Watching who registers is a cheap way to learn what the interface is going to look like in practice.

The rights you have to be able to serve

These are usually presented as a list to know. They are better understood as a list to build, because each one arrives as an email from somebody who does not know your internal vocabulary and expects an answer within a defined period.

  • Access. A summary of the personal data you are processing about her and the processing activities involved, plus the identities of the other Fiduciaries and Processors you have shared it with and a description of what was shared. There is a carve-out where the sharing was for the prevention, detection or investigation of offences.
  • Correction, completion and updating. Straightforward in principle, awkward in systems that copy a name into six places at sign-up and never reconcile them again.
  • Erasure. On request, unless retaining the data is necessary for the specified purpose or for compliance with a law. Note the shape of that: retention needs a reason you can name, and "we might want it later" is not one.
  • Grievance redressal. A readily available means of registering a grievance with you or with a Consent Manager, and a response within the prescribed period. This right has an underappreciated commercial value: the individual must exhaust it before approaching the Board, which makes your grievance mechanism the thing standing between an annoyed customer and a regulator.
  • Nomination. The right to nominate another individual to exercise these rights on her death or incapacity. This one is genuinely unusual internationally, and almost nobody has built it. It is a field, a verification process for the nominee, and a decision about what evidence you will accept.

Two engineering notes that matter more than the legal text. First, the access right quietly forces a data map: you cannot summarise what you hold if you do not know where it is, and every team that attempts this discovers a spreadsheet, a support inbox and an analytics tool that were on nobody's list. That is the same phenomenon as the forgotten environment in what a security review actually finds — the assets nobody mentions are the ones that cause the trouble.

Second, and less obvious: a rights request is an exfiltration channel if you answer it without verifying who is asking. Somebody emails from an address that looks like a customer's and asks for a summary of everything you hold, and an eager support agent sends it. The verification problem here is the same one as account recovery, with the same failure modes, and it is worth reading alongside what actually protects an account. Decide in advance what proof you require, write it down, and make it the same for everyone so that a confident, urgent requester cannot talk their way past it.

Children's data and verifiable parental consent

A child, for this Act, is anyone under eighteen. That is higher than most comparable regimes, and the practical consequence is immediate: "our users are adults" is an assumption rather than a fact unless you have done something to establish it.

Before processing a child's personal data — or that of a person with a disability who has a lawful guardian — you must obtain the verifiable consent of the parent or lawful guardian. The Rules describe what verifiable means in practice: technical and organisational measures to check that the person consenting is an identifiable adult, using identity and age details you already reliably hold about them, or a virtual token mapped to such details and issued by an entitled entity such as a digital locker service. Notice what that rules out. A checkbox saying "I confirm I am the parent" is not a measure. It is a sentence.

Three things are prohibited outright, and consent does not cure them: processing likely to have a detrimental effect on the wellbeing of a child; tracking or behavioural monitoring of children; and targeted advertising directed at children. The Rules do carve out classes and purposes — healthcare professionals, educational institutions, some narrow operational purposes such as confirming that a user is not in fact a child — and the Act allows the Government to lower the age threshold for classes of fiduciary where processing is verifiably safe.

The honest engineering position is that age assurance is a genuinely unsolved problem and every available method is either weak or invasive. A date-of-birth field is theatre. Demanding an identity document means collecting more personal data in order to protect personal data, which is a trade with a real cost. So this is one of the few places in this article where my advice to a small company is to reduce the exposure rather than solve the problem: if your product is not for under-eighteens, say so plainly, design sign-up so you are not knowingly accepting them, and do not build features whose economics depend on engagement from users you have not aged.

Significant Data Fiduciaries

A Significant Data Fiduciary is not a size band you cross automatically. The Central Government notifies a Fiduciary, or a class of them, on stated factors: the volume and sensitivity of the personal data processed, the risk to the rights of Data Principals, the potential impact on the sovereignty and integrity of India, the risk to electoral democracy, the security of the State and public order.

Being notified brings additional obligations: a Data Protection Officer based in India, responsible to the board of directors and serving as the contact point for grievance redressal; an independent data auditor to carry out a data audit; and periodic Data Protection Impact Assessments. The Rules add annual assessment and audit with significant observations reported to the Board, due diligence to verify that algorithmic software deployed for processing does not pose a risk to Data Principals' rights, and restrictions on transferring specified categories of personal data outside India.

Most small companies will never be notified, so why read the list? Two reasons. If you sell to a company that is notified, their obligations arrive at your door as contract clauses and audit questions — which is the same dynamic described in security compliance, explained, where the framework binding your customer becomes the requirement binding you. And the SDF duties are a fair description of what mature looks like: a named accountable owner, a periodic assessment, and an independent pair of eyes. Adopting that shape voluntarily while you are small costs very little and is far cheaper than retrofitting it later under a deadline.

Breach notification

On becoming aware of a personal data breach, a Data Fiduciary must intimate the Board and each affected Data Principal, in the form and manner prescribed. Two features of that make it harsher than teams expect.

There is no severity threshold in the words. Europe lets a controller skip notifying individuals where a risk to their rights is unlikely. DPDP as drafted does not hand you that judgment call, which means the question "is this big enough to report" has a different and less comfortable answer here.

The clock starts on awareness, not on confirmation. You do not get to finish the investigation and then start counting. The investigation and the notification run in parallel, which is a process design problem more than a legal one — your first notice has to be honest about what you do not yet know.

The Rules set out the shape. To affected individuals, without delay, in clear and plain language: the nature and extent of the breach, when it happened, its likely consequences for them, the mitigation you have carried out, what they can do to protect themselves, and who to contact. To the Board, an initial intimation without delay, followed by a detailed report within seventy-two hours — extendable only if the Board allows it on request — covering the facts and circumstances, the reason it happened, the mitigation, your findings about who caused it, the remedial measures, and a copy of what you told the affected people.

That third clock is the one Indian companies keep missing. CERT-In's 2022 direction already requires a wide class of cyber incidents to be reported within six hours, under the IT Act, today — not from whenever the DPDP obligations commence. The two regimes are not alternatives and neither replaces the other; the full stack is laid out in India's IT law, explained. An incident that involves personal data can easily owe a six-hour report, an immediate intimation to affected individuals, and a seventy-two-hour detailed filing, all from the same Tuesday morning.

The deliverable here is not a policy document. It is a one-page runbook that names who is allowed to declare a breach, who drafts the individual notice, who files with the regulator, where the logs live and who signs. Then rehearse it once, badly, for an hour, with the people who would actually be woken up. The gap between a team that has done that and a team that has a policy is the difference between a controlled disclosure and a scramble — which is the same argument as what an auditor actually wants from security training: evidence that people did the thing, not evidence that a document exists.

Retention limits and erasure

Two erasure triggers sit in the Act, and the second is the one that changes how systems get built. You erase when consent is withdrawn. And you erase as soon as it is reasonable to assume that the specified purpose is no longer being served, unless retention is necessary to comply with a law.

That inverts the default most companies operate on, which is: keep everything, because storage is cheap and somebody might want it one day. Under DPDP, holding data past its purpose is not thrift — it is a standing obligation you have failed, and an amount of data you have to protect, disclose on an access request and account for after an incident.

The Rules add a specific version for certain notified classes — large e-commerce platforms, online gaming intermediaries and social media intermediaries above stated user thresholds — requiring erasure after a defined period of user inactivity, with advance notice to the user before the data goes. Most small companies sit well below those thresholds. The direction of travel is the point.

What to build:

  • A retention schedule with a reason per line. One row per dataset, a period, and the reason for that period — usually a tax rule, a statutory limitation period, a contractual term, or a plain statement that the purpose ends when the account closes. A schedule without reasons is a wish list.
  • Deletion as a job that runs, not a promise in a policy. If nothing on a schedule ever deletes anything, the schedule is a document about a system that does not exist.
  • An honest answer about backups. A record deleted from the live database and alive in a nightly snapshot for two years is a record you still hold. Nobody sensibly expects surgical deletion from every backup; what is defensible is a documented rotation with a stated maximum age, and a rule that anything restored gets re-scrubbed against the deletion log before it goes back into service. Say that out loud in the schedule rather than leaving a gap.
  • The log tension, named. Security logs are themselves a retention requirement under the Rules and are how you would ever reconstruct an incident, so they are a lawful reason to keep some personal data past the purpose that generated it. That is fine. It just has to be a line in the schedule with a period and a reason, rather than an unexamined default of infinity.

The vendor contracts you need

The Act's requirement here is one sentence long: a Data Fiduciary may engage a Data Processor only under a valid contract. That brevity is deceptive, because the Fiduciary stays answerable for the Processor's failures — so the contract is the only place your exposure gets managed.

What that contract has to actually do, in the order I look for it:

  • Scope, stated exactly. Which data, which processing, which purposes, and a bar on anything else — including the vendor using your data to improve its own models or products, which is worth naming explicitly now rather than discovering later.
  • Processing on instruction only, with a route for the vendor to flag an instruction that looks unlawful rather than silently executing it.
  • Security safeguards passed through, so the vendor is bound to at least what you are bound to. The Rules require you to secure personal data with measures including encryption or masking, access control, monitoring, logs and backups; the vendor holding a copy needs the same floor.
  • Breach notification fast enough to be useful. This is the clause people get wrong most often. If your vendor has seventy-two hours to tell you, you have already missed your own deadline. Ask for immediate notification, put a number on it in hours, and require enough detail to draft your own notice rather than a reassuring paragraph.
  • Sub-processors named or notified, with the same terms flowed down. Most modern vendors are a stack of four other vendors, and the one holding your data may be a company you have never heard of.
  • Assistance with rights requests. An access or erasure request will reach data sitting in their system, and you need a contractual route to reach it within your response window.
  • Deletion or return at the end, on a stated timeline, backups included, with written confirmation.
  • Evidence rights proportionate to the risk. For a small buyer that usually means a report and answers to a questionnaire rather than a right to walk into a data centre. Ask for what you would actually use.

And the part nobody enjoys: your processors include the ones nobody procured. The analytics script somebody added to test something. The chat widget on the pricing page. The email tool on a free tier. The spreadsheet of leads in a personal drive. Each of those is a Processor if it holds personal data on your behalf, and none of them went through legal. The inventory is the hard part here; the contract is comparatively easy once you know who to send it to.

The penalty structure

The Schedule to the Act sets civil penalties, imposed by the Data Protection Board after an inquiry. The headline figures:

The penalty schedule. These are ceilings the Board may impose, not tariffs it must — and the largest one is about security, not about paperwork.
Failure Up to What it is really about
Not taking reasonable security safeguards ₹250 crore Protecting the data you hold, by any means that work
Not notifying a breach to the Board or to affected individuals ₹200 crore Telling people promptly, even while you still do not know everything
Failing the children's-data obligations ₹200 crore Verifiable parental consent, and no tracking or ads aimed at children
Failing the additional Significant Data Fiduciary duties ₹150 crore The DPO, the audit, the impact assessment
Breach of any other provision or rule ₹50 crore Where notice and consent failures sit
A Data Principal breaching her own duties ₹10,000 Impersonation, false particulars, frivolous grievances

Four things to read correctly, because the crore figures are quoted far more often than they are understood.

They are ceilings, not tariffs. The Act tells the Board what to weigh before arriving at a number: the nature, gravity and duration of the breach, the type of personal data affected, whether the conduct was repetitive, any gain made or loss avoided, whether mitigating action was taken and how promptly and effectively, the proportionality of the penalty, and its likely impact. Fast, honest, well-evidenced mitigation is written into the statute as a reason to reduce the number. That is a direct commercial argument for the breach runbook above.

The biggest number is attached to security, not to paperwork. Look at the ordering: failing to take reasonable safeguards sits at five times the band that notice and consent failures fall into. That is the legislature saying, in the only language a schedule speaks, that protecting the data matters more than documenting the collection of it. A company that spends its whole budget on a consent banner and none on access control has optimised for the cheapest row in the table.

There is no imprisonment, and no individual compensation route. DPDP creates civil penalties payable to the government, not damages payable to the person harmed — which is a real change from Section 43A of the IT Act, the provision DPDP repeals, and one that has been criticised for exactly that reason. It does not mean an aggrieved individual has no remedy at all. It means the remedy is not in this Act.

Voluntary undertakings exist. The Board can accept an undertaking from a company under inquiry, and proceedings in respect of that breach stop; breaching the undertaking re-opens the exposure. It is worth knowing that lever is in the statute before you ever need it.

What a compliant notice looks like

Enough abstraction. Here is the shape, annotated, and then what has to end up in your database afterwards.

Note what the example does not do. It does not ask you to accept a policy. It does not bundle three purposes into one tick. It does not pre-tick anything. It does not hide withdrawal behind a support request. And it names exactly three fields, which is a constraint that forces a real conversation internally about the fourth one somebody wanted to add.

What has to survive in your system afterwards is a consent record, and the design of it matters more than the screen. Each record should carry:

  • the identifier and language of the notice version that was shown;
  • the specific purposes agreed to, one row each, rather than a single combined value;
  • the timestamp, and what the person actually did — which control, on which screen;
  • how you identified the person, and if consent came via a Consent Manager, which one;
  • and any withdrawal, written as a new row rather than as an edit of the old one.

That last point is the one I would fight for in a design review. Model consent as an append-only log, not as a mutable flag. The question you will be asked is never "does this user consent to marketing today" — it is "what did this user agree to, when, against which notice, and when did that change". A boolean column cannot answer it, and by the time you need the answer the history is gone. For the same reason, do not delete the consent record when consent is withdrawn: the record is your evidence that you had consent and stopped when you were told to.

And make one service own the answer. Consent expressed as a flag in the users table, a tag in the mailing tool and a field in the CRM is three answers that will disagree within a year, and the one that ends up in front of a regulator will be the wrong one.

What is settled, what is still moving

This section will age faster than the rest of the article, which is exactly why it is here rather than implied.

Settled. The Act itself, passed in August 2023. The definitions, the two lawful routes, the six-part consent standard, the notice contents, the rights, the concept of a Significant Data Fiduciary, and the penalty schedule are all in primary legislation. Those words are not going to change quietly.

Still moving. Nearly everything operational. The Act sat largely un-operational until the DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025, and those Rules commence in phases rather than all at once: the Board and its machinery first; Consent Manager registration roughly a year on; and the substantive obligations — notice, consent, the rights, breach notification, the Significant Data Fiduciary duties — roughly eighteen months on, at which point Section 43A of the IT Act and the old SPDI Rules stand repealed. The specific forms, thresholds, response periods and figures live in the Rules and the Schedules, and those are the details most likely to have shifted between my writing this and your reading it.

What that means today. As at 31 August 2026, most Indian companies are in a transition window. SPDI is still technically the data-protection regime you are judged under. CERT-In's directions bind now. DPDP is a deadline on the calendar rather than a live obligation for most of its substance. The wrong conclusion to draw from that is that there is time. The parts of this work that take months — knowing what personal data you hold, where it lives, who you share it with, and being able to delete it on request — are the parts that have to happen first, and they are worth doing under any regime, including none.

Check the current text before you act on any number in this article. The Act is on India Code and the Rules are published by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, and both are short enough to read yourself. And to say it once more in the place a reader needs it: this is not legal advice, and if your exposure is real — a lot of personal data, a regulated sector, children, an incident already behind you — get advice from somebody who can put their name to it.

Where a small company should start

In order, because the order is most of the value. Everything below is doable by a small team without a consultant, and doing the first three properly makes the rest much cheaper.

  1. One page: what personal data you hold, where it lives, who you share it with, and why. An afternoon of honest listing, including the spreadsheets and the free-tier tools. This is the input to every other item and the thing nobody has.
  2. Decide your role per dataset. Fiduciary or Processor, one column, no ambiguity.
  3. Cut purposes. Go through the list and delete the ones nobody can defend in a sentence. This is the single highest-leverage step, because compliance cost is a function of purposes, not of revenue.
  4. Write the short notice — itemised data, itemised purposes, withdrawal, grievance, the Board. Then try translating it, and let that exercise tell you whether it is short enough.
  5. Build consent as an append-only record, owned by one service.
  6. Wire the rights. One inbox, one named owner, a documented response path, and a written identity check that is the same for everybody.
  7. Write the retention schedule, with a real reason per period, and make the deletion job actually run.
  8. Fix the contracts with the vendors that matter, starting with whoever holds the most personal data rather than whoever is easiest to email.
  9. Write the breach runbook and rehearse it once. An hour, with real names in it.
  10. Then worry about the banner. Last, deliberately.

Notice that security work sits above consent work in that order, and that is not an accident of my background. It is what the penalty schedule says, it is what an incident does to a small company, and it is the part that stays useful no matter how the Rules land.

Start with what you actually hold. Before any of the drafting, it helps to have a clear picture of your assets, who would want them, and where the trust boundaries in your system are. Questions people ask

Do we still need consent from a customer who is already paying us?

For most of what you want to do with their data, yes. This is the difference from Europe that catches people: DPDP has no lawful basis of processing necessary for the performance of a contract, and no legitimate-interests ground either. The nearest thing is the legitimate use for data the individual voluntarily provided for a specified purpose and has not indicated she does not consent to — which fairly covers using the address she typed into the delivery field in order to deliver the thing. It does not stretch to marketing her later, profiling her, or handing her number to a partner. Paying you is not consent to anything beyond the purpose the payment was for.

Does DPDP apply to a company as small as ours?

Yes. There is no revenue threshold, no headcount threshold and no small-business carve-out in the Act. If you process digital personal data in India, or you process it anywhere in connection with offering goods or services to people in India, you are in scope. The Act does let the Central Government exempt notified classes of startups from a few specific provisions by notification, which is narrower than it sounds and is not something to plan around. What genuinely reduces a small company's obligations is not size — it is holding less data for fewer purposes.

Does it cover our employees, or only our customers?

Both. Your employees are Data Principals and you are their Data Fiduciary, which surprises teams that thought of the Act as a customer-facing thing. The relief is that employment is one of the enumerated legitimate uses, so payroll, benefits, and measures to safeguard the employer from loss or liability — protecting trade secrets and confidential information, for instance — do not need consent. That ground is narrower than the whole HR relationship, though. It is a poor fit for open-ended employee monitoring, and it says nothing about how long you keep the file of someone who left four years ago.

We already comply with GDPR. Are we finished?

You are much closer than a company starting cold, and you can reuse the data map, the retention schedule, the breach runbook and most of the vendor paperwork. Four differences will still cost you work. Your lawful bases collapse from six to two, so anything you run on legitimate interests or contract necessity has to be re-based on consent. A child is anyone under 18 rather than under 16 or 13. Your notice has to be available in English or any of the 22 Eighth Schedule languages. And there is no sensitive-data tier, so the extra protection you built for health or financial data is good practice rather than a separate legal regime. Treat GDPR work as a head start, not as an equivalence.

Do we have to keep Indian personal data inside India?

Not as a general rule under DPDP itself. The Act takes the opposite approach to Europe: rather than permitting transfers only to approved countries, it lets the Central Government restrict transfers to countries it names. So the default is that transfer is allowed until a restriction exists. Two caveats matter more than that default. Sectoral rules already localise plenty of data — the Reserve Bank's payment-data directive is the one most fintechs meet — and DPDP expressly does not lower any stricter requirement in another law. And a fiduciary notified as significant can be restricted from moving specified categories of personal data out of the country at all.

If we get consent wrong, is a crore-scale penalty the first thing that happens?

No. The figures in the Schedule are ceilings, and the Act tells the Data Protection Board what to weigh before it lands on a number: the nature, gravity and duration of the breach, the type of personal data affected, whether the conduct was repetitive, any gain made or loss avoided, and whether mitigating action was taken and how promptly and effectively. Fast, honest, well-evidenced mitigation is written into the statute as a reason to reduce the penalty. It is also worth knowing that DPDP carries no imprisonment and gives an individual no statutory right to claim compensation from you, which is a real change from Section 43A of the IT Act that it repeals.

When do we actually have to be ready?

Later than the headlines suggest and sooner than is comfortable. The Act passed in 2023 but sat largely un-operational until the Rules were notified in November 2025, and those Rules commence in phases: the Board first, Consent Manager registration around a year on, and the substantive obligations — notice, consent, rights, breach notification, Significant Data Fiduciary duties — roughly eighteen months on, which is when the old Section 43A and the SPDI Rules also stand repealed. Dates in a phased rollout move, so check the current position rather than trusting this paragraph. What should not wait is knowing what personal data you hold, where it lives and who you share it with, because that work takes months, is the input to everything else, and is worth doing under any regime.

The word the Act chose is fiduciary, and I think that is the sentence worth keeping. A fiduciary is not someone who has filed the right form. It is someone who holds a thing that belongs to somebody else and is answerable for how they hold it. Most of the work in this article — the inventory, the short notice, the record you can produce, the deletion that actually runs — is what that word looks like when it is implemented rather than quoted.
