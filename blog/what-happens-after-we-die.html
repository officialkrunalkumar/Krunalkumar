What happens after we die

This is the one question every tradition has taken a position on and none has settled. I am not going to pretend to an answer. What I can do is set out honestly what the main views actually claim — including where they contradict each other in ways that usually get glossed over — and what the evidence does and does not support.

Why the question will not go away

Every human culture we know of has developed elaborate beliefs and rituals around death. That universality is sometimes offered as evidence that something is there, and sometimes as evidence that we are simply built to find the alternative unbearable. Both readings are available and neither is decisive.

What is certain is that the question is not idle. How you answer it shapes how you spend a life — what you fear, what you are willing to endure, what you consider worth doing. It deserves better than either confident dismissal or confident assertion.

What happens to the body

This part is not disputed. Circulation stops, oxygen delivery to tissue ends, and cells begin to fail — neurons within minutes, other tissues over hours. Electrical activity in the brain ceases. Decomposition begins.

The disagreement is entirely about whether that is a description of the whole event or only of its physical component. Everything below turns on that single question.

The materialist answer

On this view, consciousness is something the brain does, in the way digestion is something the stomach does. When the organ stops, the process stops. There is no one left to experience absence, so there is nothing it is like to be dead.

The supporting evidence is real and cumulative: damage to specific brain regions reliably alters specific aspects of personality, memory and perception. Anaesthesia interrupts experience entirely. Nothing we have found so far requires anything beyond the physical to explain.

The honest gap is that we do not have an account of why physical processes produce subjective experience at all — the "hard problem". That is a genuine open question in philosophy of mind. It is not, by itself, evidence for survival, but anyone claiming the matter is fully closed is overstating.

Rebirth: two views that are not the same

These get flattened together constantly, including in India where both are ambient. They differ on the point that matters most.

The Hindu view (broadly, across schools that vary considerably) holds that there is an ātman — a self or soul that is genuinely you — which persists and takes a new body. Karma shapes the circumstances of the next life. The goal is moksha: release from the cycle altogether.

The Buddhist view accepts rebirth but denies the permanent self. Anattā — non-self — says there is no unchanging essence to transmigrate. What continues is a causal process, not a passenger. The traditional image is a flame passing from one candle to another: the second flame is neither the same flame nor a different one.

That is a real philosophical disagreement, not a difference of vocabulary. One says something of you survives. The other says the thing you take yourself to be was never a fixed entity in the first place — which is simultaneously the harder claim and, to some, the more interesting one. The historical account of where that teaching came from is its own story.

The Abrahamic views

Judaism, Christianity and Islam share a broad shape — a single life, followed by judgement and a continued existence — while differing substantially in the detail, and internally as well.

Judaism has historically been the least specific of the three, with the emphasis on conduct in this life and a range of views on olam ha-ba, the world to come. Christianity centres on resurrection and on the soul's continuation, with significant differences between traditions about what happens between death and resurrection. Islam holds to barzakh, an intermediate state, followed by resurrection and judgement, with paradise and hell described in considerable detail.

Worth noting: the common Western picture of a disembodied soul floating away owes as much to Greek philosophy as to any of these scriptures. The older Abrahamic emphasis is on bodily resurrection, which is a different claim.

Epicurus's argument, and what it does and doesn't reach

Epicurus offered the sharpest philosophical response to the fear itself: death is nothing to us, because while we exist death is not present, and when death is present we do not exist. There is no moment at which we encounter it.

The related symmetry argument runs: you were not troubled by the eternity before your birth, so why be troubled by the eternity after your death? The two absences are the same shape.

It is genuinely useful against the fear of being dead. It works less well on the two fears people usually actually have — the process of dying, which can involve pain and indignity, and the loss of the people and projects you care about. Those are not answered by the symmetry, because they are not fears about non-existence at all.

What near-death experiences show, and what they do not

The reports are consistent enough to be a real phenomenon requiring explanation: tunnels, light, a sense of peace, life review, sometimes an out-of-body perspective. Across cultures the core features recur, though the figures encountered tend to match the person's own tradition.

What they establish is that the dying or severely stressed brain reliably produces a particular class of experience. What they cannot establish, on the evidence we have, is that the experience continues after brain activity fully ceases — because by definition every account comes from someone whose brain resumed.

Careful prospective studies looking for verifiable perception during cardiac arrest have not produced clear positive results. That is not proof of a negative, and it is not nothing either. Treat confident claims in both directions with suspicion.

"We do not know" is an honest position, and it is not the same as "nobody can say anything." A great deal is known about what happens. What is unresolved is what, if anything, it is like.

Living with an open question

Whatever is or is not on the other side, one thing is common to every view above: this life is the part you have access to. The materialist concludes it is all there is, and therefore precious. The Buddhist concludes that clinging to permanence is the source of the suffering. The theist concludes that it is the part being weighed. Different arguments, the same practical instruction — pay attention to it.

I would add one thing I have come to believe from watching people face this rather than from any doctrine: people rarely regret the work they did not finish. They regret the things they did not say, to people who were still there to hear them. That is actionable regardless of which of the above turns out to be right.
