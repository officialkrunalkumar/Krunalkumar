The practical half of the same question

I wrote a reflective piece about what happens after we die — the question every tradition has answered and none has settled. This is its practical companion, and the opposite kind of writing. Nothing here is unresolved. It is all admin, and it lands on someone who is grieving.

The core problem fits in a sentence. Your accounts are secured against exactly the people who will need them. Every measure you took to keep strangers out works identically well against your family, because the system cannot tell the difference — and that indifference is the point of the design, not a bug in it.

The classic version is the phone. Two-factor codes arrive on a handset, the handset needs the SIM, and cancelling the mobile number is one of the first tidy administrative jobs a family does. The moment it is deactivated, every account whose recovery runs through SMS becomes unrecoverable, and nobody in the room realises what they have just done. The fragility that makes a number attackable — see how a SIM swap works — makes it a poor inheritance path. It is a bearer token held by an operator, not by you.

There is no clean answer. A dormant line costs money and operators eventually recycle released numbers. The narrow advice: do not cancel it in the first week, and move what matters off SMS long before any of this is relevant.

What the platforms actually give a survivor

Several large providers offer something you can set up in advance. The shared shape matters: almost none of them hand over the account. They hand over a copy of selected data, under conditions, to a person you named while you were alive.

Google Inactive Account Manager

You choose how long the account has to sit unused before Google concludes something is wrong. It tries to reach you first. If it cannot, it notifies the people you nominated, shares the parts of your data you selected, and deletes the account afterwards if you asked it to. A data handover on a dead-man switch. Not a login.

Apple Legacy Contact

You nominate one or more people and each receives an access key. That key plus a death certificate lets them request access to data in your Apple Account. The limits are the interesting part: it excludes keychain items — saved passwords and payment details — and licensed media does not transfer. The one category that would unlock everything else is the one left out. Defensible as security; it makes the tool smaller than people assume.

Facebook legacy contact and memorialisation

An account can be memorialised — frozen, and marked as belonging to someone who has died — or deleted outright if you asked for that in advance. A legacy contact can pin a post, respond to friend requests and download what you shared if you enabled it. They cannot sign in as you or read your messages. Instagram memorialises accounts but has no contact you can nominate, as far as I am aware.

Microsoft

Microsoft is the outlier: I am not aware of anything you can configure in advance. Next of kin go through a formal request process, the outcome varies by jurisdiction, and an unused account is eventually closed anyway.

Check before you rely on any of this. I have described these tools as I understand them at the time of writing, and platform policies change without announcement. None work retrospectively: a setting you did not configure while alive and well is not available afterwards.

The password manager is the highest-value hour

If you do one thing on this page, do this one. Most password managers offer emergency access: you nominate a trusted person, they submit a request, a waiting period runs during which you can decline it, and if you never decline, they are let in. The exact shape differs by product — some grant vault access directly, some work through a family organiser — so read your own provider’s documentation rather than mine.

It is the highest-value item because it is the only mechanism here that produces genuine access rather than a curated export, and because every other recovery path in your life is already inside that vault.

The limits, because they are real:

  • It only covers what you actually saved. The accounts people forget to add are usually the old ones with money in them.
  • It depends on the person you named still having an account with the same provider, and on that provider still existing.
  • It is a genuine transfer of risk. From the moment you set it up that person can start a request, and if you are ill or not reading your email, the wait elapses and they are in. Choose someone you would give a key to your house.

I would rather say that out loud than pretend the feature is free. It is a trade, and for most people the right one.

Two-factor and recovery, specifically

Two-factor authentication is where good security turns into a locked door with the key inside it.

Authenticator apps. A TOTP code is not sent to you. It is computed from a shared secret and the clock, and that secret — the seed — is effectively the account. Seeds that live only in an app on a handset nobody can unlock go with the handset. Seeds kept in a synced authenticator or your password vault inherit that vault’s access path, which is the practical argument for putting them there despite the purist objection that it merges your two factors.

If the seed still feels abstract, watch one work. The TOTP lab derives a live code from a secret in front of you — counter, HMAC, truncation — inside your own tab. Once you can see the six digits are only a function of the seed and the clock, it is obvious what has to survive you.

Open the TOTP lab

Printed backup codes. Every serious provider will issue a sheet of single-use recovery codes. They need no device, no network, no battery and no living account, which is why a printed sheet in a sealed envelope still beats every more modern option for this one purpose. Keep it where the will is kept, write on the outside what it is and when you printed it, and reprint after any account change.

Treat that envelope as a key, not a document. Backup codes are bearer tokens: whoever opens it is in, with no further check, no notification and no way to tell afterwards who used one. That is what makes them work, and why they belong in a safe rather than a filing drawer.

Hardware keys and passkeys. A security key cannot be copied — the private half never leaves the device, which is the whole reason it resists phishing. It also means one key with no documented second is a single point of failure no death certificate, lawyer or support ticket can undo. Register two keys on every account that matters and write down where the second lives. Synced passkeys inherit more easily because they ride inside a keychain or vault — but then they inherit that vault’s story, and Apple excludes keychain items from Legacy Contact. The passkey and WebAuthn explorer takes a real credential apart in the browser and shows why the private half cannot be exported; I have written separately on what actually protects an account.

What not to do

Three well-intentioned things that make it worse.

Do not write a master password into a will. In many jurisdictions a will admitted to probate becomes a public record anybody can inspect. Even where it does not, the timing is wrong: you will change that password several times before you next revise the will, and an estate holding a confidently wrong instruction is worse off than one holding none. A will is the place to say who should have access, never how.

Do not hand out credentials in advance. Sharing a password breaches most providers’ terms of service. More importantly, someone signing in as you after your death is authenticating with credentials that are not theirs — which, depending on where you are, has the same shape as an offence, and which nobody reading the logs can distinguish from an intruder doing precisely the same thing. For the Indian statutory framing, I have written about the IT Act and what followed it.

I am not predicting prosecutions. Families sign in to a dead relative’s email constantly and, as far as I know, almost nothing follows. The point is narrower: you can give the people you love a path that does not rely on nobody looking closely.

Do not leave a list of passwords in a drawer. It has every weakness of the sealed envelope and one more: it is stale the day after you write it, and goes on looking authoritative anyway.

The India layer, and why to ask a lawyer

I am not a lawyer. This is not legal advice, and it is the section where I most want you to get some.

Nomination exists in India on bank accounts, demat accounts and insurance policies, and it is where families discover that what they arranged is not what they thought. The mistake is rarely a missing nomination. It is the assumption that nomination decides who owns the money.

Broadly, and with exceptions that matter, a nominee is the person the institution is permitted to pay. That is a discharge mechanism for the bank or depository — a lawful way for them to hand the asset over and be finished — not a determination of ownership. Who is entitled to keep it is a question of succession, decided by the will where there is one and by personal law where there is not. Personal law here differs by religion, so two families with identical paperwork can reach different outcomes. Insurance carries a wrinkle of its own, where certain close relatives can be named in a way that gives them beneficial rights rather than a bare receipt.

I have stated the general principle as I understand it and gone no further: the exceptions are where disputes happen, and a blog post is no substitute for somebody who knows your family.

The part that is admin rather than law: check the nominations exist and the names on them are current. Ones made when an account was opened are frequently a marriage, a divorce or a name change out of date, and nobody sends a reminder.

When there is a business attached

If anything you run has a domain attached, there is a failure chain worth understanding. The registration lapses. DNS stops resolving. Mail on that domain stops being delivered. Every password reset link for every other account then goes to an address that no longer accepts mail. A missed renewal takes the email, and the email takes everything else.

This site is the example I am entitled to use: hand-written static HTML, no dependencies, deployed on Vercel, with the contact address in its footer on the domain itself. The code is the safe part — it is in Git, and anyone holding the repository could redeploy it. The risk sits in the registration and the DNS, the two things not in the repository at all.

What helps:

  • Register for several years at a time. Auto-renew looks like protection, but it depends on a payment card, and cards are cancelled when their holder dies. Prepaid years are the only part of this that keeps working with nobody doing anything.
  • Add a second contact to the registrar account, using an address that is not on the domain being registered. A recovery address inside the thing you are recovering is not a recovery address.
  • Write a one-page runbook. What runs where, which registrar, which host, which payment processor, and when each renews. No passwords — pointers only.
  • Know your own expiry date. The domain registration lookup reads the public RDAP record — expiry, nameservers, transfer and delete locks — from your own browser. In my experience most people have never run it on their own domain.

An afternoon’s checklist

None of this needs a specialist. In rough order of value:

  1. Turn on emergency access in your password manager and name someone.
  2. Set up Inactive Account Manager, an Apple Legacy Contact and a Facebook legacy contact, on whichever of those you use.
  3. Print fresh backup codes for your two or three most important accounts. Seal and date the envelope, and keep it where the will is kept.
  4. If you use a hardware key, register a second one and write down where both live.
  5. Move your most important accounts off SMS, so cancelling a phone line stays a small decision.
  6. Write one page in plain language: which email address is the root of everything, which vault holds the passwords, and where the envelope is. No passwords on that page.
  7. Check the nominations on your bank, demat and insurance accounts, and talk to a lawyer if there is anything substantial to pass on.
  8. If a domain is involved, note the registrar, check the expiry and add a second contact.
  9. Tell the person you named that you named them, and tell them where that page is.

The last one is the step people skip, and skipping it undoes the rest. A legacy contact who was never told they are one will not think to act, at exactly the moment nobody is thinking clearly. Say it out loud now, while it is an awkward conversation rather than an urgent one.
