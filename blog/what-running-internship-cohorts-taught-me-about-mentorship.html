Mentorship

What running an internship cohort is teaching me about mentorship

When I started mentoring, I assumed the hard part would be teaching. It wasn't. The hard part was everything around the teaching — picking the right people, designing work that actually matters, and learning when to step back. Most of these lessons come from years of mentoring interns and engineers; running my own cohort now is testing every one of them.

A crowd is not a cohort

The first thing I got wrong was thinking bigger was better. More applicants felt like success. But a mentorship group is not an audience — it is a working team, and teams have a size beyond which the magic disappears. Past a handful of people you stop mentoring individuals and start broadcasting to a room. Questions go unanswered, quieter people vanish, and the whole thing flattens into a lecture.

So I keep my free cohort deliberately small — two or three seats. Not because demand is low, but because attention is finite. I would rather change three careers than entertain thirty inboxes. Small enough that I remember what each person is stuck on without checking my notes; that is the right size.

Select for hunger, not résumé

Early on I over-weighted credentials — grades, the name of the college, a long list of courses. It is the easiest thing to sort on and almost the least useful. Across the interns and mentees I have mentored over the years — and now in my own cohort — the ones who grew fastest were rarely the ones with the shiniest CVs. They were the ones who showed up with a half-broken project they had built on their own, because that told me the one thing a transcript never can: they will do the work when nobody is watching.

Now I look for three signals — evidence they have built something unprompted, the ability to describe a problem clearly, and honesty about what they don't yet know. Someone who admits "I haven't learned this yet" and then tells me how they plan to learn it is worth more to me than any confident bluffer.

Give real work, not exercises

Nothing kills motivation like busywork that everyone knows is fake. A toy assignment that gets thrown away teaches people that their effort doesn't matter. So I hand interns slices of real problems — a genuine automation a client actually needs, a research question with no answer key, a bug in something that ships. The stakes are small and the safety net is real, but the work is not pretend.

Real work does something an exercise can't: it produces the messy, ambiguous, "the requirements just changed" moments that are the actual job. When an intern navigates one of those and ships something a real person uses, the confidence that follows is earned, and earned confidence sticks.

Mentorship is mostly restraint

The instinct when someone is stuck is to jump in and fix it. Resisting that is the whole skill. Every time I solve a problem for someone, I rob them of the struggle that would have taught them to solve the next one alone. The useful move is almost never the answer — it is the next question that unblocks their own thinking.

I try to follow a simple rule: let them get stuck, but never let them stay stuck alone. There is a window between productive struggle and demoralizing frustration, and a good mentor lives in that window — present enough that nobody drowns, absent enough that everybody learns to swim.

Why I still do it for free

I keep one track completely free and selective, and a separate open track that is paid. People ask why give anything away. The honest answer is that mentoring sharpened me as much as anyone I taught. Explaining a concept to someone who has never seen it exposes every gap in your own understanding. Reviewing a beginner's code reminds you why the "obvious" conventions exist. I got a researcher's habits and a builder's instincts partly by having to articulate them to someone else.

That is the real return. Not the fee — the fact that teaching is the fastest way I know to keep getting better myself.
