What your browser tells every site you visit

None of these values needed a permission prompt, and none of them identifies you on its own. Together they usually do, and unlike a cookie there is nothing for you to clear.

Read your own values first. There is a page on this site that performs every permissionless read described below and prints the answers in one table: your public address, browser string, time zone, core count, GPU and a canvas fingerprint. The rest of this article is easier to follow with your own numbers in front of you.

What arrives before any script runs

The first facts about you arrive before the page has begun thinking about you. They are part of the request itself.

Your IP address is the return address on the packets, so it is not optional and not a tracking decision. It says which network you are on — usually your internet provider, or your employer — and roughly where that network is. It does not say your name, your street, or which person in the building is typing. Geolocation databases map address ranges onto places, and in my experience they get the country right far more often than the city, though I have not measured that systematically. Many home connections rotate addresses, so an IP is a weak long-term identifier by itself.

User-Agent names your browser, its version, its engine and your operating system, and on a phone often the device. Accept-Language lists the languages you want the page in, which alone often narrows a country. Referer — misspelled in the standard since 1996 and never corrected — says which page sent you, though most browsers now send only the origin across sites. None of this involves JavaScript. Turning scripts off does not turn it off.

How a page learns your public address

Browsers ship WebRTC so video calls can connect two machines directly, which means each side must discover what address the other would dial — a question put to a public STUN server. A page can open a peer connection to nobody, let the browser gather those candidates, and read the addresses out. No prompt, because the API was designed for calling rather than for asking permission to be called. That is how VPN leak checkers work, and what the leak page does on load. It is also the one thing there that is not purely local: learning a public address means showing it to somebody, so Google’s and Cloudflare’s STUN servers see yours in the asking, and the page says so. Chrome and Firefox now hide your address on your own network behind a random .local name; the public one cannot be hidden.

What a page can read once a script runs

Now the page is running code, and code can ask questions. Each item below is an ordinary API call with no permission attached, and a row in the leak table:

  • Screen and window. Resolution, device pixel ratio, and the window you are reading in; screen size is stable, window size moves when you resize.
  • Time zone, from Intl.DateTimeFormat. Your rough location, with no location prompt and no address lookup.
  • Languages, platform and touch points — what you read, and phone, tablet or desktop.
  • CPU cores from navigator.hardwareConcurrency, and on Chromium device memory rounded coarsely. Both exist so pages can size their workloads, and both distinguish machines.
  • Preferences you never told the page — dark or light mode, and whether you asked your system to reduce motion, an accessibility setting any page can read.
  • The graphics stack. A WebGL extension called WEBGL_debug_renderer_info returns your GPU model as plain text. Not a category. The model.

Three more usually appear on lists like this and the leak page does not read them, rather than let you hunt for missing rows. Installed fonts can be probed by rendering a string in each candidate font and measuring the width, though Safari and Firefox restrict this to system fonts. Battery level came from the Battery Status API, which Chromium still ships, Firefox removed and Safari never implemented. Connection type and a bandwidth estimate come from navigator.connection, a Chromium feature. They exist; I did not measure them for you.

None of it identifies you. The combination usually does.

Take any single row alone and it is harmless. Millions of people run a 1920 × 1080 screen, share your time zone, or have eight cores.

The mechanism is that the answers combine. If a value splits the population roughly in half, knowing it is worth one bit, and bits add. Thirty-three bits of genuinely independent information gets you to about one person on Earth, because two to the power thirty-three is roughly 8.6 billion. That is arithmetic, not a measurement of you: real attributes are correlated, so they are worth less than face value, and how much less depends on the population measured.

Every fingerprinting statistic you read, mine included, comes from whoever ran the test and whoever visited their page. I have not measured the entropy of my own visitors.

What survives is the shape of the thing. A rare GPU string joined to an unusual core count joined to a thinly populated time zone joined to a three-entry language list is not four weak facts. It is one strong one. Fingerprinting is not a measurement. It is a join.

Canvas, WebGL and audio, without the hand-waving

Canvas. The leak page creates a 240 by 60 canvas that is never shown, fills a rectangle in orange, writes text at 16px with an emoji on the end, then draws a circle over it with globalCompositeOperation set to multiply. It calls toDataURL() and hashes the result. The instructions are identical on every machine; the pixels are not, because rasterisation, anti-aliasing, font hinting and subpixel layout are decided by your GPU, driver, fonts and browser build. A few pixels differing by one value is plenty: a hash turns any difference into a completely different number. Run it tomorrow and the number is the same, cookies cleared or not, because nothing was ever stored.

WebGL. Two levels. The blunt one has come up: WEBGL_debug_renderer_info hands over the GPU model as a string. The subtle one is that a page can render a scene off-screen and hash the pixels exactly as with canvas, and also read the supported extension list, maximum texture size and shader precision ranges. That is a profile of your driver rather than a name for your card, so two machines with the same GPU can be told apart when their drivers differ.

Audio. This one draws nothing. A page builds an OfflineAudioContext, feeds an oscillator through a DynamicsCompressorNode, renders faster than real time, and hashes the resulting samples. No sound is played and no microphone is involved. The output varies because the compressor is implemented in software and floating-point behaviour differs across builds and platforms. The leak page does not do this one, so there is no audio row to look for. How discriminating it is next to canvas, I have not tested.

Why a fingerprint is worse for you than a cookie

A cookie is a badge the site hands you and asks you to wear. You can look at it, delete it, refuse it, and it expires on a date somebody had to choose. A fingerprint is not a badge, it is a description. Nothing was placed on your machine, so there is nothing to delete, no expiry, and no panel anywhere listing what sites have worked out.

“Clear browsing data” does not touch this. It clears what was stored. Your GPU, screen, fonts and time zone were never stored; they are read fresh on every visit and they will read the same tomorrow.

Private browsing barely moves it either. A private window starts with empty storage, which defeats cookies, then reports the same screen, core count, GPU and canvas hash. It does not make you a new person, it makes you the same person with an empty pocket.

It also works across sites with nobody cooperating: two unrelated sites running the same measurements arrive at the same value, with no shared third party and no dialogue box. Under most consent regimes, including the one I wrote about in what consent actually means under the DPDP Act, reading device characteristics to identify a person is exactly the processing meant to require consent. The reading still happens. The remedy is legal, and arrives afterwards.

The part most privacy advice gets backwards

Here is the counter-intuitive bit, and the reason I wanted to write this. The instinct is that revealing less makes you harder to identify. For fingerprinting, it frequently makes you easier to identify.

A fingerprint does not need your details to be sensitive. It needs them to be rare. A stock laptop running a default browser disappears into an enormous crowd of identical answers. Install twenty extra fonts, run an unusual window size, spoof your time zone somewhere thinly populated, adopt a niche browser, and each choice lifts you out of that crowd.

Inconsistency is its own signal. A browser claiming to be an iPhone while reporting sixteen cores, a mouse pointer and a 3840 × 2160 screen is not a common iPhone. It is a distinctive liar, and the mismatch is a stable feature to key on. A canvas that refuses to return pixels is rarer than any particular hash, so blocking a read can be more identifying than answering it. The goal is therefore not to say less but to look like more people, a different objective that most advice never states.

Test the claim instead of trusting me. Open the same page in the browser you consider private and in one on default settings, then compare. If the hardened setup gives the more unusual answers, that is this effect on your own machine.

Compare two browsers

What would change my mind: randomisation done properly. If a browser returns a different fabricated canvas value to every site and session — the approach Brave calls farbling — then being unusual stops mattering, because there is no stable value left to join on. Whether that survives a site sampling the same measurement many times and averaging the noise away is a real open question, and not one I have tested.

What actually helps, and how much

In rough order of effort, each with its limit attached, because a defence without its limits is marketing.

A mainstream browser on default settings. Boring on purpose, free, and it puts you in the largest crowd available. The limit: that crowd is large along each axis separately, and the combination of GPU, screen and time zone can still pick you out. Defaults stop you standing out, not being described.

Safari’s reduced system information. Apple has for years limited what a page can learn about the machine, and says so publicly. It is on by default, which matters more than the strength of any single measure, because a defence everybody has is a crowd. It does not make you identical to other Safari users, and I have not measured how much it removes.

Firefox’s resist-fingerprinting mode. Enabled through privacy.resistFingerprinting, it reports a common time zone rather than yours, rounds the window through letterboxing, generalises the user agent, restricts fonts and interferes with canvas reads. It is the most thorough thing outside Tor Browser, from which it was inherited. Two limits: it visibly breaks pages, and hardly anybody turns it on, so it can become the unusual thing that marks you.

Tor Browser. The only project that treats the problem correctly, by making every user look the same rather than making each reveal less. The costs are real: it is slow, some sites break and some refuse Tor outright. To watch what its three-relay circuit does, there is a step-through of one on this site.

On the address specifically. If you use a VPN, compare the public address in the leak table against the one your provider claims to give you. If they differ, the WebRTC request escaped the tunnel. Block WebRTC outright and every browser video call stops working. There is no version where you keep both.

Where I actually land on this

Most people reading this do not need Tor Browser. The useful question is not how to stop all of it but who you are trying not to be recognised by. Ordinary ad tech is frustrated far more cheaply than a determined adversary with a specific reason to care about you.

It is worth keeping in proportion too. A good deal of the fear I get asked about is misplaced; I wrote a whole piece on why your phone feels hacked and probably is not, and the machinery described here is usually the real explanation for an advert that feels like it was listening. The larger exposure for most people is not their canvas hash. It is what they have already published. For that, paste your own bio into the self-check and read what comes back.

And the exception I owe you. This site loads Google Analytics on every page, mine included, so Google receives the ordinary pageview signals: browser, screen size, language, and a location derived from your address. Several of those are rows in the very table I have been pointing you at. I could remove it and I have not, because I want to know which articles get read. A trade I made, not a principle I would defend.
