Which AI tool for which job

Nobody actually wants "the best AI tool." They want the right tool for one specific job — a deck by Thursday, a research summary, a voiceover, a workflow that stops the copy-paste. So this is organised by job, not by brand. For each one: what it's genuinely good at, what the free tier gives you, and the exact point where free stops being enough.

On prices and limits. Everything below was checked in August 2026. This category moves faster than any blog can keep up with — plans get renamed, free quotas get cut, and new tiers appear every few weeks. Treat the numbers here as the shape of the market, not a quote. Always confirm on the vendor's own pricing page before you pay.

Start with the job, not the tool

The reason most "best AI tools" lists are useless is that they answer a question nobody asked. You don't have a tool problem. You have a Thursday problem: a deck to build, a hundred emails to triage, a paper to understand, a client demo to record. The tool is downstream of that.

So before you open a single pricing page, answer three questions. What is the actual output? A file, a decision, a saved hour? How often does this job come back? Once, or every week? What happens if the output is wrong? A typo you fix, or an email your client reads? Those three answers pick your tool faster than any comparison table, because they tell you whether you need a generalist, a specialist, or an automation — and whether a free tier can carry it.

A one-off job that fails harmlessly? Use a free generalist. A weekly job with real stakes? Pay for the specialist. A job that repeats a hundred times a month? Stop shopping for tools and build a workflow. That last one is most of what I do for clients, and it's usually the cheapest answer of the three.

The all-rounders: chat assistants

This is where nearly everyone starts, and for most people one good assistant covers 70% of what they'd otherwise buy five tools for. The honest truth in 2026 is that the top models are close enough that habit and integration matter more than benchmarks. Pick one, learn it properly, and you'll beat someone who hops between four.

  • ChatGPT — the broadest feature surface: image generation, data analysis, voice, agent-style browsing, and a huge library of custom GPTs. If you want one assistant that does a bit of everything and you don't want to think about it, this is the default. Free tier is real but capped, and OpenAI has started putting ads in the free tier in some markets. Paid tiers run from a cheap entry plan around $8/mo up through Plus at $20/mo and a $200/mo Pro tier.
  • Claude — my daily driver, and the one I reach for when the writing has to sound like a person wrote it. Strongest on long documents, careful reasoning, following complicated instructions without drifting, and code. Free tier is genuinely usable for light work; Pro is $20/mo (a bit less annually), with Max tiers at $100 and $200/mo for heavy use.
  • Google Gemini — the one to use if you live in Google Workspace. It sits inside Docs, Sheets, Gmail, and Drive, which means it can act on your files without you pasting anything. Free tier is generous; the paid AI Pro plan sits around the usual $20/mo mark with a higher Ultra tier above it.
  • Microsoft Copilot — the same logic for the Microsoft world. Free consumer version is decent; the version that actually matters is Microsoft 365 Copilot inside Word, Excel, Outlook, and Teams, licensed per seat. Worth it for organisations already on M365, hard to justify otherwise.
  • Grok — its distinguishing feature is live access to X. If your work involves tracking real-time public conversation, that's a genuine edge. If it doesn't, this is a general assistant like the others, bundled with an X subscription.
  • DeepSeek and Mistral Le Chat — the best "capable and actually free" options. DeepSeek in particular gives you strong reasoning, web search, and file uploads at no cost. For students and anyone on a tight budget, these two deserve far more attention than they get. Read the data policy before you put anything confidential in — that applies to all of these, but people skip it fastest on the free ones.

What I'd do: pick one paid assistant that matches the ecosystem you already work in, and keep one free one open as a second opinion. Two is useful. Five is procrastination.

Research and reading

This is the category where a specialist genuinely beats a generalist, because the job isn't "write me something" — it's "tell me what's true, and show me where it came from."

  • Perplexity — a search engine that answers in prose with citations attached. Use it when you need current facts and you intend to check the sources. The free tier gives you unlimited basic searches plus a small daily allowance of the deeper "Pro" searches — about five a day, which is enough to learn whether you need more. Pro is $20/mo, and there's discounted student pricing worth claiming if you're eligible.
  • Gemini Notebook (what NotebookLM became in mid-2026) — the most underrated tool on this entire page. You upload your sources — PDFs, notes, papers, transcripts — and it answers only from those, with inline citations back to the page. That grounding is the whole point: it can't wander off and invent a statistic, because it's confined to what you gave it. The audio and video overview features, which turn a pile of documents into a listenable discussion, are genuinely useful for revision. The free tier is unusually generous, with room for many notebooks and a solid daily query allowance.
  • Deep Research modes — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Perplexity all now ship a mode that spends several minutes reading dozens of sources and returns a cited report. This is the single most valuable paid feature across all of them, and the one most tightly rationed on free plans. If you do any real research, this is the feature that justifies a subscription.

Use a chat assistant when you want it to think. Use a research tool when you want it to be right. Confusing the two is how people end up quoting a statistic that doesn't exist.

Writing, slides, and documents

This is the category everyone asks me about, and where the specialists earn their money.

  • Gamma — for presentations, it deserves the reputation. You describe the deck, it builds something that already looks designed, and you edit from there instead of from a blank slide. It's fastest for the "I need a decent deck in twenty minutes" job. The catch is the credit model: the free plan hands you a one-time allowance of around 400 credits that does not refill — good for roughly ten to fifteen decks, and then it's over. Paid plans (roughly $8–$20/mo for individuals, more for the heavy tier) top you up monthly and remove the Gamma branding, which matters the moment a deck goes to a client.
  • Canva — the broader design suite, now with a full set of AI features. If you need presentations plus social graphics, documents, and video from one subscription, this is better value than Gamma. The free plan includes a small monthly AI credit allowance (around 50), which runs out fast if you lean on generation. Pro sits in the $12–$18/mo range depending on billing.
  • Grammarly — no longer just a spellchecker; it does tone, clarity rewrites, and full generation. The free plan is more useful than most, covering grammar, spelling, and tone detection plus a monthly cap of AI prompts. Pro is around $12/mo annually. Worth it if you write to clients daily; skip it if your assistant already handles your drafting.
  • Notion AI — the argument here isn't quality, it's context. It can search and write across your own workspace, which a standalone chatbot can't. Free and Plus plans include only a limited trial of AI; the full capability is bundled into the Business tier. Only makes sense if your team already runs on Notion.
  • The assistants themselves — for long-form writing, an assistant with a good prompt still beats every "AI writer" SaaS product, most of which are thin wrappers around the same models you're already paying for. Before you subscribe to a writing tool, check whether it's doing anything your $20 assistant can't.

Images, video, and voice

The fastest-moving corner of the market, and the one where I'd advise the most caution about annual plans. The leader changes every few months.

  • Images. Midjourney still produces the most distinctive aesthetic, but has no free tier at all — $10/mo minimum, up to $120. Canva and the assistants (ChatGPT and Gemini both generate images) cover ordinary business needs perfectly well and cost nothing extra. Adobe Firefly is the one to use when commercial licensing and rights clarity matter, which is a real consideration for client work. For anything with text inside the image — a poster, a thumbnail with a headline — the assistants have improved enormously and are usually the fastest route.
  • Video. Runway and Kling lead on generated cinematic clips, roughly $8–$15/mo to start, and Kling has a meaningful free tier worth testing first. Google Veo comes bundled with the higher Google AI plans. Note that OpenAI has been winding down standalone Sora access during 2026, with that generation quality now reaching people mostly through third-party platforms — a good illustration of why you shouldn't build a business process on one video model.
  • Talking-head and training video. HeyGen and Synthesia generate an avatar presenting your script — genuinely useful for onboarding material, course content, and localised training video. HeyGen's free plan gives you about three videos a month; Synthesia starts around $18/mo with a monthly minutes cap. Both are far cheaper than filming, and both look like what they are, so use them where that's acceptable.
  • Voice. ElevenLabs is still the clear leader for text-to-speech and voice cloning. The free tier gives you around 10,000 characters a month — about ten minutes of audio — and is non-commercial only, with attribution required. That restriction catches people out constantly. Paid plans start around $5/mo and are what you need for any client-facing use. Descript is the better pick if you're editing podcasts or video, because it lets you edit recordings by editing the transcript.

Licensing is the trap in this category. Free tiers for images, voice, and video are very often restricted to personal, non-commercial use. Using one for a client deliverable — or even your own marketing — can breach the terms. Check the commercial-use clause before you generate, not after you've shipped.

Meetings, notes, and admin

The least glamorous category and probably the highest return per rupee, because it removes work you were never going to enjoy doing anyway.

  • Otter — real-time transcript as the meeting happens. Free plan covers around 300 minutes a month with a cap on individual meeting length.
  • Fireflies — strongest on CRM integrations, so notes and action items land where your sales process already lives. Free tier is limited by total stored minutes rather than monthly usage.
  • Fathom — the most generous free tier of the group: unlimited recording with a monthly cap on AI summaries. If you just want a searchable archive of your calls at zero cost, start here.
  • Granola — the interesting one, because it doesn't send a bot into your call. It listens locally and merges what you typed with what was said, which produces notes that read like yours. Free tier covers a limited number of meetings; paid runs around $14–$18/user/mo.

One caution that isn't about price: in many jurisdictions, recording a conversation requires the consent of everyone on the call. A note-taker joining silently is not consent. Say it out loud at the start.

Coding and building

The category where AI has changed the actual work the most, and where I have the strongest opinions, because this is what I do.

  • GitHub Copilot — best value for autocomplete-style assistance inside the editor you already use. There's a real free tier (a couple of thousand completions a month), and Pro is $10/mo, which is the cheapest genuinely useful paid AI subscription on this entire page.
  • Cursor — an editor built around AI rather than an editor with AI bolted on. Multi-file edits, codebase-wide refactoring, and agent mode are noticeably ahead of plain autocomplete. $20/mo, with higher tiers for heavy use. Worth the premium over Copilot if you work across whole codebases rather than single files.
  • Claude Code and OpenAI Codex — terminal-based agents that read your repository, make changes across many files, run tests, and iterate. This is a different mode of working from autocomplete, and the one I use most. Claude Code is included in the Claude Pro and Max subscriptions rather than billed separately, which makes Max unusually good value if you code daily.
  • v0, Lovable, Bolt, and Replit — prompt-to-app builders. Genuinely excellent for prototypes, landing pages, and internal tools; genuinely risky as the foundation of a real product, because the generated code becomes yours to maintain and you may not understand it. Use them to find out whether an idea is worth building, then build it properly.

The thing I tell everyone I mentor: these tools raise your ceiling, not your floor. They make a good engineer much faster and a confused one much more confidently wrong. Learning to read the output critically is now a bigger part of the job than writing the first draft ever was. I've written more about that ordering in the four-step path.

Automation and agents

This is where AI stops being something you chat with and starts being something that runs while you sleep. It's also the category with the biggest gap between what people spend and what they get.

  • n8n — my default. The self-hosted Community Edition is free with no execution limits; you pay only for the server it runs on. That, plus the ability to keep sensitive data on your own infrastructure, is why most of my client builds land here. Cloud plans start around €24/mo if you'd rather not host it yourself.
  • Make — the most generous free tier of the mainstream three: around 1,000 operations a month across two active scenarios, which is plenty to prove out an idea. The visual builder is the friendliest of the group.
  • Zapier — the largest app library and the easiest start, with a free plan covering about 100 tasks a month on two-step workflows. It's also the one that gets expensive fastest, because it bills per task rather than per workflow run. That difference is not a rounding error; on a multi-step workflow running thousands of times a month, it can be an order of magnitude.

The pattern I've built over and over — trigger, gather context, classify, draft, act — is written up in detail in the n8n email automation guide, and the full working system is open source in my AI SDR post if you'd rather read code than prose.

What I actually pay for

Lists are cheap, so here's the disclosure. Out of everything above, I pay for a small number of things and use free tiers for the rest.

  • One assistant subscription, at the higher tier. Claude, mostly for writing, reasoning, and Claude Code. This is the single line item I'd defend hardest.
  • A research tool. Because citations matter in my work, and the deep-research modes are the one paid feature I use every week.
  • A design subscription. Covers slides, graphics, and social in one place instead of three.
  • Server capacity for self-hosted n8n. Which costs less than any automation SaaS plan and does more.

Everything else — second-opinion chat, meeting notes, occasional voice, occasional video — runs on free tiers, and has for a long time. The total is roughly the price of two subscriptions, not eight. If your AI spend is climbing past that and you're not shipping proportionally more, you're collecting tools, not using them.

Where the free tiers stop

Everyone publishes the free-tier list. Almost nobody tells you where the wall is. Here it is — the specific moment each category stops being free in practice.

What free actually gets you, and what pushes you over the line. Checked August 2026.
Job Best free option Where free stops
General chat DeepSeek or Mistral (uncapped in practice); Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini free tiers The moment you work in long sessions. Message caps reset every few hours and always bite mid-task.
Research Gemini Notebook; Perplexity's basic search Deep-research runs. Every platform rations these hard — it's the main thing you're buying.
Presentations Gamma's one-time credit grant; Canva free Gamma's credits don't refill — roughly 10–15 decks total. Branding removal is also paid-only.
Images ChatGPT / Gemini / Canva built-in generation Commercial licensing clarity, and volume. Midjourney has no free tier at all.
Video Kling free tier; HeyGen's ~3 videos/month Almost immediately. Video is the most compute-expensive category and free tiers reflect that.
Voice ElevenLabs free (~10k characters/month) The instant it's commercial. Free is non-commercial with attribution — that's the real limit, not the character count.
Meeting notes Fathom (unlimited recording, capped summaries) Integrations and team sharing. Solo use can stay free almost indefinitely.
Coding GitHub Copilot Free (~2,000 completions/month) Agent-style, multi-file work. Autocomplete is cheap; agents are what you pay for.
Automation Self-hosted n8n (unlimited); Make's ~1,000 ops/month n8n barely stops — you pay for a server, not seats. Zapier's 100 tasks/month goes in a day.

Read that table one more time and notice the pattern. Free tiers are generous exactly where compute is cheap (text) and stingy exactly where it's expensive (video, deep research, agents). They're also generous on capability and stingy on volume and rights. Which means the question "is the free tier good?" has a consistent answer: yes, for learning whether you need the paid one. That is what free tiers are for, and it's a completely legitimate way to use them.

The mistakes I see most

  • Collecting subscriptions instead of building habits. Six tools used shallowly lose to one tool used well, every time. Cancel anything you haven't opened in a month.
  • Paying annually in a market this volatile. The discount is 15–25%. The risk is that the tool is second-best in four months. Go monthly until something has earned a year of your money.
  • Pasting confidential material into free tiers. Free plans generally have the weakest data guarantees, and often the broadest training-use terms. Client data, personal data, and anything under NDA belong on a paid or self-hosted setup, and that decision should be made before the deadline pressure hits.
  • Trusting output that sounds confident. Fluency is not accuracy. Anything with a number, a name, a date, a citation, or a legal consequence gets checked by a human. Every time.
  • Automating a process you haven't defined. AI applied to a messy process gives you a faster mess. Write the steps down by hand first. If you can't describe it, you can't automate it — and this is where most automation projects actually die.

A sixty-second decision rule

If you take one thing from this page, take this. When you next wonder which AI tool to use, run down this list and stop at the first line that matches:

  1. Is it a one-off thinking or writing job? Your existing assistant. Don't buy anything.
  2. Does it need to be verifiably true? A research tool with citations, not a chatbot.
  3. Is the output a designed artifact — a deck, a graphic, a video, a voiceover? A specialist. Generalists are still mediocre at final-form design.
  4. Does this job repeat more than weekly? Stop evaluating tools. Build a workflow.
  5. Does it touch client data, money, or anything you can't take back? Whatever you use, put a human approval step in front of it.

The tools will keep changing — half the specifics on this page will be stale within a year, and I'll update it when they are. The five questions won't change, because they're about the work, not the vendor. Get those right and the tool almost picks itself.
