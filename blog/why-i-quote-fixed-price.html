Why I quote fixed price, what it costs me, and when I refuse

A fixed price is a promise about a defined thing. That makes it the fairest structure I know when the scope is genuinely fixed, and a trap for both sides when it is not. Here is the case from the client’s side of the table, the part of the bill that is really a risk premium, and the five kinds of work I will not quote this way.

What a fixed price actually buys the client

Nearly every engagement I take ends up fixed-price, and my services page says so plainly. I want to argue for that properly, which means starting on the client’s side of the table and only then admitting what the arrangement costs the person doing the quoting.

The first thing a fixed price buys is approvability. A number is a thing a person with a budget can say yes to; an hourly rate is not, because a rate is a rate and a hope. Spend is generally approved on totals rather than rates, so an hourly quote has to be converted into a guess before anyone can sign it, and that guess is usually made by whoever has the least information about the work. A fixed price does the conversion at the only table where it can be done honestly.

The second thing is quieter, and I suspect it matters more, though I cannot prove it. A fixed price switches off the meter. Under hourly billing every clarifying question the client asks costs them money, so they ask fewer of them — and those questions are how the work becomes correct. When the price is fixed, a call costs the client nothing, and I end up being told more than I would otherwise have been told.

The third is that it ends the argument about efficiency before it starts. Nobody has to wonder whether a task took four hours because the problem was hard or because I was slow — a question that cannot be answered from the outside, and that sits in the room all engagement.

The overrun risk belongs to whoever can control it

Under time and materials, the client carries the cost of every wrong turn I take. If I pick the wrong library, misread the data model, or spend a day learning something I arguably should have known, the meter runs and they pay for it. They have no lever on any of that. They cannot see the wrong turn coming and could not stop it if they could.

I hold to a general principle here: put the risk where the control is. Whoever chooses the approach, the sequence and the tools should carry the cost of choosing badly, because that is the only arrangement in which the incentive points at doing it well. A fixed price does exactly that. If I estimate badly, the estimate was mine.

The principle has a limit, and the limit is the honest part. It only holds for risks I actually control. Late feedback, a decision-maker who goes quiet, credentials that arrive three weeks after they were promised, a data export that turns out not to exist — none of those are mine, and a fixed quote that pretends otherwise is just a way of absorbing somebody else’s risk for free. So the quote carries an assumptions list: what I need, from whom, by when, and what happens to the price if it does not arrive. Scoping the work before you quote it is where that list gets built.

This is also the seed of every refusal later in this article. Where control genuinely sits with somebody else, a fixed price stops being a fair transfer of risk and becomes a bet.

It forces the scope argument to happen first

To put a number on something I have to know what done looks like. That sounds obvious, and it is the whole mechanism. Fixed pricing makes under-definition my problem in week zero, when it costs an email, rather than the client’s problem in week six, when it costs a relationship.

The conversation is often uncomfortable and always cheap. What is explicitly not included. How many rounds of revision. What has to be true for the deadline to be real. Who signs off. These are the questions that turn into scope creep when left unasked, and a fixed price forces them into the open because I cannot price the work without the answers.

Sometimes the conversation kills the project, which is a good outcome arriving early. Suppose a team asks for a dashboard over a process that three people currently update by hand in a spreadsheet. Pricing the dashboard means asking which decision it will change, and the honest answer is sometimes none — the process underneath is the problem, and I have written separately about what a manual process actually costs. That is a hypothetical, but the shape of it comes up often enough that I now ask about the decision before the dashboard.

An hourly engagement can skip all of this. It can start on Monday with nobody quite sure what is being built, and it will still bill correctly every week, which is precisely the problem.

What a fixed price costs me

A post that only listed the benefits would be an advertisement. Here is the bill.

Every fixed price contains a risk premium. I can only be wrong in one direction, so I price the version of the project where the awkward things happen. Most of the time they do not, and the client has paid for a contingency that was never used. That is not a scandal — it is how insurance works, and the certainty is the product. But it does mean a well-organised client with clean data and a decision-maker who answers email is partly paying for the projects that go sideways. If that is you, you are subsidising somebody else’s chaos. I have not measured my own premium across engagements, so I will not put a figure on it, but structurally it is always there.

It punishes optimism. Estimates skew short; the tendency to underestimate our own future work is well documented in psychology and is not a personal failing. Under hourly billing that optimism costs the client a little. Under a fixed price it costs me all of it, so I have to quote the pessimistic version, which makes my number look worse than the optimistic number from someone who has not done that arithmetic. In my experience the optimistic number wins the work and then argues about it in month two.

Mid-project discovery becomes a commercial event. Under time and materials, finding out in week three that the old system has an undocumented behaviour is just a Tuesday. Under a fixed price it is a conversation about money, and the quiet pressure is to route around the discovery rather than raise it. I name that pressure because naming it is most of the defence: when I catch myself reaching for the workaround, I make myself say whether I am choosing it because it is right or because it is cheaper for me.

It pushes towards defensive scoping. The safest scope is the narrowest one, so a fixed price nudges me towards the most conservative reading of what was asked. The client can then receive exactly what the document says and still less than what they had pictured. That is the model’s worst failure, and the worst part is how it looks from the outside: delivered on time, delivered in scope, and nobody got what they wanted.

A fixed price is only honest if the scope is fixed

Everything above depends on one condition. A fixed price is a promise about a defined thing. Put the same number against a vague description and it becomes a trap, and it traps both sides.

A fixed price on a vague scope is not a bargain, it is a deferred argument. One of you is going to lose that argument in month two, and which one depends mostly on who wrote the document.

There are two ways it goes wrong and neither is good. Either I pad the number until it survives any reading of the request, in which case you pay for uncertainty I could have removed with better questions. Or I do not pad it, and I spend the second half of the project defending a sentence instead of doing the work. Padding is the more honest of the two and it is still worse than the clear conversation you should have been offered.

The test I use is simple. Both of us write down, separately, what done looks like. If the two descriptions do not match, the scope is not fixed and the price should not be either. It works when you are the one hiring too: ask your supplier for their version before you send yours.

The payment structure matters as much as the number. A fixed price paid in stages against observable deliverables is a fixed price the client can walk away from, which is most of what makes it safe for them. Where a scope has not agreed something more specific, my refund policy treats advances for work not yet started as refundable in full and accepted milestones as not. A fixed price paid entirely up front is a different and worse product wearing the same name.

Put the number in a document, not in an email. The quotation side of my invoice lab produces a clean quotation or GST invoice in the browser, with the tax split decided from the place of supply and nothing uploaded anywhere. It will not scope the work for you — that part is unavoidably yours — but it stops the number living in a paragraph nobody can find later.

When I refuse, and what I offer instead

This is the part with teeth. There are five kinds of work I will not put a fixed price on. In every case I have something else to offer, because refusing a pricing model is not the same as refusing the work.

Open-ended research

“Find out whether this is possible” cannot be quoted, because the cost of an answer depends on the answer. Anyone giving you a firm number for that has either decided in advance what they are going to find or padded it enormously. What I offer instead is a paid discovery: time-boxed, ending in a written recommendation, with a real quote for the build if a build turns out to make sense. My own research is why I will not pretend otherwise — the one paper I have published took the time it took, and no quote could honestly have predicted the result.

Anything gated on a third party I do not control

A vendor API with undocumented behaviour, another supplier’s delivery date, an approval queue at a regulator, a platform that can change under me mid-project. The risk is real and it is not mine to manage, so a fixed price here is a bet on somebody else’s conduct. I have written about how automations break at exactly these seams. What I offer is a split: fixed price for the part I control, and time and materials with a hard cap for the integration surface, so the exposure still has a ceiling even though it is no longer one number.

When the client cannot yet describe done

Not unwilling — unable. This is common and it is not a criticism. Describing done is genuinely difficult, and it is often the thing the client needs help with in the first place. But I cannot price a finish line neither of us can see. What I offer is a small paid piece whose deliverable is the definition itself: the scope, the acceptance criteria and the quote. The client owns that document either way, including if they take it elsewhere.

Ongoing operations

Keeping an automation running, watching a queue, being reachable when something breaks. That is not a project, it is a retainer, and pricing it as a project is a category error — a project has an end and operations do not. What I offer is a retainer with a stated scope of cover, a review interval and an explicit list of what is not covered. The same applies to security work that is really a posture rather than a one-off engagement; I have described the difference in what a security review actually finds.

When the scope conversation is most of the work

Sometimes pinning down the requirement is the engagement and the implementation afterwards is comparatively small. Quoting that as a build misdescribes what is being bought and almost always underprices the hard half. I would rather sell the deciding as the deliverable and quote the building separately, once there is something to build.

One thing I say before any discovery starts: a discovery that ends in do not build this is a successful engagement, not a wasted one. Saying it in advance matters, because otherwise there is a pull towards justifying the spend with a build nobody needed.

What would change my mind

I want to be careful not to turn a risk-allocation tool into a moral position. Fixed price is not more virtuous than hourly. It is a different distribution of the same uncertainty.

Structure Who carries an overrun Fits when Fails when
Fixed price Me Done is observable and written down Scope is vague, or discovery is likely
Time and materials with a cap Shared up to the cap, then me Direction is clear, detail is not The client needs a total to get approval
Retainer Both, inside an agreed window The work is continuous with no end state It is used to smuggle a project in

The strongest challenge to my own default is the middle row. Time and materials with a hard cap is arguably better for the client than a fixed price, because they keep the saving if the work goes smoothly and still have a ceiling if it does not. My reasons for not defaulting to it are real but unglamorous: it asks the client to trust my time reporting, it creates more administration on both sides, and it is harder to get approved internally because the number on the page is a maximum rather than a total. When a client proposes it, I take it.

What would change my mind more broadly is measurement. If I tracked quotes against actual effort across enough engagements and found the premium I build in consistently exceeds what the certainty is worth to the people buying it, the right response would be to stop defaulting to fixed price. I have not done that measurement, and I am wary of anyone who claims they have without showing the workings.

What I am confident about is narrower than the title of this article suggests: the pricing model should be chosen after the scope conversation rather than before it, and chosen by asking who can actually control the thing most likely to go wrong. Anyone telling you one model is always right is selling the model.
