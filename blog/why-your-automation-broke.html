Why your automation broke, and how to build one that fails loudly

The sentence that opens almost every one of these conversations is "we didn't change anything." It is usually true, and it is the reason the thing stopped rather than a defence against it. Your workflow was the only part of the arrangement holding still. Here are the eight causes that actually account for it, what each one looks like from the outside — which is normally silence — and the specific guard that catches it.

Nothing on your side changed

An automation is not a program you run. It is a standing agreement between several programs you do not control: a trigger owned by one vendor, an authentication token issued by a second, a record shape defined by a third, a schedule kept by a platform, and a network in between that is nobody's in particular. Every one of those pieces is on somebody else's release calendar, with somebody else's opinion about what counts as a breaking change.

So "we didn't change anything" is not a defence. It is the diagnosis. The workflow held still while the ground underneath it moved, and the useful question is never what did we break. It is two other questions: what moved, and why did a customer find out before we did.

That second question is the one this article is really about. The first half below is a list of what actually moves — eight causes that between them account for nearly everything I have been called in to look at. The second half is the engineering that turns any of them from a three-week silence into a message you get on the morning it happens. If you only have an afternoon, skip to alerting on absence, which is worth more than the other three guards combined.

Why the failure is silent

Every alarm most teams own is wired to an error. A step throws, the platform catches it, an email goes out, somebody looks. That is a real mechanism and it works — for the failures where something ran.

It does nothing at all for the failures where nothing ran. And the most common breakages sit at the edge of the workflow rather than in the middle of it: the trigger that stops polling, the credential that stops being accepted, the schedule that quietly detaches after a platform migration. In every one of those, no step executes, so no step throws, so the error alarm has nothing to fire on. The run history simply stops having new rows in it, and a table with no new rows looks exactly like a table you have not scrolled to the top of.

There is a second reason, and it is the one that stings. An automation is a thing you deliberately stopped watching. That is what you paid for. Nobody notices the invoices that did not get created for the same reason nobody notices a lift that is not making a noise.

Keep that picture in mind through the next eight sections. For each cause I have written what it looks like from outside, because that is the only view you get at three in the morning, and the specific guard that catches it. Several of the guards repeat. That is not padding — the same three or four checks cover most of the list, which is the good news buried in a long article.

1. A vendor deprecated an API version

From outside. Two shapes, and the second is far worse than the first. The loud shape is an HTTP 410, or a 400 whose body mentions a version you have never typed anywhere. The quiet shape is an endpoint that still answers 200 while the field you read is now nested one level deeper, or renamed, or gone — so your workflow runs green and writes empty strings into a CRM for a fortnight.

Why it happens. Almost never without warning. The warning went to the email address that created the vendor account, which on a small team is a founder's inbox, an alias nobody reads, or an ex-employee, eight months before the cut-off date. It was also on a status page and in a changelog, both of which are pull mechanisms in a world where nothing else about your operations is a pull mechanism.

The guard. Pin the version explicitly — in the path, the header, or wherever the vendor puts it — and never rely on the default. "The default" means "whatever is current on the day this runs", which is a moving target you have handed to a stranger. Then subscribe a shared address that a working human reads to the changelog and status feed, and record every pinned version with its announced end date somewhere you will actually look, which for me means a comment in the repository next to the call. The HAR analyser is useful when you are trying to establish what a vendor is really returning today versus what you assumed a year ago: capture the exchange from the browser and read the actual response rather than the documentation.

2. A refresh token expired or was revoked

From outside. Pure silence, and this is the archetype of the whole article. The trigger stops firing. Nothing errors, because nothing runs. Some platforms show a "connection needs reauthentication" banner in a settings page that nobody has opened since the day it was built.

Why it happens. There is a long list and every item is ordinary. The employee whose account the integration was connected under left, and IT deprovisioned them properly. A user changed a password, and the identity provider invalidates refresh tokens on password change. An administrator revoked third-party app access during a security tidy-up, correctly. The refresh token had an absolute lifetime and simply reached it. Or — the one I would name specifically because it catches people repeatedly — the OAuth client is still in a provider's testing or unverified state, where refresh tokens are deliberately short-lived, so the integration works beautifully for a week and then does not.

The guard. Three things, in order of how much they buy you. Connect integrations under a service account or a shared mailbox that survives a person leaving, not under whoever happened to be at the keyboard. Add a token-health check that runs on a schedule, calls the cheapest authenticated endpoint the vendor has — a /me or a /whoami — and alerts on a 401 rather than logging it. And alert on absence, because a token failure that kills the trigger will never reach any check that lives inside the workflow. If your access token is a JWT, the JWT lab decodes one in the browser and shows you its expiry claim, which is the fastest way to find out whether you are looking at a one-hour token, a ninety-day one, or something with no expiry at all.

3. A rate limit that only bites at scale

From outside. Partial results. Some records processed, some not, and no error anywhere if the workflow treats a 429 as just another response and carries on to the next step. A run that says success and did two thirds of the job is worse than a run that failed, because the first one is invisible and the second one is a ticket.

Why it happens. Limits are per second, per minute, per day, and often per bursty window on top of that — and you were comfortably underneath all of them for a year. Then a customer imports a spreadsheet, or a backfill runs, or a retry storm from an unrelated failure eats the minute's budget, or the vendor tightens the number because somebody else abused it. Nothing in your workflow changed. Its volume did.

The guard. Treat 429 as a first-class outcome with its own branch, never as a generic failure and never as a shrug: respect the Retry-After header when the vendor sends one, back off exponentially with jitter when it does not, and cap your own concurrency rather than trusting the platform's default parallelism. Then add the cheapest and most underrated check in this article — count the records you took in and the records you wrote out, and alert when they differ. A run that reports "processed 46 of 512" is a run that tells you the truth. This is the same trade-off I spent a research project on in a different domain: a limiter that protects a system by refusing honest work has only moved the failure, and the only way to know which side of that line you are on is to measure the work that did not happen.

4. A field was renamed in the CRM

From outside. Again two shapes. Loud: an HTTP 422 naming a field you do not recognise. Quiet, and far more common: the automation writes into a key the system no longer knows and the system accepts the request and drops the value, or reads a field that is now null and cheerfully writes an empty string. Everything is green. Nothing is populated.

Why it happens. Somebody in sales operations renamed a field, changed a picklist value, or rebuilt a stage. That is their job and they are allowed to do it. The problem is on your side: most CRMs keep a stable internal identifier and a friendly label, and the integration was bound to the label because the label is what the connector's dropdown showed you when you built it.

The guard. Bind to the immutable identifier wherever the vendor exposes one, even when the label is what a human would read. Validate the record before you use it: assert that the fields you depend on are present and non-empty, and fail the run when they are not. That last part is the important half — most no-code platforms make skipping easier than failing, and a workflow that skips is a workflow that has learned to lie to you. And tell the CRM owner, in one sentence, which fields an automation depends on. That conversation costs five minutes and prevents the rename.

5. A webhook arrived twice, or never

From outside. Duplicates, which are visible but embarrassing — two invoices, two Slack messages, two rows, one confused customer. Or the opposite and quieter failure: a delivery that never arrived at all, because the vendor retried three times into a five-minute outage on your side and then gave up. A webhook that was never received leaves no trace anywhere in your systems. There is nothing to search for.

Why it happens. At-least-once delivery is the contract almost every webhook ships with, and it is the right contract. A sender that cannot get an acknowledgement cannot distinguish a request that failed from a request that succeeded slowly, so it sends again. Which means the common duplicate is not a vendor bug at all: you did the work, then answered after their timeout, and they did what the documentation says they will do.

The guard. Answer 200 as soon as you have durably stored the event, and do the actual work afterwards, so a slow downstream system can never turn into a duplicate. Deduplicate on the vendor's event id with an idempotency key, covered properly below. And for the missing-delivery half, which no amount of receiver-side care will fix, add reconciliation: once a day, pull the vendor's list of events for the period and compare it against what you processed. That job is boring, it runs in a minute, and it is the only thing on this list that can find an event you never knew existed.

6. A timezone, or a DST boundary

From outside. A report that arrives at the wrong hour. A daily job that on one day a year does not run at all, and on another day runs twice. A "yesterday's records" query that on those same days covers 23 hours or 25 hours, so a day of data goes missing or gets counted twice — and nothing anywhere errors, because 23 hours of records is a perfectly valid set of records.

Why it happens. The schedule is expressed in one zone, the platform's servers keep another, the customer lives in a third, and the vendor's API returns timestamps in a fourth. India does not observe daylight saving, which means a team here can build and operate something for years without ever meeting the boundary, and then meet it through a European or American counterpart's data. A job scheduled at a local wall-clock time that does not exist on the spring-forward day simply does not run; on the autumn day, that same wall-clock time happens twice.

The guard. Schedule in UTC and convert only for display. Make every window explicit and half-open — greater than or equal to the start, strictly less than the end — rather than asking for "yesterday", which is a word and not a boundary. Store timestamps with their offset rather than as bare local strings. And for anything that must happen once a day, prefer logic that says "if this has not run since the last boundary, run it now" over logic that fires at an instant, which is the same idempotency idea arriving from a different direction. The Unix timestamp entry in the glossary is the short version of why an integer that does not carry a zone at all is often the safest thing to store.

7. Pagination changed underneath you

From outside. Nothing. This is the purest silent failure on the list. You ask for a list, take the first page, and stop — because for a year the first page was the whole list. Then the account grows past the page size, or the vendor reduces its default from 100 to 50 to shed load, and from that day you process the first 50 of 300 records on every run, successfully, forever. There is no error at any point in the life of that bug.

Why it happens. The loop was never written, because there was never a second page to write it against. It is not carelessness; it is a test that could not have failed at the time. This is the same shape as most security findings I write up — a decision that was correct under the conditions of the day, and stopped being correct when the conditions moved.

The guard. Always follow the cursor until the vendor says there is no next page, even when you are certain there is only one page. Always pass an explicit page size rather than accepting a default that is theirs to change. And keep the count check from the rate-limit section, with one addition: if the number of records you processed is exactly equal to the page size, treat that as a signal rather than a coincidence, because it almost always is one.

The pattern under the last three. A renamed field, a changed page size and a daylight-saving boundary have nothing technically in common, and they fail identically: the workflow reports success while producing incomplete or empty output. Any check that only asks "did the run finish" is blind to all three. A check that asks "how many records went in, how many came out, and does that match yesterday" catches all three, with one query.

8. A schema gained a required field

From outside. A 400 or a 422 on write, naming a field you have never sent. This one is at least loud, which is why it is last. The nastier variant is a field that becomes mandatory on create but not on update, so half your workflow carries on working and the half that onboards new records does not — which reads, on a dashboard, as a drop in new customers rather than as a broken integration.

Why it happens. The vendor added a concept — a currency on a line item, a consent flag, a region on a customer — and made it mandatory for new records. Plenty of vendors do not classify adding a required field as a breaking change, because every client they test with already sends it. From your side it is unambiguously breaking.

The guard. A contract test, described below, is the only thing that finds this before your customer's record does. A scheduled job that creates and deletes one throwaway object of each shape you write will fail the morning the requirement lands, rather than the morning somebody signs up.

Four distinct guards cover all eight rows. The rest of this article is those four, plus the two practices that make fixing any of them a small job instead of an archaeology exercise.

Alert on absence, not on error

If you take one thing from this article, take this one. It is an afternoon of work and it covers more ground than everything else here put together.

A workflow that has stopped running produces no errors at all. Every alarm wired to an error is therefore blind to the failure mode that stays hidden the longest.

The mechanism is deliberately dull. At the end of every successful run, write a heartbeat: a timestamp in a table, a row in a log, a ping to a dead-man's-switch service that expects to hear from you on an interval. Then have something outside the workflow — and it must be outside, because a monitor that lives inside the thing it monitors dies with it — check the age of the newest heartbeat and alert when it exceeds the interval plus a margin.

Two refinements are worth the extra hour. First, alert on the count, not only the age: successful runs per hour, records processed per run. A workflow that runs on schedule and processes zero records every time is failing, and an age check will never notice. Second, alert on the step change as well as the zero — yesterday's run handled 500 records and today's handled 12, which is either a quiet holiday or the pagination bug from section seven.

The honest caveat: this check will page somebody for a benign gap. A vendor has an outage, a genuinely quiet Sunday drops the count, a deployment pauses the queue for six minutes. The response to that is to widen the window or exclude the known-quiet period — not to delete the check, which is what usually happens on the second false alarm. A monitor everybody has muted is worse than no monitor, because it is also a story you tell yourself about being covered.

Practise reading the evidence. When an automation misbehaves, the first artefact you get is a log, and the skill is spotting the gap rather than the error — the hour with no entries, the status code that appears fifty times in a minute, the request that stops recurring. The log analyser on this site takes a pasted log and surfaces those patterns, entirely in your browser. Nothing you paste is uploaded anywhere.

Idempotency keys

Retries are not optional. Networks drop, vendors time out, platforms replay. Anything that will be retried has to be safe to retry, and an idempotency key is how you get that with one lookup.

The idea: before doing work that has a side effect, record a key that identifies this specific unit of work. If the key is already recorded as complete, skip the work and return the stored result. Two rules make the difference between a key that works and a key that is decoration, and the diagram earlier in this article draws both:

  • Use the sender's identifier, not one you generate. A key you generate at the top of the handler is a different key on the retry, which defeats the entire mechanism. Use the vendor's event id where they give you one. Where they do not, derive it deterministically from the natural key of the work — the order id plus the word "invoice" hashed together will do, and the hash lab is a quick way to convince yourself that the same input really does give the same digest every time.
  • Write the key in the same transaction as the work. If you record the key first and then do the work, a crash in between leaves a key claiming completion for work that never happened. If you do the work first and then record the key, a crash in between leaves work with no key and the retry duplicates it. The gap between the two writes is the bug, and the fix is to not have a gap.

Two practical notes. Give the key a lifetime that comfortably outlives the vendor's retry window — if they retry over 24 hours, a key you expire after an hour is a key that is absent when it matters. And check whether the vendor accepts an Idempotency-Key header on writes, which many payment and billing APIs do: sending one makes your retries safe on their side, which is the other half of the same problem and costs you one header.

A dead-letter queue you actually drain

A dead-letter queue is a place failed items go, with their input intact, so they can be inspected and replayed rather than lost. It is a straightforward idea that fails in practice for two straightforward reasons.

The first is that nobody looks at it. A queue with no alarm on it is a folder. Alert on depth greater than zero, not on rate — one stuck item is a signal, and waiting for a spike means waiting for the thing you were trying to avoid. The second is that items arrive without enough context to replay: an error message and a record id, and by the time anybody opens it the record has moved on and the original payload is gone. Store the complete input, the error, the attempt count, the timestamp and a correlation id that ties the entry back to the run that produced it.

Then make replay a single command. If draining the queue means somebody reconstructing a request by hand from a screenshot, the queue is a graveyard with good intentions. And test the replay path deliberately, on purpose, before you need it — the first replay is where you discover that the downstream system has since started rejecting the payload shape you stored.

One honest limit. A dead-letter queue only catches items that entered the system and failed. It catches nothing from sections two, six or seven of this article, where the item never entered at all. That is not an argument against having one; it is an argument against believing an empty queue means everything is fine. An empty queue and a silent workflow look identical.

Contract tests against the vendor

Your own tests check your code. A contract test checks your assumption about someone else's, and it is the only guard in this article that finds a vendor's change before your data does.

It is small. A scheduled job — daily is usually enough — that calls the endpoints you depend on and asserts the things you actually rely on: that the field exists, that it has the type you expect, that a list endpoint returns a next-page cursor in the shape you parse, that creating and deleting one throwaway object of each kind you write still succeeds. It fails loudly and it names the field. Compared with discovering the same fact three weeks later through a customer, it is close to free.

Two caveats, because this one gets oversold. Vendor sandboxes drift from production — a sandbox schema is often behind, occasionally ahead, and sometimes has different limits — so a green contract test against a sandbox is evidence rather than proof. And plenty of vendors have no sandbox at all, in which case you are testing against production with a tagged, disposable record that you create and delete in the same job, and you should agree that in writing with whoever owns the account before you start writing rows into their live system on a schedule.

Pin the version, and keep the workflow in git

Two practices that do not prevent anything by themselves and make everything else cheaper.

Pin the version. Say which version of the API you are calling, everywhere, explicitly. An unpinned integration is a promise that a stranger's release schedule is your release schedule. Pinning does not make you safe forever — versions do get retired — but it converts a surprise into a dated obligation, and a dated obligation can be put in a calendar.

Keep the workflow in version control. Export the workflow definition on a schedule and commit it. The moment you do, "what changed and when" is a diff rather than an argument, and the question of who edited the live workflow at 6pm on Friday has an answer. This is one of the real differences between the platforms, and it is why I said in the honest comparison of n8n, Make and Zapier that a change you cannot review in a pull request is a change nobody is reviewing. If you want the underlying mechanism to stop feeling magical, the git objects lab takes a commit apart to show why a diff between two exports is cheap and reliable.

One caveat that matters: an exported workflow can contain credentials, endpoint URLs and sample payloads with real customer data in them. Scrub the export before it lands in a repository, and store credentials by reference rather than by value. Putting a working key into git history to gain reviewability is a bad trade, and it is one of the most common findings in a security review.

What to ask a vendor before you build

Most of the eight causes above are decided before you write a line — by which vendor you picked and what they promise. These are the questions I ask, and they take one email:

  • What is your deprecation policy, and what notice period do you commit to? A number in writing is worth more than a reassurance on a call.
  • Where is the changelog, and can I subscribe an address that is not the account owner's? This single question fixes cause number one.
  • Is webhook delivery at-least-once, and do you send a stable event id? Without an id you cannot deduplicate, and you will have to reconcile instead.
  • What is the retry schedule and the delivery timeout, and can I fetch events I missed? The last clause is the one people forget to ask, and it is the only recovery path for an outage on your side.
  • What are the rate limits, per what window, and what do you return when I hit them? "It depends on your plan" is an answer you can work with; no answer is not.
  • Is there a sandbox, and does its schema match production? The second half of that question is the interesting half.
  • How long does a refresh token live, and what invalidates it? Password changes, admin revocation and app-verification state all belong in the answer.
  • Can I pin an API version, and how long do you keep old ones running?
  • Is there a status page with a feed, and does it cover the API or only the product? Those are frequently not the same system, and only one of them is the one you depend on.

If a vendor cannot answer most of these, that is information about the integration you are about to build rather than a judgement on the person who replied. Sometimes you build on them anyway, because the business reason is good — and then you budget for more monitoring, because you have just been told you will get less warning. Either way, that answer belongs in the estimate before it belongs in the invoice, which is the same argument as scoping the work before you quote it.

Fast detection beats prevention

Here is the part that a services page would leave out. You cannot prevent most of this.

A vendor can ship a change with no notice at all. An account can be suspended by an automated fraud system on a Saturday. A data centre can lose a region. A client's IT department can revoke an app during a security review nobody told you about, entirely correctly. There is no architecture that makes an integration immune to a company you do not own, and any consultant who implies otherwise is selling you a feeling.

So the goal is not zero breakage. It is time to detection, measured honestly. Every guard in the second half of this article exists to shorten the distance between the moment something moves and the moment a human knows — from three weeks to ten minutes — and to make sure that when a human does know, the input is still sitting there so the work can be replayed rather than reconstructed.

The metric I would hold a system to is not how many failures it had. It is how you found out about each one. If the answer is "a customer told us" more than once in a year, the problem is not the vendor who changed something. It is that the automation was built to succeed quietly and allowed to fail quietly too, and those are the same design decision made once.

Build automations that are boring when they work and loud when they do not. The second half is the part that gets skipped, because on the day you build it there is nothing to hear.

A worked example of the whole shape — trigger, classification, human gate, and what happens when a step fails — is in the walkthrough of automating email replies with n8n, and the code for a production system built the same way is public in the AI SDR I open-sourced. Both are worth reading with this article's list beside them, because you will spot the guards in them, and also the places where I chose to accept a risk rather than engineer it away.

Questions people ask

My automation stopped and the logs are completely empty. Where do I start?

An empty log is itself the finding: it means nothing executed, so the fault is at the edge rather than in the middle. Check three things in this order. First the credential — open the connection in the platform and look for a reauthentication prompt, or call a cheap authenticated endpoint by hand and see whether you get a 401. Second the trigger — confirm the workflow is still active, that a schedule survived any recent platform migration, and that a webhook endpoint registered with the vendor still points at a URL that exists. Third the vendor's status page and changelog for the window in which the last successful run sits. Between them those three cover the large majority of empty-log cases, and all three are faster to check than reading the workflow.

How often does this actually happen?

Often enough that I plan for it rather than react to it, and I would rather give you a rule than a statistic I cannot back with published data. The rule is that the expected lifetime of an integration without maintenance is roughly the interval at which its slowest-moving vendor ships a breaking change, and for mainstream business software that is a matter of months rather than years. If a workflow matters, budget for it to need attention two or three times a year, and treat a year of complete silence as a sign that nobody is checking rather than evidence that nothing has moved.

Is self-hosting more or less reliable than a hosted platform?

Neither, and the question is really about which failures you would rather own. A hosted platform absorbs patching, uptime and connector maintenance, and in exchange you get their outages, their retry policy and their deprecations on their timetable. Self-hosting gives you control over every one of those and hands you the pager. What actually changes reliability is not the hosting model — it is whether anybody is alerted when the thing stops. I have seen self-hosted workflows with better monitoring than enterprise deployments, and the reverse, and in both cases the monitoring was the variable.

Do I really need a dead-letter queue for a five-step workflow?

Probably not, and I would not build one first. For a small workflow the useful order is: alert on absence, then handle retries safely with an idempotency key, then add a dead-letter queue when you find yourself losing items you cannot reconstruct. The signal that you need one is specific — a failure where you cannot answer the question "which records did not get processed, and can I run them again". Until you have hit that question, the queue is scaffolding for a problem you do not have, and the monitoring is not.

How do I test a change without touching the vendor's live data?

Use a sandbox where the vendor offers one, and check whether its schema matches production before you trust a green result. Where there is no sandbox, the workable pattern is a disposable record you create and delete in the same run, tagged clearly enough that a human finding it in the CRM knows what it is. Keep a separate set of credentials for testing so a mistake is contained, run tests against a copy of the workflow rather than the live one, and get written agreement from whoever owns the account before anything scheduled starts writing into their production system.

The vendor emailed about a deprecation months ago. Why did it still surprise us?

Because the notice went to an inbox rather than to a system. Vendor announcements arrive as email to whoever created the account, at a moment when the change is not yet actionable, and they are read by a person who is not the person who will have to do the work. The fix is mechanical rather than cultural: route changelog and status feeds to a shared address that a team reads, record every pinned version and its announced end date in the same place you track other dated obligations, and let a contract test be the backstop for the notices that get missed anyway. Assume some will always get missed.

Should I just rebuild it in code instead?

Only if the reason is one of a short list: volume that makes per-operation pricing absurd, logic that has outgrown what a visual canvas can express readably, or a requirement for tests and review that the platform cannot give you. Rebuilding in code does not remove a single cause from the first half of this article — the vendor still deprecates, the token still expires, the page size still changes. What it changes is that the guards become ordinary code you can test, and the workflow becomes a diff. That is a real gain, and it arrives with a maintenance burden that a no-code platform was carrying for you.

None of the eight causes above is exotic, and none of the guards is clever. That is rather the point: the difference between an automation that quietly stops for three weeks and one that tells you within the hour is a handful of dull checks that nobody is excited to build on the day there is nothing to hear. If you recognised two of these in something you are running, the next step is an afternoon with a heartbeat and a count — or a conversation.
