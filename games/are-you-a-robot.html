What the squares are actually for

The puzzles lost the arms race

Distorted text died because software got better at reading it than people were — Google said in 2014, when it introduced the tick box, that its own recogniser handled the hardest variants with better than 99% accuracy. Traffic lights and crossings were the replacement, and machine vision has caught up with those too. The hard ones now stop tired humans more reliably than they stop scripts.

The modern ones score you, not your answer

reCAPTCHA v3 shows no puzzle at all. It returns a number between 0.0 and 1.0 and leaves the site to decide what to do with it; Cloudflare Turnstile works much the same way. What they weigh is how the pointer moved, how the typing was timed, what the browser looks like, which cookies you already had and what your address has been doing lately. A grid of squares appears mostly when that score is borderline, or because somebody wanted the visitor to see a check happening.

You were also doing unpaid work

The original reCAPTCHA showed you two words: one it knew, one that optical character recognition had failed on while digitising scanned books and newspaper archives. Your answer to the second was the product. Later it moved to Street View house numbers, then to labelling images. The test was training the very thing it was meant to be testing for.

And the cost lands on the wrong people

Image grids are hard or impossible with a screen reader, the audio alternative is worse, and low vision, dyslexia and slow connections all make it harder again. So do shared addresses and VPNs, which draw more challenges. The people who get shown the most squares are usually the ones already having the worst time, and none of it is what stops a determined attacker.

Questions people ask

Can I fail it?
No. Every screen accepts every answer, including no answer at all. That is the point being made rather than a shortcut — a fail state would teach the opposite of what the last screen says.
Does it record anything about me?
It counts your pointer moves, your clicks and the seconds you spent, in variables in your own tab, so the last screen can show you the one signal a real check would care about. The numbers are shown once and are gone when you reload. Nothing is stored and nothing is sent.
Are the pictures real photographs?
No, they are labelled boxes. Nothing loads over the network on this page. It also means a screen reader can read the challenge out, which real image grids manage badly if at all.
Could this be used as a real CAPTCHA?
No, and it would stop nothing. A script would clear all eight screens in milliseconds, which is roughly the situation genuine puzzle-based checks are in as well.
Why do real ones keep getting harder?
Because it is an arms race with a scoreboard only one side reads. Each time recognition software catches up, the puzzle gets more awkward, and the awkwardness is felt by people rather than by the software. That is why the newest systems gave up on puzzles and went to scoring behaviour instead.
