What these seven things actually are

Encoding is not encryption, and that is the useful part

Encryption needs a key, and without the key the output is no use to anybody. An encoding has no key at all — it is only a different way of writing the same characters, and reversing it is a one-click operation that the encoder in the Labs section will happily do for you. Binary, Base64 and ROT13 get mistaken for security constantly, usually by somebody who has just Base64-ed a password into a config file. All that hides it from is a casual glance.

Characters and bytes are different counts

The two numbers in the bar disagree the moment you leave ASCII. A plain Latin letter is one byte in UTF-8, an accented one is two, most Devanagari characters are three and an emoji is four. That is why a form with a 20-character limit sometimes rejects a name that is plainly shorter than twenty letters: somebody counted bytes and called them characters.

Every row is somewhere real

Hex is what a memory dump or a hex editor shows you. Base64 is how an image gets into an email or a data: URL. Morse is a telegraph code with no lower case, so anything it cannot carry is dropped and the line under it says how many. ROT13 was a Usenet convention for hiding spoilers. Leetspeak matters because password crackers have known the letter-to-digit swaps for thirty years — changing e to 3 buys you nothing.

Questions people ask

Is what I type sent anywhere?
No. The conversion happens in the page and there is no request of any kind. Close the tab and it is gone; nothing is stored either.
Can I turn the binary back into text?
Yes, and so can anyone else — that is the point being made. Paste any row into the encoder and decoder at /labs/encoding and it comes straight back.
Why does one emoji count as four bytes?
UTF-8 uses one byte for ASCII and up to four for everything else. An emoji sits far outside ASCII, so it needs all four, even though it is a single character and a single code point.
Why are some characters missing from the Morse row?
Morse only defines codes for A to Z, the digits and a handful of punctuation marks. There is no Morse for an emoji, an accented letter or a non-Latin script, so those characters are left out and counted underneath rather than quietly mangled.
Is ROT13 or leetspeak any use in a password?
No. Both are fixed, public substitutions, and every cracking tool applies them automatically to its wordlists. Substituting digits for letters in a dictionary word leaves you with a dictionary word.
