Big-O, measured rather than asserted

Every complexity table you have ever read is a claim somebody made. This page checks the claim on the machine in front of you: nine algorithms, each run over a ladder of growing inputs, each timed with a clock you can see the resolution of, each plotted as it is measured. Then it fits candidate curves to your numbers and tells you which class the data supports — including the times it cannot tell two classes apart, which is more often than a textbook admits.

  • Six complexity classes, timed live
  • Log–log axes: the slope is the exponent
  • A fitted verdict, with its own doubts
  • Warm-up passes, medians, timer calibration
  • Insertion against merge, and the crossover

What the picture is showing you

On log–log axes, the slope is the exponent

This is the whole reason the page exists. If time grows like np, then taking the logarithm of both axes turns the curve into a straight line of slope p. Linear work draws slope 1. Quadratic work draws slope 2. An algorithm you were told was linear and which measures 1.9 is not linear, and you can see that with a ruler rather than a proof. Switch the axes and watch a jumble of squashed curves become a fan of straight lines.

An exponential is the one that will not straighten

Power laws are straight on log–log; exponentials are not, and no amount of squinting makes them so. Put time on a log axis against a plain linear n and it is the other way round — the exponentials become straight and every polynomial flattens out. Flipping between those two views is a complete test for which family a curve belongs to, and it takes one click.

Constants are thrown away by the notation, not by the machine

Two algorithms in the same class draw parallel lines. The gap between those lines is the constant factor, and big-O deliberately discards it. That is the right abstraction for reasoning about growth and a terrible one for choosing what to ship, which is why bubble sort and insertion sort share a class, share a slope, and differ by a factor you would notice.

The verdict is allowed to say it does not know

The fit ranks every candidate class by how well it tracks your measurements, and when two of them are inside the noise it says so instead of picking one. Between five hundred and a hundred thousand elements the log in n log n only grows by about half, and for a fast algorithm that is smaller than the run-to-run variation. A tool that named a winner there would be inventing precision it does not have.

Everything that makes browser timing lie

All six of these are handled in the code. They are worth knowing about anyway, because they are the same six that will bite you the next time you benchmark something of your own.

JIT warm-up

A JavaScript engine runs unfamiliar code in a slow tier and only hands it to the optimising compiler once it has seen it run hot. The first few executions of anything are therefore unrepresentative, sometimes by an order of magnitude. Every cell here runs three passes that are thrown away before a single number is kept. Benchmarks that skip this step routinely measure the interpreter and report it as the algorithm.

The clock is deliberately blunt

Browsers coarsen performance.now() on purpose, because a high-resolution timer is half of a cache-timing side channel. Depending on the browser and whether the page is cross-origin isolated you may get five microseconds, a hundred microseconds, or a full millisecond. Timing a forty-nanosecond array index against that measures the clock. So each cell first works out how many repetitions it takes to fill at least two milliseconds, runs that many inside one timed region, and divides. The resolution it actually measured is printed in the panel.

Garbage collection, and everything else on the machine

A collection landing inside a timed region can triple it. So can a background tab waking up, or a laptop deciding to drop its clock speed. Each cell is run several times and the median is plotted, not the mean, because one bad sample drags an average and leaves a median alone. The chart draws a whisker from the fastest to the slowest run of every cell, so the noise is on screen rather than smoothed away behind your back.

Cache, which is a hidden log factor

The array-index workload does exactly the same number of operations at every size and still gets slower, because past a few hundred thousand elements the probes stop fitting in cache. The linear scan shows the same effect from the other side: at a thousand elements it all sits in L1 and the per-element cost is the lowest it will ever be, so the smallest points sit below the line and a fit that includes them reports something suspiciously sub-linear. That is why the fit drops them by default, and why the control that puts them back is on screen.

Dead code elimination

An optimiser that can prove a result is never observed is entitled to delete the work that produced it, and then you have benchmarked an empty loop. Every algorithm here returns a number, every number is accumulated, and the total is written into the page when a run finishes. It costs one addition per iteration, which is the honest price of measuring anything at all.

Background tabs are throttled

A hidden tab has its timers clamped and can be frozen outright. Numbers collected there are not comparable with numbers collected in the foreground, so a run in progress pauses when you switch away and resumes when you come back, and says so. The alternative — quietly collecting samples that mean something different — would poison the middle of a curve and leave no trace of why.

The crossover, and why real sort implementations have a threshold

Race insertion sort against merge sort. This is the part of the page with a practical answer at the end of it.

The worse algorithm wins first

Insertion sort is quadratic and merge sort is n log n, and for a small array insertion sort is faster — not slightly, but by a wide margin. It is one tight loop over one array: no recursion, no allocation, no comparator calls, and near-perfect locality. Merge sort has to pay for its better asymptotics up front, and below the crossover the input is not big enough to earn that back.

A crossover is a region, not a point

Near the crossing the two curves are within a few percent of each other, which is the same size as the noise, so the measured winner flips back and forth across several sizes. The lab reports that honestly: if it finds more than one sign change it gives you the range rather than a single confident number. A benchmark that reports a crossover to four significant figures has not looked at its own error bars.

Every serious sort library hard-codes the answer

V8’s TimSort sorts short runs with binary insertion sort rather than recursing into them, and libstdc++’s introsort stops recursing at sixteen elements and finishes the entire array with one insertion-sort pass at the end. Those thresholds are crossovers somebody measured, once, on the hardware of the day. You have just run the same experiment.

Same class does not mean same speed

Race bubble sort against insertion sort instead and there is no crossover at all: two quadratic curves run as parallel lines with a constant gap and never meet. That is the visual difference between a constant factor and a complexity class, and it is worth seeing once. If you want the mechanism rather than the timing, the algorithm visualizer animates the same sorts step by step with the comparison and swap counts ticking.

How far this can be trusted

Measuring is not proving

Big-O is a statement about behaviour as n goes to infinity. Three decades of input size is not infinity, and no amount of curve fitting over a finite range can settle an asymptotic claim. What the fit tells you is which class describes the data you have, which is a genuinely useful and genuinely smaller thing. The verdict is worded to keep that distinction rather than blur it.

It is your machine, not a reference machine

The absolute times depend on your CPU, your browser, your battery state and what else is running. What should survive all of that is the shape: the slopes, the ordering of the curves, and roughly where two of them cross. If the shape comes out wrong on your hardware, that is a result worth telling me about.

The Fibonacci one is not O(2ⁿ)

The naive recursion is almost always written down as O(2ⁿ) and that is not quite right: the call tree makes about φⁿ calls, where φ is the golden ratio, 1.618. The exponential fit here measures the base instead of assuming it, and lands near 1.61 rather than anywhere close to 2. The subset walk beside it is a genuine 2ⁿ and comes out within about a percent of 2.00, which is the control that shows the fit is reading your data rather than the label on the algorithm.

What it does not attempt

No worst-case or average-case analysis: every workload is run on one distribution of input, random for the sorts, and the shape of that input matters enormously — insertion sort on already-sorted data is linear. No memory profiling. No comparison against another machine, because there is nowhere to send the numbers to. The OS algorithms lab is the counterpart that counts operations exactly instead of timing them, and the compiler and VM lab is the layer below this one — the bytecode a loop actually turns into, which is where the constant factors are born. Neither replaces measuring.

Questions people ask

Are these numbers real, or a canned animation?
They are real. Every point on the chart is a timing taken on this device, in this tab, seconds ago: the algorithm runs over a ladder of growing input sizes, each size is measured several times, and the median is plotted. Nothing is precomputed and nothing is fetched. Close the page and the numbers are gone.
Why does the slope of the line matter so much?
Because on log-log axes a power law is a straight line whose slope is the exponent. A curve that measures slope 1 is linear, slope 2 is quadratic, and slope 1.1 is n log n wearing a disguise. It turns a question about asymptotics into something you read off a picture with a ruler, which is the one thing a table of complexity classes cannot do for you.
Why does it sometimes disagree with the textbook?
Usually because the range is too short to prove what the textbook is claiming. Between five hundred and a hundred thousand elements the log in n log n only grows by about half, and if the algorithm is fast that difference sits under the measurement noise, so O(n) and O(n log n) both fit. The verdict says so rather than picking a winner. The other common reason is the memory hierarchy: a genuinely constant-time array index gets slower as the array outgrows cache, and that curve is real even though the operation count never changed.
What are the warm-up runs and the median for?
A JavaScript engine runs new code in a slow tier and only optimises it once it has seen it run hot, so the first few executions are not representative and are thrown away. The median is there because a garbage collection or another tab waking up can triple a single sample, and one bad sample would drag an average. The chart draws a whisker from the fastest to the slowest run of each cell, so you can see the noise instead of taking my word for it.
Does insertion sort really beat merge sort?
Below a threshold, yes, and you can measure where. Insertion sort is one tight loop with no allocation and no recursion, and for a small array that beats an algorithm doing asymptotically less work. This is not trivia: V8 sorts short runs with binary insertion sort inside its TimSort, and libstdc++ stops recursing at sixteen elements and finishes with a single insertion-sort pass. The thresholds in those libraries are the crossover you just measured, decided once by somebody running this same experiment.
Why does the page not freeze while it is measuring?
Because the unit of work is one timed repetition, not one algorithm. Each repetition is scheduled as its own task, so between any two of them the browser gets to paint, scroll and notice that you pressed Stop. A single run that passes a 140 millisecond budget also stops its ladder climbing any further, which is why bubble sort gives up earlier than insertion sort on a slower machine, and the panel says when that has happened.
Will I get the same numbers you did?
No, and you will not get the same numbers twice in a row either. The absolute times depend on your CPU, your browser, your battery state and whatever else is running. What should survive all of that is the shape: the slopes, the ordering of the curves and roughly where two of them cross. If the shape comes out different on your machine I would genuinely like to hear about it.
