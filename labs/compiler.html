A compiler, and the machine it feeds

Every explanation of compilers I have read draws the stages as four boxes with arrows between them, and leaves the boxes empty. This one fills them with your program, all four at once: the source, the tokens it lexes into, the tree the parser builds, and the bytecode that comes out. Click anything in any pane and the other three light up the part that corresponds to it — that mapping is the point. Then hand the bytecode to the stack machine and step it, one instruction at a time, with the value stack and the call frames on screen.

  • A real lexer, parser and emitter
  • Four panes, one selection
  • A stack machine stepped one instruction at a time
  • Recursion, with the frames visible
  • Syntax errors with a caret, runtime errors with a call stack
  • Nothing uploaded, no server

Before you start

This runs entirely inside your browser — the lexer, the parser, the emitter and the machine that executes the result, all of it on your own device. Nothing is uploaded and there is no server.

Good to know

  • Everything runs on your device
  • The program you type is gone when you close the tab
  • Long runs stop at a limit rather than hanging the page
  • Nothing is uploaded or stored

Why it is here

  • Seeing the stages connected beats reading about them
  • Works offline once the page is cached
  • Free, permanently

What the four panes are showing

Tokens are the easy part

The lexer walks the text once, left to right, with a single character of lookahead and no idea what a program is. It cannot tell you that print print is nonsense, because deciding that is not its job — it will hand over two perfectly good keyword tokens and let the next stage refuse them. Almost every “unexpected token” message you have ever seen comes from the stage after this one.

The tree is where precedence lives

There is no precedence table anywhere in the parser. There is a chain of functions — equality calls comparison, which calls term, which calls factor — and each one binds more tightly than its caller. That chain is the precedence. Load the precedence example and compare the two trees: 2 + 3 * 4 and (2 + 3) * 4 are the same seven tokens in the same order, and the shape decides the answer.

A loop is a jump backwards

The emitter walks the tree once and writes instructions as it goes, which means it reaches a jump before it knows where the jump goes. So it writes a placeholder, carries on, and comes back to fill in the address once the branch is finished — backpatching, and the reason a single-pass compiler is possible at all. What comes out is not a loop or a conditional but two kinds of jump, and you can read the addresses.

The stack is the whole machine

There are no registers here. Every instruction takes its operands from the top of one stack and leaves its result there, and a local variable is nothing more than a slot in that same stack at a fixed offset from the current frame. Declaring one costs no instruction whatsoever, which surprises people until they watch it happen. A call pushes a frame; a return throws one away and puts the result where the callee used to be.

Questions people ask

Is this a real compiler?
It is a real front end and a real back end for a small language: a hand-written lexer, a recursive descent parser, a single-pass emitter and a stack machine that executes the bytecode. Nothing is handed to the browser's own compiler, and nothing is eval'd. What it is not is an optimiser, and it does not target a real processor - the machine it compiles for is the one running in the pane beside it.
Which language is this?
One I defined for this page. It has numbers, strings, booleans, variables, arithmetic with the usual precedence, comparisons, if and else, while, functions with parameters and recursion, and print. That is enough to write fibonacci and greatest common divisor, which is enough to make the call frames worth watching.
Why bytecode instead of walking the tree?
A tree-walking interpreter would be shorter and would hide the two things most worth seeing. Bytecode makes control flow explicit: a while loop becomes a jump backwards, an if becomes a jump forwards over the branch you did not take, and the instruction pointer has something to point at.
What happens when a program is wrong?
A syntax error stops the pipeline where it broke and shows the offending line with a caret under the exact column. A runtime error stops the machine and shows the call stack as it stood at that instant, innermost frame first. Both come out of the same code paths the working case uses; neither is a canned message. Two of the example programs are broken on purpose so you can see each.
What is deliberately missing?
Closures, objects, arrays, garbage collection, and any optimisation at all. Functions are declared at the top level and see only their own parameters and locals, so there is no environment chain to draw and no function values to pass around. Each of those is a chapter of its own, and adding them would bury the pipeline this page exists to show.
Why does it stop after so many instructions?
Every step is recorded so that stepping backwards is exact rather than a re-simulation, and that record has to fit in memory. The machine stops at 25,000 instructions, at 64 call frames and at 512 values on the stack, and says which. An endless loop therefore ends as a message with the stack still readable rather than as a frozen tab.
