Colour contrast, with the arithmetic shown

Nobody believes a contrast number until they see where it came from. So this one shows the whole chain: two colours, three channels each, through the sRGB transfer function into a relative luminance, and out as a ratio. Then it shows you what that ratio still cannot tell you.

  • Every step of the WCAG 2.x formula
  • AA and AAA, text and UI
  • APCA alongside, not as a verdict
  • Four colour-vision simulations
  • Smallest fix that passes

Before you start

This tool runs entirely inside your browser. Nothing you paste or drop here is uploaded, logged or stored — which for security work is the whole point, not a footnote.

What it does not do

  • No uploads — there is no server to upload to
  • No account, no history, nothing saved
  • No judgement about APCA — that number is shown, never enforced
  • No lookups against any external database

Why that matters here

  • Evidence files stay on your machine
  • A token or key you paste is never transmitted
  • Nothing you paste ever appears in your Network tab
  • Works with no internet once the page is cached
  • Free, permanently

Hex, rgb(), hsl() and oklch() are all understood, along with a short list of colour names. Anything else is refused rather than guessed at.

The smallest change that passes

          
The move happens in OKLCH, where lightness is roughly
            perceptual, so nudging it does not swing the hue the way nudging an HSL
            lightness does. Hue is held exactly. Chroma is kept unless sRGB cannot hold
            it at the new lightness, and if it has to fall the panel says by how much.

          

        


        

          
The same pair under four colour-vision models

          
Each panel re-renders the pair, a filled button and a
            green/red status pair through one model. The green and red are fixed at
            #16a34a and #dc2626 on purpose: they pass contrast
            against most backgrounds and still collapse into one colour under two of these
            models, which is the entire argument against carrying meaning in hue alone.

Where the number comes from

        

        

          

            
The transfer function is piecewise

            
sRGB is not a plain 2.2 gamma. Near black it is a straight line, c / 12.92, and above 0.04045 it becomes ((c + 0.055) / 1.055) raised to 2.4. The linear segment exists because a pure power curve has infinite slope at zero, which quantises badly in eight bits. The output pane marks which branch each channel took, because the near-black one is where hand-rolled implementations quietly go wrong.

          

          

            
The 0.05 is glare, not maths

            
Both luminances get + 0.05 before the division. That is a flare term — an allowance for ambient light bouncing off the screen and lifting the blacks. It is why black on white comes out at 21:1 rather than infinity, and why the formula stops discriminating usefully between very dark colours: below the flare term, the difference is drowned.

          

          

            
Green does almost all of the work

            
The weights are the Rec. 709 luminance coefficients: 0.2126 red, 0.7152 green, 0.0722 blue. Change the blue channel of a colour by fifty and the ratio barely moves; change the green by ten and it does. This is also why pure blue text on black is a disaster the formula agrees with and pure blue on white is a disaster it does not.

          

          

            
Three thresholds, not one

            
4.5:1 for normal text at AA, 3:1 for large text — 24 px, or 18.66 px at bold — and 3:1 for UI components and meaningful graphics under SC 1.4.11. AAA raises the first two to 7:1 and 4.5:1 and adds nothing for components. Logotypes and genuinely disabled controls are exempt, which is a real exemption and not a place to hide a low-contrast button.

          

        

      


      

        

          
Where WCAG 2.x is known to be wrong

          
This is not a fringe complaint. The 2.x formula has understood limitations, they are widely acknowledged, and they are worst in exactly the place modern interfaces live.

        

        

          

            
It cannot tell which one is the text

            
The formula sorts the two luminances and divides. That makes it symmetric: dark grey on white and white on dark grey score identically. Your eye does not agree. Light text on a dark ground blooms — the glyph spreads into its background — so the same ratio reads thinner and harder. Every dark theme that measured fine and felt wrong is this effect.

          

          

            
It was calibrated for a different room

            
The thresholds came out of work on paper-like reading conditions and were fixed in 2008. They do not model the light behind you, an OLED panel with true blacks, or a phone at maximum brightness in daylight. A single flare constant stands in for all of it. That is not a scandal — a standard has to draw a line somewhere — but it is worth knowing which line you are standing on.

          

          

            
What APCA does differently

            
APCA drops the piecewise curve for a plain 2.4 power, softly clamps very dark values instead of letting a flare constant do it, and uses different exponents depending on polarity, so light-on-dark and dark-on-light are genuinely different calculations. The output is an Lc value, roughly ±0 to ±108, read against a table of sizes and weights rather than one pass mark.

          

          

            
Its status, plainly

            
APCA is a candidate method inside WCAG 3, which is a W3C working draft with no target date and no conformance model you can be audited against. The algorithm itself has revised several times; this page implements APCA-W3 0.1.9. Design with it if it helps you argue about a dark theme. Do not write it into a contract, and do not tell a client they failed it.

          

        

      


      

        

          
What a passing ratio does not guarantee

          
The formula takes six numbers — two colours, three channels each. Everything below is outside it, and every one of these has made readable text unreadable in something I have worked on.

        

        

          

            
Weight and size

            
A 300-weight face at 4.6:1 can be harder to read than a 600-weight one at 4.3:1. WCAG 2.x has exactly one size input — whether the text counts as large — and no weight input at all. If your body copy is set in a thin face, the ratio is telling you less than you think it is.

          

          

            
Anti-aliasing and the panel

            
Sub-pixel rendering, greyscale smoothing and the OS text stack all change how much ink actually lands. The same hex pair renders visibly differently between macOS and Windows, and between an LCD and an OLED. Light text on a true black OLED blooms more than the same pair on an IPS panel with a grey floor.

          

          

            
The room, and the person

            
Sunlight on a phone screen wipes out contrast the formula never modelled. So does a dimmed laptop in a dark room, in the other direction. And the thresholds were set against a population baseline: someone with low vision, cataracts, or simply forty extra years of eye may need considerably more than 4.5:1 on the same page.

          

          

            
The background it actually sits on

            
A ratio needs one opaque background colour. Real interfaces put text on gradients, photographs, translucent overlays and cards that tint with the page. This tool refuses a translucent background rather than inventing one, and composites a translucent foreground over the colour you gave it and says so. A checker that silently assumes white is where most wrong numbers come from.

          

          

            
Colour used as meaning

            
Two colours can pass every threshold against their background and still be indistinguishable from each other. Red and green status dots are the classic case, and the simulation panels above show it collapsing. The fix is never a better ratio; it is a shape, a label, or a position that carries the meaning as well.

          

          

            
Everything that is not text

            
Focus rings, chart series, disabled-looking-but-not-disabled controls, placeholder text that turns out to be the only label. SC 1.4.11 covers some of this at 3:1 and a lot of it falls between criteria. A page can pass every automated contrast check and still be unusable with a keyboard because the focus ring is 1.4:1 against the button it sits on.

          

        

      


      

        

          
How I checked this, and what I did not

        

        

          

            
Against this site’s own measurements

            
The stylesheets here carry measured contrast numbers in their comments, written down when the light theme was corrected. This tool reproduces them: the muted text in the typing lab is noted as 4.74:1 on #020617 and computes as 4.74:1, and white on #0284c7 is noted as 4.1:1 and computes as 4.10:1. Those notes and the rest of the working are in the colophon.

          

          

            
The self-check in the toolbar

            
Nine assertions run when the page loads, and the toolbar shows the count. Black on white is 21:1; white has luminance 1 and black 0; a colour against itself is 1:1; the ratio is symmetric; APCA is signed by polarity and zero when there is no difference; and OKLCH round-trips six colours back to the same bytes, which matters because every suggested fix is a trip through that space and back.

          

          

            
APCA is not vector-checked

            
I implemented APCA-W3 0.1.9 from its published constants and I have not run it against the reference test suite line by line. The self-check only asserts its structural behaviour. That is why it is presented as a comparison and never as a verdict — a green tick for something I only eyeballed would be worse than no tick at all.

          

          

            
The simulations are a model

            
Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009), severity 1.0, applied in linear light because that is the space they were derived in. Achromatopsia is not from that paper — it is Rec. 709 luminance written to all three channels. All four are dichromacy at full strength, and most colour vision deficiency is milder than that, so treat them as a lower bound on what people see.

Questions people ask

        

          

            Is this the number an auditor will use?
            
For WCAG 2.1 and 2.2, yes. Success criteria 1.4.3, 1.4.6 and 1.4.11 are all defined against this exact formula, and the page computes it straight through with no rounding until the last line. Where two checkers disagree it is almost never the arithmetic - it is that one of them guessed at a background the CSS never actually resolves to.

          

          

            Why is APCA here at all if it does not count?
            
Because it explains failures you can already see. WCAG 2.x scores light-on-dark and dark-on-light identically, and anyone who has built a dark theme knows they do not look identical. APCA computes a different curve for each polarity, which matches that experience much better. It is still a draft method inside a draft standard, so I show the number and refuse to turn it into a pass or a fail.

          

          

            Can I be failed for ignoring the APCA score?
            
No, and you cannot claim conformance with it either. WCAG 3 is a working draft with no target date, and APCA inside it is a candidate method that has already changed several times. Use it to argue about a dark theme with your designer. Conform against WCAG 2.2, and put that in the contract.

          

          

            Does passing 4.5:1 mean the text is readable?
            
No. The formula takes two colours and nothing else. It never sees the typeface, the weight, the size beyond the large-text cut-off, the anti-aliasing, the panel, or the light in the room. Thin 300-weight text at 4.6:1 can be meaningfully harder to read than 600-weight at 4.3:1, and no contrast ratio can tell you that.

          

          

            Are the colour-blindness simulations accurate?
            
They are the Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes matrices at full severity, applied in linear light, which is the usual way to do this and is still a model of dichromacy rather than a window into anyone's eyes. Most colour vision deficiency is anomalous rather than absent, so real people generally see more than these panels show. Use them to find meaning that is carried by hue alone, not as a rendering of somebody's experience.
