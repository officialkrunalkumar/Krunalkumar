Email header analyzer

The From: line is decoration — anyone can write anything there. The Received: chain and the authentication results are what actually record where a message came from, and those are what this reads.

  • Full Received: hop chain with timing
  • SPF, DKIM and DMARC verdicts
  • Reply-To mismatch detection
  • Originating IP
  • Headers never uploaded

Before you start

This tool runs entirely inside your browser. Nothing you paste or drop here is uploaded, logged or stored — which for security work is the whole point, not a footnote.

What it does not do

  • No uploads — there is no server to upload to
  • No account, no history, nothing saved
  • No WHOIS, geolocation or reputation lookups
  • No DNS queries — SPF and DKIM results are read, not re-checked

Why that matters here

  • Evidence files stay on your machine
  • A token or key you paste is never transmitted
  • Nothing you paste ever appears in your Network tab
  • Works with no internet once the page is cached
  • Free, permanently

Received headers read bottom to top

            
Each server prepends its own line as the message passes through, so the last entry in the raw headers is the first hop chronologically. This tool reverses them for you and shows the gap between each pair — a large gap means the message sat in a queue or a filter, and a negative one means the chain was forged or the clocks disagree.

          

          

            
What SPF, DKIM and DMARC each prove

            
SPF says the sending server was authorised to send for that domain. DKIM says the message is cryptographically signed and unmodified. DMARC says the domain owner accepts the combination. All three passing proves the message really came from that domain — not that it is honest. A lookalike domain registered yesterday passes all three too.

          

          

            
The Reply-To mismatch

            
A From: line showing a familiar name and a Reply-To on a different domain is the standard setup for business email compromise. The recipient sees the trusted name, and their reply goes to the attacker. This is flagged in red.

          

          

            
Why nothing is looked up

            
A WHOIS query, a geolocation call or a reputation check all tell a third party which address you are investigating, and in a targeted case that leaks the investigation itself. The originating IP is extracted and shown; what you do with it is your call.

          

        

      


      

        

          
Questions people ask

        

          

            Where do I find the raw headers?
            
Gmail: open the message, click the three-dot menu, choose "Show original". Outlook: File, Properties, then the Internet headers box. Apple Mail: View, Message, All Headers. Thunderbird: View, Headers, All.

          

          

            Are my headers uploaded?
            
No. They are parsed in your browser. This matters more than usual here, because headers carry internal hostnames, IP addresses and recipient addresses - most popular header analysers are web services that receive all of it.

          

          

            Does SPF pass mean the email is safe?
            
No. It means the sending server was authorised for that domain. An attacker who registers a lookalike domain gets SPF, DKIM and DMARC passes on their own domain. Authentication proves origin, not honesty.

          

          

            Can Received headers be faked?
            
The ones added by servers you trust cannot, but a sender can inject fake ones before the message reaches those servers. Only the hops added after the message entered infrastructure you trust are reliable, which is why the timing checks matter.

          

          

            Why is the originating IP private?
            
If the first hop shows a 10.x, 192.168.x or 172.16-31.x address, the message started inside a private network and the public IP appears at the next hop out.
