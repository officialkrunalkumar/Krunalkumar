Email security audit

Whether somebody can send mail pretending to be a domain comes down to a handful of public DNS records. This reads them, explains what each one actually enforces, and grades the result — without touching the target’s servers at all.

  • SPF parsed, including the 10-lookup limit
  • DMARC policy and alignment
  • Optional DKIM selector sweep
  • MTA-STS and BIMI
  • Public DNS only

This tool uses the network

Most of Labs runs with no server at all. This one is different, and the notice is different to match: using it sends a request to Google Public DNS, and they will see it.

What leaves your browser

  • They see which domain you are auditing, and your IP address.
  • Your IP address, as with any request you make
  • Nothing is sent until you press the button
  • Five DNS queries for the audit; the DKIM sweep adds sixteen more

What still holds

  • No proxy — the request is not relayed through me
  • I never see it, log it or store it
  • No account, no history, no cookies sent
  • Every request is echoed on screen as it goes out
  • Free, permanently

If you are investigating something sensitive, that matters: Google Public DNS learns which domain you looked up and roughly when. Agreeing here does not affect the offline tools, and agreeing to those did not bring you through this notice.

What each record does

        

        

          

            
SPF says who may send

            
A list of servers authorised to send for the domain, ending in a default. -all tells receivers to reject anything else; ~all says accept but mark; ?all enforces nothing. There is a hard limit of ten DNS lookups — exceed it and SPF fails to evaluate at all, which is worse than a permissive record.

          

          

            
DMARC decides what happens on failure

            
Without DMARC, SPF and DKIM results are advisory and receivers mostly deliver anyway. p=none is monitoring only and protects nothing; it is the right starting point and the wrong resting point. p=reject is where a domain that cares ends up.

          

          

            
DKIM cannot be enumerated

            
A DKIM key lives at {selector}._domainkey.{domain}, and the selector is an arbitrary label chosen by whoever set it up. The sweep guesses sixteen common ones; finding nothing proves only that none of those existed. The reliable way is to read the s= tag in a real message’s DKIM-Signature header.

          

          

            
Domains that send no mail still need records

            
A parked or web-only domain with no SPF and no DMARC is a free spoofing target. Publishing v=spf1 -all and p=reject costs nothing and closes it.

          

        

      


      

        

          
Questions people ask

        

          

            Does this send any email or touch their servers?
            
No. It reads public DNS records, which is exactly what every receiving mail server does before accepting a message. Nothing is sent to the domain and no port is touched.

          

          

            Why does it say DKIM not found for a domain I know uses DKIM?
            
Because DKIM selectors cannot be enumerated. The tool guesses sixteen common selectors; a domain using a custom one will show nothing. This is a limitation of DNS, not a bug.

          

          

            What does p=none actually protect against?
            
Nothing. It only asks receivers to send reports. It is the correct first step while you find your legitimate senders, but a domain left at p=none indefinitely is unprotected.

          

          

            Is a passing audit a guarantee nobody can spoof the domain?
            
It means direct spoofing of the exact domain is hard. It does nothing about lookalike domains, display-name spoofing, or compromised legitimate accounts.

          

          

            Can I audit a domain I do not own?
            
Yes, and it is entirely legitimate: every record read here is public DNS that any mail server on the internet consults automatically.
