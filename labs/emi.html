Loan EMI, and the four things the brochure stops short of

Every bank site has an EMI calculator and every one of them ends at the same place: one number a month, and a pie chart. The parts that decide what a loan actually costs are never on that page — how little of an early instalment touches the debt, which prepayment option saves three times more than the other, what a rate rise quietly does to your tenure, and how a processing fee makes a lower headline rate the dearer loan. This one keeps going.

The four things this page exists for

The first instalment barely touches the debt

Interest is charged each month on whatever is still owed, and at the start that is the entire loan. On thirty lakh over twenty years at a normal rate, the first payment is about eighty-two per cent interest and knocks under five thousand rupees off the balance. Principal does not overtake interest until roughly the twelfth year. The chart draws that for every instalment, and the shape is more persuasive than the sentence.

Two prepayment answers, and one is far better

Hand a lender extra money and it will ask whether you want a smaller instalment or an earlier finish. Most people say smaller instalment, because that is the one you can feel. On the default loan here, cutting the tenure saves about three and a half times what cutting the EMI saves — on exactly the same money. Both are computed side by side, so you are choosing rather than guessing.

A rate rise usually arrives as extra months

When a floating rate moves up, the lender’s default is to leave your instalment exactly where it is and add months to the end. It needs no signature and it changes nothing you can see on your bank statement, which is why so few people notice. A one-point rise two years into a twenty-year loan can add four years. Raising the EMI instead costs more per month and far less overall, and the lab prices both.

A rate is not a price

A processing fee is money you borrowed and never received, so it makes the loan more expensive than the advertised rate says. The comparison tab prices the whole cashflow — what actually reached your account against what actually leaves it — and reports the rate that balances the two. That number is regularly a quarter point above the one on the poster, and it is the one worth comparing.

How the arithmetic is done

None of this is difficult. It is only hidden, which is a different problem, so here is all of it.

One formula, printed on the page

The EMI is the standard annuity payment: principal times the monthly rate times (1 + r) to the power n, divided by the same power minus one. Every lender in the world uses it. The lab prints it with your numbers substituted, so you can check the result by hand, and it prints the zero-rate case too, where the whole thing collapses into a division.

The schedule is walked, not approximated

There is no closed-form shortcut in here. Each month the engine charges interest on the balance that exists, subtracts what the payment covers, applies any extra you have paid, and moves on. That is the only honest way to model a prepayment landing in month thirteen, a rate change in month twenty-five and a recast instalment afterwards, because all three of them break the closed form.

Rounding is done the way a lender does it

The instalment is rounded to the rupee, interest is charged to the paise, and the final payment is whatever clears the balance rather than the EMI. That is why the last row is never a round number and why a closure statement never matches the sanction letter. Where an option exists to hold the end date — a recast after a prepayment — the new instalment is rounded up, so the loan cannot slip a month to a rounding error.

The effective rate is solved, not estimated

The effective cost is the internal rate of return of the real cashflow, found by bisecting on the discount rate until the present value of every instalment equals the money that actually reached your account. Eighty halvings put it far past any precision worth quoting. There is a self-test in the module: with no fee it must reproduce the nominal rate exactly, and adding a fee must raise it.

How far this can be trusted

The arithmetic is right. Whether it describes your loan is a separate question, and these are the places the two come apart.

Your contract wins, always

This models a monthly reducing balance with a level instalment, which is what the overwhelming majority of retail loans are. Lenders still differ on rounding, on daily versus monthly rests, and on the broken period between disbursal and the first instalment. Those move the schedule by rupees, not by thousands, but the sanction letter is the document that binds and this page is not.

What it deliberately does not model

Staged disbursal on an under-construction property, and the pre-EMI interest you pay in the meantime. Moratoriums and step-up instalment plans. Insurance premiums funded into the loan. Prepayment penalties, which a floating-rate loan to an individual generally does not carry and a fixed-rate one generally does. Any of those changes the answer, and none of them is guessed at here.

It says nothing about whether to borrow

Prepaying is not automatically right. Money spent clearing a cheap home loan is money not earning anywhere else, and after tax relief the comparison is closer than it looks. Beating a home loan rate is possible; beating a personal loan or a credit card rate is not, and that is the one case where the answer is not really in doubt. This page prices the loan. What to do about it is yours.

The tax panel is the least stable part

It is switched off until you turn it on, and it applies only to a home loan under the old regime. The ceilings are hard-coded and budgets move them; the new regime, default since FY 2023-24, gives you neither on a self-occupied house. Pre-construction interest, 80EE and 80EEA, joint ownership and surcharge are all outside it. Same rule as the salary breakdown lab: confirm it against the current year, and it is arithmetic, not advice.

Questions people ask

Is anything I type here sent anywhere?
No. There is no server behind this page and no network call anywhere in the code: no upload, no lookup, no analytics on the numbers you enter. A loan amount, a salary and a tax slab are exactly the details people hand to a random website without thinking about it, and this one has nowhere to send them even if it wanted to. Close the tab and the numbers are gone.
Reduce the EMI or reduce the tenure, which should I pick?
Cutting the tenure almost always saves more, often several times more, and the lab computes both on your own numbers so you can see the gap rather than take my word for it. The reason is that interest is charged on the balance, so a rupee taken off the balance early stops accruing interest for every month that was left. Cutting the EMI hands part of that saving back by keeping the debt alive for the full original term. The honest case for cutting the EMI is cash flow, not cost: a smaller instalment is what you want when the monthly payment is the thing straining.
My lender raised the rate and my EMI did not change. Is that good?
It means the tenure moved instead, which is the default at almost every lender and needs no signature from you. It is not a favour. The same rise absorbed by a longer tenure costs considerably more in total interest than the same rise absorbed by a higher instalment, and the rate-change tab prices both on your loan. After any repricing, look at your statement for the field that actually changed.
Will my bank’s numbers match these exactly?
The EMI will, to the rupee, because everybody uses the same annuity formula. The schedule can drift by a few rupees, because lenders differ on how they round, on whether interest is computed on a daily or a monthly reducing balance, and on what they do with the broken period between disbursal and your first instalment. Those gaps are small and they are not the point. The shape is the point. The sanction letter is the document that binds, not this page.
Why is the effective rate higher than the rate I was quoted?
Because a fee is money you borrowed and never received. The effective rate here is the internal rate of return of the actual cashflow: what reached your account on day one, against what leaves it every month until the loan closes. Two loans can carry the same headline rate and different prices, and a loan with a lower rate and a larger fee is regularly the dearer of the two.
The total cost and the effective rate sometimes disagree. Which one is right?
The effective rate, and the disagreement is worth understanding. Adding up rupees across twenty years treats money paid today and money paid in year nineteen as the same thing, and they are not. A fee is paid in full on day one; the interest it buys you out of arrives slowly. Discounting them, which is what the effective rate does, is the arithmetic your lender uses on its own side of the deal. The lab shows both figures and says so when they point different ways.
Can I trust the tax panel?
Treat it as arithmetic, not advice. I am not a tax professional. The ceilings are written into the code, two lakh under section 24(b) for a self-occupied house and one and a half lakh under 80C, and budgets move those numbers. It also assumes the old regime, which has not been the default since FY 2023-24 and gives you neither deduction on a self-occupied house under the new one. Confirm every figure against the current year. What the panel is genuinely useful for is seeing which years the deduction is worth anything in, which are the early ones, where the interest is largest.
What is in the CSV export?
Every instalment of whichever scenario you exported: the month, the opening balance, the payment, how that payment split between interest and principal, any extra you paid, and the closing balance. The numbers are written plain, with no currency symbol and no thousands separators, so a spreadsheet reads the columns as numbers rather than as text it has to be argued with. The file is assembled in your browser and handed over through a blob URL, so it is never uploaded anywhere.
