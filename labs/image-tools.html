Compress, convert and resize images without uploading them

Search for an image compressor and every result asks you to upload the picture first. Your photographs, your client’s floor plans, a scan of a document — on a stranger’s disk before the progress bar finishes. None of that is technically necessary. Browsers have shipped a competent image encoder for over a decade, so this page uses yours: drop files in, and the work happens on your machine.

  • JPEG, PNG, WebP and AVIF
  • Before and after, with real byte counts
  • Resize by size, percentage or target
  • Batch, zipped in the browser
  • Nothing is uploaded, ever

Before you start

This tool runs entirely inside your browser. Nothing you paste or drop here is uploaded, logged or stored — which for security work is the whole point, not a footnote.

What it does not do

  • No uploads — there is no server to upload to
  • No account, no history, nothing saved
  • No format this browser has no encoder for
  • No HEIC output, and HEIC input only in Safari
  • 60 MB per file, 40 files at a time

Why that matters here

  • Evidence files stay on your machine
  • A photograph of someone is never transmitted
  • Nothing you drop ever appears in your Network tab
  • Works with no internet once the page is cached
  • Free, permanently
Aspect ratio

Stripping removes the camera and lens model, the body serial number, the capture time to the second and the GPS coordinates. If you would like to see what is in a photograph of yours before deciding, the EXIF viewer shows the whole block and decodes the coordinates.

The queue

    What happened to each file

    
          
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    
          
    The pitch
    
          
    The upload was never the technical part
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            
    What you are actually agreeing to
    
            
    Uploading a photograph to a compressor hands a copy to a company you have not read the terms of, over a connection you cannot audit, to be written to a disk you cannot see and deleted on a schedule you are asked to take on trust. For a holiday snap that is a small risk. For a client’s site plan, a medical form, a passport scan or a photograph of a child, it is not a small risk, and it is the one everybody takes because the tool asked politely.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    The browser has done this since 2011
    
            
    canvas.toBlob gives every page a JPEG and PNG encoder, and current browsers add WebP and, in some, AVIF. That is the same libjpeg-turbo family of encoders a server-side tool would reach for. So the upload buys you nothing in quality; it buys the operator a copy of your file and a page of adverts around the download button.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    What a server can genuinely do better
    
            
    Two things, and I would rather name them than pretend. MozJPEG and libaom tuned hard will beat a browser encoder by a few per cent at the same visual quality, and a server can decode HEIC, which only Safari does here. If those few per cent matter more to you than where the file goes, use a desktop tool like ImageMagick or cjpeg — also local, also nothing uploaded.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    Metadata is the part that follows the file
    
            
    Re-encoding through a canvas discards every metadata segment, because a canvas has nowhere to put one. That is a privacy feature by accident, and here it is the default on purpose. The keep option exists because photographers legitimately want the copyright and camera fields to survive, and it works by splicing the original EXIF block back into the JPEG — with the orientation tag reset, or the picture would rotate twice.
    
          
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    
          
    Finding the point where quality stops paying
    
          
    This is what the split view is for. Not to prove that compression works, but to find the setting where the file has stopped getting meaningfully smaller and has not yet started looking worse.
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            
    The curve is not a straight line
    
            
    Between quality 100 and about 85 a JPEG loses most of its size and almost none of its appearance. Between 85 and 70 it keeps shrinking and the damage stays hidden in textured areas. Below about 60 the block edges arrive in flat regions — skies, skin, gradients — and once you have seen them you cannot unsee them. The interesting number sits near the knee, and it moves with the picture.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    Look at 1:1, not at a fitted preview
    
            
    A photograph shrunk to fit a 900-pixel box hides exactly the artefacts you are trying to judge, because the downscale averages them away. That is why there is a 1:1 toggle: it draws one image pixel per device pixel, centred, on both sides of the divider. Judge quality there, then set the slider, then look at the byte count.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    Generation loss is real and cumulative
    
            
    Every lossy encode throws information away permanently. Re-saving a JPEG at quality 90 does not restore it to quality 90; it adds a second generation of loss on top of whatever the first encoder did, and makes a bigger file while doing it. Work from the largest original you still have, resize and compress once, and keep that original somewhere.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    Format matters more than quality often does
    
            
    WebP at quality 80 is routinely 25–35% smaller than JPEG at a visually matched setting, and AVIF smaller again where the browser can write it. But a WebP emailed to somebody on an old phone may not open, and AVIF encoding in a browser is slow enough that you will notice it on a large photograph. Pick for the destination, not for the benchmark.
    
          
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    
          
    The awkward cases, said out loud
    
          
    Every one of these has produced a wrong-looking file in some tool I have used. They are handled here, and this is what is being done about each.
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            
    An image too large for a canvas
    
            
    Browsers cap both the largest canvas dimension and the total pixel area, and the failure is silent: the canvas allocates, drawing does nothing, and you get a blank image with a plausible file size. So the corner pixel is written and read back before any real work starts. If it fails, the tool bisects downwards to find roughly what this browser will accept at your aspect ratio and prints that number, so you know what to resize to.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    An orientation tag that must be respected
    
            
    A phone usually stores the sensor’s raster and a tag saying which way up it was. A canvas cannot carry that tag, so a naive resizer produces a file that is sideways everywhere. Rather than trusting the decoder, this reads the tag and the stored raster size out of the JPEG itself and compares them with what came back: if the decoder rotated the pixels, nothing more is needed; if it did not, the transform is applied here.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    A progressive JPEG
    
            
    Progressive files encode the image in successive passes so it sharpens as it loads, and they are usually a little smaller than baseline. No browser canvas encoder writes progressive output, so a progressive original comes back baseline. That is often a slightly larger file at the same quality, which would otherwise look like a bug. The tool detects the SOF2 marker in the source and says so on that row.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    A codec this browser does not have
    
            
    canvas.toBlob does not report an unsupported format — it hands back a PNG and lets you name it whatever you like. The page probes each encoder on a 2×2 scratch canvas at load and disables what is missing, with the reason on the option. On the decode side, HEIC from an iPhone opens only in Safari, and the error says that rather than “invalid file”.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    Transparency going into a JPEG
    
            
    JPEG has no alpha channel. Left alone, a canvas hands transparent pixels to the encoder as black, so a logo on a transparent ground comes back on a black square. Here the canvas is filled white first, which is what almost everyone wants, and the row says the flattening happened. If you need the transparency, keep PNG or WebP.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    A file that comes out bigger
    
            
    This happens constantly and most tools hide it. If the original was saved at quality 60 and you ask for 90, the result is larger and slightly worse. If you ask for PNG output from a photograph, it can be five times larger. The row shows a growth rather than a saving, in a different colour, with the likely reason, because a compressor that silently returns a bigger file has failed at its one job.
    
          
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    
          
    How the batch ZIP is built
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            
    A ZIP is a much simpler container than its reputation suggests. Each member gets a local file header — a signature, a version, some flags, the compression method, a DOS timestamp, a CRC-32 of the contents and two lengths — followed by the bytes themselves. After the last member comes a central directory repeating that information with an offset to each local header, and after that a twenty-two byte end-of-central-directory record saying how many members there are and where the directory starts. That is the whole format for this purpose, and it is written out here in about eighty lines.
    
            
    Every member is stored rather than deflated. That sounds lazy and is actually the right call: JPEG, WebP, PNG and AVIF payloads are already entropy-coded, so deflate finds close to nothing in them — a fraction of a per cent, in exchange for shipping a compression library to every visitor of this page. Method 0 is a first-class part of the specification, and every unarchiver on every platform opens the result. The only thing the writer refuses is an archive past four gigabytes or 65,535 members, which needs ZIP64 records it does not emit; the per-file and per-batch caps make that unreachable, but refusing beats writing something that opens as garbage.
    
          
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    
          
    Questions people ask
    
        
    
          
    
            Is my image uploaded?
            
    No. The file is read, decoded, resized and re-encoded by your own browser, and the ZIP is assembled in the same tab. There is no upload step and no server to upload to. Open your Network tab while you use it - that is the only proof worth anything, and it is the reason this page exists at all.
    
          
    
          
    
            Which formats can this browser actually write?
            
    It depends on the browser, so the page probes at load rather than promising. JPEG and PNG are everywhere. WebP encoding is in every current browser. AVIF encoding is not - it is in Chromium-based browsers and, at the time of writing, not in Safari or Firefox. Any format this browser cannot write is disabled in the menu with the reason attached, rather than quietly coming back as a PNG wearing the wrong extension.
    
          
    
          
    
            Why did my file get bigger?
            
    Almost always because the original was already compressed harder than the setting you asked for. Re-encoding a JPEG that was saved at quality 60 at quality 90 produces a larger file that looks slightly worse, because nothing can recover what the first encoder threw away. Asking for PNG output from a photograph does the same thing more dramatically. The row tells you it grew instead of hiding it.
    
          
    
          
    
            Does resizing here lose quality?
            
    Yes, and unavoidably. Making an image smaller throws pixels away, and every lossy re-encode is another generation of loss on top of the first. So do it once, from the largest original you have, rather than repeatedly from copies of copies. If you only need the file smaller and not the dimensions, lower the quality and leave the dimensions alone.
    
          
    
          
    
            How is the ZIP made without a library?
            
    Field by field: a local file header per member, a central directory listing them all, and an end-of-central-directory record. Every member is stored rather than deflated, because JPEG, WebP, PNG and AVIF payloads are already entropy-coded and compressing them a second time saves a fraction of a percent in exchange for a megabyte of library. It is an ordinary ZIP and every unarchiver opens it.
