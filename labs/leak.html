What your browser tells every website

None of this needed a permission prompt. Every number below was read the moment you opened the page, and any site you visit can read the same.

  • Your public IP, via WebRTC
  • Your address on your own network
  • GPU, time zone, cores, screen
  • A fingerprint that survives clearing cookies
  • None of it reaches this site — though finding your public IP means asking a STUN server, so Google and Cloudflare see it

Your addresses

A page can open a WebRTC connection to nobody, collect the addresses the browser offers, and read them straight out. No prompt, no warning. It is how VPN leak checkers work. The one thing it cannot do alone is learn your public address: that answer comes from a STUN server, so this page asks Google’s and Cloudflare’s the moment it loads, and they see your IP in the asking.

Which of these actually matter

Most single values here are harmless. Your screen size tells nobody anything. The problem is that they combine: screen, time zone, language, GPU, core count and font rendering together are usually enough to pick one browser out of millions, and none of it is stored in a cookie you can clear.

The canvas fingerprint is the sharpest one. The same drawing instructions produce microscopically different pixels depending on your GPU, driver and font stack. Hash those pixels and you get a number that stays the same across visits, survives private browsing in many cases, and has nothing to do with cookies at all.

The public IP address is the one people are usually surprised by, because they assume a VPN hides it. It often does. But WebRTC asks a STUN server directly, and if that request escapes the tunnel, the address you see above is your real one rather than the VPN’s. If those two differ, your VPN is leaking.

If your local address shows as a random name

Something like a3f9c1d2-….local instead of 192.168.1.14 means your browser is doing its job. Chrome and Firefox now hide the real local address behind a random mDNS name unless the site already has camera or microphone permission. The public address is not hidden that way, and cannot be — it is how packets find their way back.

What to do about it

  • Use a browser that resists fingerprinting if it genuinely matters to you — Tor Browser makes everyone look identical, which is the only real defence.
  • If you use a VPN, check the public address above against what the VPN claims. They should match.
  • Blocking WebRTC entirely stops the address leak, and also breaks every video call in the browser. That is the trade.
  • Clearing cookies does nothing to a canvas fingerprint. Be clear-eyed about what “clear browsing data” actually clears.

Everything on this page was computed in your tab and is thrown away when you close it. The one exception is the STUN lookup, which is a question asked of a public server — and that is precisely the thing being demonstrated.

Questions people ask

Does any of this reach your server?
No. Every value in the table is computed in your tab and thrown away when you close it, and this page stores nothing of its own. Its one request is the WebRTC probe, which asks the Google and Cloudflare STUN servers what address you appear to come from — there is no way to learn a public address without showing it to somebody. Analytics loads here as on every page of this site, so Google does receive the ordinary pageview signals: browser, screen size, language, and a location derived from your address. Several of those are rows in the table above, which on this page of all pages is worth saying plainly.
Why is my public address missing?
Gathering finished without one, usually because a firewall is dropping UDP; the five-second timer is only the outer bound. If WebRTC is switched off in your browser altogether, the row reads unavailable rather than going blank. Extensions that merely restrict which addresses WebRTC may use often still let the public one through, so a blank here is the stronger signal.
I use a VPN. Should the public address be the VPN one?
Yes. Compare the address above against whatever address your VPN provider says it is giving you. If they differ, the WebRTC request escaped the tunnel and every site you visit can read your real address the same way this page just did. That is what a VPN leak is.
Why is my local address a random name ending in .local?
Your browser replaced the real one. Chrome and Firefox hide the local address behind a random mDNS name unless the site already holds camera or microphone permission, so seeing that name means the defence is working. The public address gets no such protection, because it is how packets find their way back to you.
Does clearing cookies change the canvas fingerprint?
No. This page stores nothing of its own, so the number is recomputed from the same drawing on each visit, and it comes out identical because your GPU, driver and fonts have not changed. Private browsing often does not change it either. The value shown belongs to this page and its particular drawing; another site drawing something else gets a different number with the same staying power.
