Media forensics: signals, not verdicts

People ask me for a deepfake detector. Nobody has one — not this page, not a paid service, not any tool that runs in a browser. What a browser can honestly do is surface the signals a forensic examiner would look at, say what each one means and what innocently produces it, and leave the conclusion where it belongs. Drop a photo, or a short video, and see for yourself.

  • Provenance, C2PA and EXIF first
  • ELA, noise floor, FFT, copy-move
  • An artefact playground, not a face swapper
  • Video frames sampled in the tab
  • No score, no verdict, nothing uploaded

Before you start

This tool runs entirely inside your browser. Nothing you paste or drop here is uploaded, logged or stored — which for security work is the whole point, not a footnote.

What it does not do

  • No verdict, no score, no confidence percentage
  • No face detection, and no face swapping
  • No C2PA signature check — that needs a network fetch
  • No uploads — there is no server to upload to
  • No codec or container work on video

Why that matters here

  • Evidence files stay on your machine
  • A photograph of someone is never transmitted
  • Nothing you drop ever appears in your Network tab
  • Works with no internet once the page is cached
  • Free, permanently

This tool does not decide whether an image is real. It reports signals and explains them. Every one of them has innocent causes; the absence of a signal proves nothing, and the presence of one is not an accusation.

The design constraint

          
Why there is no verdict, and no face swapper

        

        

          

            
A browser cannot reliably tell a real photograph from a generated or manipulated one, and nor can anything else, so a page that printed GENUINE or DEEPFAKE would be lying in a confident font. Every technique here measures processing history, not truthfulness: cropping a holiday snap lights them up, and a competent fake saved once through a messaging app puts them all out. So this tool returns signals, explains what each does and does not imply, and says the two things people forget as often as it takes — the absence of a signal proves nothing, and the presence of one usually has a boring explanation. There is no score anywhere on this page, and that is a decision rather than an omission.

            
The other half of the request was a deepfake generator. I did not build a face swapper, because that tool's dominant real-world use is non-consensual imagery, with fraud a distant second, and putting one on the same page as the detector meant to fight it would be indefensible. What teaches the same lesson honestly is the opposite move: take a photograph whose history you already know — your own — add exactly one artefact with a slider you control, then run the detector and watch one signal move. Cause and effect on a known input is what makes a signal legible. A gallery of fakes somebody else made only teaches you that a tool agrees with its own captions.

          

        

      


      

        

          
What each signal actually is

          
And, more usefully, what innocently produces it.

        

        

          

            
Provenance — the only real evidence here

            
A C2PA manifest, an XMP block, an IPTC record, an EXIF Software field. This is the one thing on the page that is evidence rather than inference, which is why it is reported first. It is also almost always missing: every large platform strips metadata on upload and so does every screenshot, so absence is the expected state and means nothing. Presence of a signed manifest is not validity either — checking a signature needs a certificate chain and a network request, and this page makes none.

          

          

            
Error level analysis, read correctly

            
Re-encode at a known quality, subtract, amplify. Bright regions compress differently from their neighbours. That is a map of compression history, not of manipulation, and the popular reading — bright means pasted — is wrong often enough to be dangerous. Sharp edges, saturated colour and fine texture are bright in every untouched photograph. It is useless on a screenshot, on a PNG, and on anything re-saved once since the edit you are hunting. The amplification factor is printed with the map, because turning the gain up until something looks suspicious is how ELA is usually used to mislead.

          

          

            
Quantisation tables and double compression

            
A JPEG carries the tables it was quantised with. Inverting the standard scaling recovers the quality setting exactly, and tells you whether a general-purpose encoder wrote the file or something shipping its own tables — a camera, Adobe, Apple. Then the ghost sweep re-encodes across a range of qualities: a second dip in the curve is consistent with two compression generations. Consistent with, not evidence of. Cropping, rotating, a chat app and a CMS thumbnailer all produce exactly the same double history.

          

          

            
The noise floor

            
Sensors write a faint, roughly uniform grain across the whole frame. Real PRNU work correlates that against a fingerprint built from dozens of images off the same camera body, which cannot be done from one dropped file — so this measures internal consistency instead: how much fine detail each region carries for how much structure it contains, and which regions disagree with the rest. Normalising by local structure is what stops the map being a picture of foliage and sky. A smoother-than-expected patch is what a denoised or generated region looks like. It is also what a defocused background looks like.

          

          

            
The frequency plot

            
A 2D FFT of a windowed crop. Upsampling layers in older GAN-era generators left a periodic checkerboard, which shows here as bright spots away from the centre and off the axes. Two honest caveats, both large. Current diffusion models have largely engineered that artefact away, so a clean plot says nothing about origin. And a resize, a photograph of a screen, a halftone print, a fabric weave and the JPEG block grid itself all produce periodic peaks in exactly the same place.

          

          

            
Copy-move

            
Hash small blocks, group the identical ones, and keep only groups whose members share the same displacement — forty blocks all shifted by the same vector is a region that was cloned. Flat blocks are skipped, because a clear sky matches itself everywhere, and enormous hash buckets are dropped, because bricks and tiles are repeated texture rather than a paste. It finds duplicates at the same scale and rotation; anything rotated, resized or blurred after cloning slips straight past it.

          

          

            
Faces: look here yourself

            
There is deliberately no face detection. A green box with a number beside it implies the number came from analysing a face when it came from analysing whatever was inside a rectangle, and it quietly moves the judgement from you to the tool — on this question your eyes are still better. So you get a checklist and a 6× magnifier instead: catchlights that should agree between the eyes, ear and jaw discontinuities, teeth as individual objects, the hairline boundary, and whether a straight line in the background survives passing behind the head.

          

          

            
Video, kept deliberately shallow

            
Nine frames sampled through a video element and a canvas, compared for fine-detail energy, with any one of them promotable to the working image so the still analyses can run on it. A composited face is blended frame by frame, so its noise floor can breathe in a way the rest of the picture does not, and at a blend boundary that reads as flicker. There is no codec work, no container parsing and no motion estimation, so nothing here sees the encoder, the GOP structure or the container metadata — a hard cut, a flash and autofocus hunting move the same number.

          

        

      


      

        

          
How to use the playground

          
The point is cause and effect on a file whose history you already know.

        

        
    
          
  1. Drop one of your own photographs and press Run the signal set. Read that output as the baseline — it is what an ordinary file looks like, including whatever ordinary handling it has already had.
    2. 
          
  2. Click the part of the image you care about, so the analysis crop and the artefact land there rather than in the middle of the frame. Arrow keys do the same thing if you would rather not use a mouse.
    3. 
          
  3. Pick one artefact, set a strength, and press Apply. One at a time is the whole method: stack three and you learn nothing about which one moved which number.
    4. 
          
  4. Run the signal set again and compare against the baseline. Each artefact's description names the section to watch, and says which numbers will not move — a whole-frame average barely notices a change confined to one ellipse, so for local artefacts the maps react and the summary numbers do not.
    5. 
          
  5. Try the warp at full strength and notice that no section catches it. Geometry is verified by eye, against straight lines in the background — which is the lesson that artefact exists to teach.
    6. 
          
  6. Press Reset to original between experiments, and Export watermarked copy if you want the result as a file. Exports carry a burned-in watermark and EXIF marking them synthetic.

Questions people ask

        

          

            Can this tell me whether a photo is a deepfake?
            
No, and neither can anything else that runs in a browser. It surfaces the signals a forensic examiner would look at and explains what each one does and does not imply. Every signal here has innocent causes, and a competent fake — or one extra re-save — erases all of them. If a tool hands you a percentage, that number was invented.

          

          

            Why is there no score or confidence percentage?
            
Because there is nothing honest to compute one from. A score implies the signals combine into a measurement of authenticity, and they do not: they measure processing history. Putting a number on the page would make it look more reliable than it is, which is the exact failure this page exists to avoid.

          

          

            Why does my untouched photo show signals?
            
Because ordinary handling produces them. Cropping, resizing, a messaging app, a CMS thumbnailer, a phone camera's own processing, and simply opening and re-saving a file all leave recompression and noise differences behind. That is why nothing in the output is phrased as a finding.

          

          

            Why is there no face swapper in the playground?
            
Because that tool's dominant real-world use is non-consensual imagery, with fraud second, and building it on the same page as a detector meant to fight it would be indefensible. The playground applies individual artefacts to your own image instead, so you can watch one signal move at a time. That is what actually teaches someone to read these.

          

          

            Does it verify Content Credentials or C2PA?
            
It reports whether a manifest is present, and it does not check the signature. Verifying one needs a certificate chain and a trust list, which means a network request, and this page makes none. Presence is not validity — take the file to a C2PA verifier if the claim matters.

          

          

            What does it do with video?
            
It samples nine frames through a video element and a canvas, compares their fine-detail energy, and lets you run every still analysis on any one of them. It does no codec or container work at all, so it sees nothing of the encoder, the GOP structure or the container metadata.

          

          

            Why does the ELA map look bright all over my image?
            
Sharp edges, saturated colour and fine texture are bright in the ELA of every untouched photograph ever taken, and a screenshot or a re-saved file carries one uniform compression history that makes the whole map meaningless. ELA shows recompression, not manipulation.

          

          

            Is my file uploaded?
            
No. Every pixel is read in this tab, and the only file that leaves is one you ask to download. A tool that made you upload a photograph in order to check whether it could be trusted would be a strange bargain.
