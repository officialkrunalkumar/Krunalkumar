What a stranger can learn about you

Not from anything hidden — from the things you already published. A bio, a handle, an out-of-office reply, a CV, a photograph of your desk. Paste your own and see each detail mapped to the security question or password reset it helps somebody answer.

This only reads what you give it about yourself. It never looks anyone up and it makes no network request of any kind — there is no lookup to run, on you or on anybody else. If the text or the photo belongs to another person, this is the wrong page: that is not a self-check, it is surveillance, and I would rather say so at the top than in a footnote.

  • Your own bio, handle, CV, reply or photo
  • Each finding tied to the reset it opens
  • No lookups — on you or on anyone
  • No network request of any kind
  • A hardening checklist you can actually work through

Before you start

This tool runs entirely inside your browser. Nothing you paste or drop here is uploaded, logged or stored — which for security work is the whole point, not a footnote.

What it does not do

  • No uploads — there is no server to upload to
  • No lookup of a name, a handle or an address, ever
  • No account is checked on any site
  • No verdict, no score, no percentage
  • No account, no history, nothing saved

Why that matters here

  • The subject of this tool is a person — you
  • A tool that found the accounts would be a stalking tool
  • Nothing you paste ever appears in your Network tab
  • Works with no internet once the page is cached
  • Free, permanently

What it discloses


          

        

      


      

        

          
How this reads what you paste

        

        

          

            
Every finding names the door it opens

            
A list of things you mentioned is not useful. What is useful is the mapping: your dog is called Coco is not interesting until you notice it is the literal answer to a security question you set in 2014. So each finding prints the text that triggered it, what it hands a stranger, and the recovery question, reset flow or pretext it feeds.

          

          

            
The danger is arithmetic, not any one fact

            
A city is harmless. A school is harmless. A birth year is harmless. Together they are an identity, and with a pet name on top they are most of a password reset. The summary at the end of each report collects the doors rather than the details, because that is the part worth acting on.

          

          

            
There is no score, deliberately

            
Nothing here is calibrated against a measured population of real profiles, so a percentage would be invented. It would also be the part you remembered instead of the reasons. Findings are grouped high, medium and low, and low genuinely means low — a bare year on its own is weak evidence until it sits next to a school name.

          

          

            
It only knows the patterns I wrote down

            
A clean result is not a clean bill of health, and the output says so rather than congratulating you. The most revealing things people post have no pattern in them at all: the cafe you are always in, the ceiling of a hospital ward, the fact that you replied at three in the morning. Read your own text again afterwards with the question “what could somebody do with this”.

          

        

      


      

        

          
A photo gives away more than its metadata

          
Stripping EXIF is the easy half, and it is where nearly everybody stops. The rest is in the pixels, survives every stripper, and is what actually locates people.

        

        

          

            
The metadata half, done properly elsewhere

            
This tool walks the file’s segments and tells you whether an EXIF, XMP, IPTC or PNG text block is present, and how large. It does not decode the tags, because the EXIF viewer already does that properly and hands you a stripped copy of the file. A second half-built parser here would be worse than a link.

          

          

            
Reflections

            
Glasses, a window, a kettle, a car door, a phone screen, a spoon. Reflections have identified rooms, streets and the person holding the camera, and nobody ever checks them because nobody is looking at the background of their own photograph.

          

          

            
Paper, screens and barcodes

            
An envelope with an address window, a prescription, a delivery label, an inbox open on the monitor behind you. A boarding pass barcode carries the booking reference, which is often enough to open the booking and read the rest of the itinerary — you can see what one contains in the QR and barcode lab, using a code you generate yourself.

          

          

            
Numbers, plates and nameplates

            
A number on a gate, a society board, a letterbox, a vehicle registration, the sticker on a router with the network name and default password on it. One number plus a neighbourhood is an address, and a network name is searchable in public wardriving databases.

          

          

            
Uniforms, badges and lanyards

            
A school crest on a child’s uniform names the school and the neighbourhood, twice a day, five days a week. A work badge names the employer and often prints the employee number, which is the detail that makes a helpdesk caller sound like they are already inside.

          

          

            
The background you always have

            
The same tiles, the same curtain, the same corner of the same balcony. Across enough posts a repeated background becomes a location even when no single photograph shows one, which is why removing the coordinates from one file does much less than people think.

          

        

      


      

        

          
Why the username panel will not find your accounts

          
The technique is worth understanding. Performing it for you is not something this site is going to do.

        

        

          

            
How enumeration works

            
Profile URLs are predictable. A tool holds a list of templates, asks for each one and reads the status code: 200 means the name is taken, 404 means it is free. Sites that answer 200 for everything are matched on the words in the body instead. Sign-up forms leak the same fact from the other direction — “that username is already taken” is a yes — and so does a reset form that says “no account with that address” instead of the careful “if an account exists, we have sent an email”.

          

          

            
Why this page does none of it

            
A tool that takes a handle and returns a list of that person’s accounts is a stalking tool, whatever its author intended. So this one prints the variants an enumerator would derive, explains the mechanism, and gives you the checklist of account types to work through in your own browser. The searching stays a deliberate act, done by you, on you.

          

          

            
A match is a hypothesis, not proof

            
Common handles collide, people get impersonated, and old accounts get resold. A confident list of “your accounts” is somebody’s guess that they are about to act on. That is worth remembering both when you audit yourself and when you see one of these lists produced about someone else.

          

          

            
The one lookup worth doing on purpose

            
Check your address against a breach index. The breach check does that with k-anonymity — and unlike this page it genuinely does make a network request, which it says on the card and on the page, because the difference matters. While you are there, what your browser leaks shows what a site learns about you before you have typed anything at all.

          

        

      


      

        

          
The hardening checklist

          
In the order I would actually do them. Most of this is twenty minutes of settings and one uncomfortable half hour with your own old posts.

        

        
    
          
  1. Turn location off in the camera app, not just in the app you post from. The coordinates are written when the photo is taken, and they travel with the file to everyone you email it to.
    2. 
          
  2. Change the security questions your feed already answers. Put a long random string in the answer field and keep it in your password manager. A question whose answer is a fact about you is not a secret; it is a quiz your posts have already sat.
    3. 
          
  3. Move to an authenticator app or a passkey wherever it is offered. A generated code beats a question your bio answers — there is a working TOTP explorer here, and passwords, passkeys and what actually protects you covers the choice.
    4. 
          
  4. Take your recovery email and recovery phone number off every public profile. They are the two fields a takeover needs, and they are usually published for convenience.
    5. 
          
  5. Search your own handle in quotation marks, then work down the account-type checklist the tool prints. Empty the accounts you no longer use before you close them — closing one often leaves the content up.
    6. 
          
  6. Use a boring, unshared handle for anything sensitive: banking, health, anything legal. Reuse is convenient precisely because it is linkable.
    7. 
          
  7. Strip the out-of-office reply to two lines, or switch off the external one entirely. No date, no reason, no named colleague, no mobile number. Most mail systems can send a fuller message inside the company only.
    8. 
          
  8. Take the date of birth, home address, parent’s name, photograph, signature and referees off any CV you have made public. A city and a country is all an employer needs before a real conversation.
    9. 
          
  9. Set the privacy zone in your fitness app so it covers home, work and the school run, and check what the default was before you changed it.
    10. 
          
  10. Go back through old photo albums with the checklist in front of you. Documents, keys, uniforms, house numbers and screens are what you are looking for, and they will mostly be in pictures you posted years ago.
    11. 
          
  11. Check your email addresses against a breach index and change anything reused. The breach check and the password strength lab are both here.
    12. 
          
  12. Ask the people close to you to do the same. A good deal of what identifies you was posted by somebody else, tagged, and left public — a marriage biodata is the sharpest example of that, and what a marriage biodata leaks goes through one line by line.
    13. 
        

      


      

        

          
What this deliberately will not do

        

        

          

            
It will not check anybody but you

            
The tool cannot tell whose text you pasted, so nothing stops you technically. That is precisely why the rule is written down rather than enforced by a check that would not work. Point it at yourself. If you want to point it at somebody else, close the tab.

          

          

            
It will not make a request

            
No fetch, no XHR, no remote image, no font from a third party, no analytics event carrying anything you typed. Open your Network tab and use the whole page — nothing appears. That is the only form of this promise that can be verified rather than believed.

          

          

            
It will not keep anything

            
Nothing you paste or drop is stored, in this tab or anywhere else. Reload the page and it is gone. The one thing that persists across the Labs section is the flag saying you have read the consent card, which is a single value in local storage.

          

          

            
It will not tell you that you are safe

            
No rule matching is not the same as nothing disclosed, and the output refuses to pretend otherwise. Treat a clean result as “none of my patterns fired”, then read your own words again as a stranger would. For the wider picture of how this material gets used, types of cyberattacks is the longer piece.

          

        

      


      

        

          
Questions people ask

        

          

            Does this look anyone up?
            
No. There is no fetch, no XHR, no remote image and no username probed against a site. Everything on this page is string matching and arithmetic in your own tab, over text and files you supplied yourself. Open your browser's Network tab and use the tool: nothing appears.

          

          

            Can I check somebody else with this?
            
Please do not. The tool cannot tell whose text you pasted, so nothing stops you technically, and that is exactly why the rule is written down rather than enforced. It exists so you can look at your own exposure. Pointing it at another person turns a self-check into surveillance, and if that is what you want, this is the wrong page.

          

          

            Why does it not tell me which accounts my username has?
            
Because a tool that takes a handle and returns a list of that person's accounts is a stalking tool, whatever the intention of whoever wrote it. So this shows you how enumeration works, prints the variants a tool would derive, and hands you the checklist of account types to work through in your own browser. The searching stays a deliberate act by you, on you.

          

          

            Does a clean result mean I am safe?
            
No, and the output says so. This only knows the patterns in its own rule table. Plenty of real disclosure is an ordinary sentence with no pattern in it: the cafe you are always in, the ceiling of a hospital ward, the fact that you replied at three in the morning. A clean result means no rule matched, not that nothing was given away.

          

          

            Why does it not read the EXIF in my photo itself?
            
Because that tool already exists here and does it properly. This one walks the file's segments to tell you whether a metadata block is present, then sends you to the EXIF viewer at /labs/exif to decode the tags and download a stripped copy. What it adds instead is the half that stripping does not fix: reflections, documents, house numbers, uniforms and barcodes, which live in the pixels.

          

          

            Is anything I paste or drop uploaded?
            
No. There is no server to upload to and no request is made. Your photo is decoded from a blob URL, which is a local address for bytes already in the tab, and your text never leaves the textarea. Nothing is stored either: reload the page and it is gone.

          

          

            Why is there no risk score?
            
Because nothing here is calibrated against a measured population, so a number would be invented. It would also be the part people remembered instead of the reasons, and the reasons are the useful bit. Findings are grouped as high, medium and low, and each one names the door it opens.
