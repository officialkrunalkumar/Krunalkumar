The periodic table, drawn as the elements actually look

Every periodic table colours the tiles by category and stops there, which leaves you knowing sulfur is a “reactive nonmetal” and no idea that it is a startling lemon yellow. This one draws the substance as well as the data: silvery metal that reads as metal, bromine’s dark red-brown liquid fuming orange, chlorine’s pale green, caesium’s gold, graphite’s black lustre, a noble gas glowing in a discharge tube. Click any element for a shell diagram built from its electron configuration and the numbers behind it.

What this one does differently

It shows you the substance

Each element carries an explicit appearance descriptor — a base colour, a phase, and a surface kind such as lustrous metal, dull metal, brittle crystal, black lustre or glowing gas — and the renderer builds the highlight, the shadow and the grain from that. Copper is copper-coloured, gold is gold, iodine is a blue-black crystal that fumes violet, mercury beads up.

Drawn, not photographed

There is not a single licensed photograph in this repository and nothing on this site fetches an image from anywhere else, so the swatches are procedural: CSS gradients for the small tile, inline SVG with real gradients and an feTurbulence grain for the large one. It will never be a photograph. It is meant to be recognisable, and honest about being a drawing.

The shell diagram is derived

The electron configuration is stored once, in a machine-readable form. The noble-gas shorthand is computed by matching the previous noble gas as a prefix, and the shell occupancies are computed by summing each subshell into its principal quantum number. Three views of one fact, so they cannot drift apart — and the real anomalies (chromium, copper, palladium, platinum, the lanthanide irregularities) are in there rather than rounded off to what aufbau predicts.

Watch the table melt

Set the colouring to state at temperature and drag the slider. At 298 K exactly two elements are liquid. At 400 K every alkali metal but lithium has melted; by 3700 K carbon alone is still solid, tungsten having gone five kelvin earlier. It is the melting and boiling data doing something you can actually see instead of sitting in a column.

It says unknown when it does not know

Above fermium the elements have never existed in weighable amounts, so their melting points, densities and radii are predictions from relativistic quantum chemistry rather than measurements — and several have no accepted prediction at all. Those cells read unknown and the element is flagged as predicted. Inventing a plausible number for rutherfordium’s density would make the page look more complete and be worse than useless.

Keyboard first

The arrow keys move between elements by their real position in the table, skipping the gaps, and moving the selection updates the panel — freezing it would make the table unreadable with a keyboard. Every tile carries a spoken label, the panel is a live region, and the 57–71 and 89–103 markers are real buttons that jump down into the pulled-out rows.

Where the numbers come from

Worth knowing before you quote anything off this page.

The units, stated plainly

Atomic weights are the IUPAC abridged values; a number in square brackets is a mass number rather than a weight, because that element has no stable isotope and there is nothing to average. Melting and boiling points are in kelvin at one atmosphere, with the Celsius value alongside. Densities are quoted with their units in the value, because gases are measured in grams per litre and solids in grams per cubic centimetre and silently mixing the two would be a lie in the fourth decimal place.

Radii, and the gaps in them

The radii are the empirical values, which is why the noble gases have none: those figures are derived from bond lengths, and the noble gases do not form the bonds. Rather than swap in a value from a different scale and quietly mix two definitions in one column, the page leaves it blank and says so.

The f-block has no group numbers

IUPAC assigns no group numbers across the lanthanides and actinides, and which two elements belong in group 3 — lanthanum and actinium, or lutetium and lawrencium — is still argued over in the literature. This table shows no group number for those thirty elements rather than picking a side and presenting it as settled.

Allotropes complicate everything

Carbon is graphite and diamond; phosphorus is white, red and black; tin has two forms that look nothing alike. One swatch cannot show all of that, so the swatch draws the common form and the description names the others. Where a density depends on the allotrope, the value says which one it is.

Questions people ask

Are the swatches photographs of the real elements?
No, and they are not trying to be. This site ships no licensed photographs and fetches nothing from anywhere else, so every swatch is drawn in your browser from an explicit descriptor - one base colour, a phase, and a surface kind such as lustrous metal, brittle crystal, glowing gas or liquid metal - using CSS gradients and inline SVG. The aim is that a metal reads as metal and a gas reads as a gas, not that it passes for a photograph.
How accurate are the numbers?
The atomic weights are the IUPAC abridged values; melting and boiling points are in kelvin at one atmosphere; radii are the empirical (Slater) values, which is why the noble gases have none - those values come from bond lengths and the noble gases do not form the bonds. Everything is stated to the precision the source gives it. Where a value is genuinely disputed or has never been measured, the page says unknown rather than filling the gap with something plausible.
Why do so many of the heavy elements say unknown?
Because nobody has measured them. Above fermium the elements have never existed in weighable amounts - most have been made a few atoms at a time and lasted milliseconds - so their melting points, densities and radii are relativistic-chemistry predictions rather than measurements, and several have no accepted prediction at all. Those elements carry a warning in the panel and their unmeasured values read unknown. A number invented to fill the cell would look more complete and be worse than useless.
Why are the lanthanides and actinides in separate rows at the bottom?
Purely to make the table fit. Elements 57 to 71 and 89 to 103 belong between groups 2 and 3 of periods 6 and 7; leaving them there makes the table 32 columns wide. Pulling them out is the conventional compromise, and the 57-71 and 89-103 markers stand where the block really belongs - pressing Enter on one jumps down to the row. IUPAC assigns no group numbers across the f-block, and which two of these elements belong in group 3 is still argued over, so this table shows no group number for them rather than picking a side.
What does the temperature slider do?
Set the colouring to state at temperature and the slider recolours every element solid, liquid or gas for that temperature at one atmosphere, worked out from its melting and boiling points. At 298 K only mercury and bromine are liquid; wind it up and you can watch the table melt in order. Three elements are handled as special cases: carbon and arsenic sublime rather than melt at atmospheric pressure, and helium does not freeze at atmospheric pressure at any temperature.
Is the shell diagram how electrons really behave?
No. Electrons do not orbit the nucleus in rings; they occupy probability clouds with shapes the diagram cannot show. A Bohr picture is wrong about where the electrons are and right about how many are in each shell, which is the fact it is here to make visible. The ring counts are summed straight out of the electron configuration printed beside them, so the picture and the text cannot drift apart.
Can I use it with a keyboard?
Yes. Tab into the table and the arrow keys move between elements by their real position, skipping the gaps; Home and End jump to the ends of a row, Ctrl+Home and Ctrl+End to hydrogen and oganesson. Moving the selection updates the detail panel, which is announced as a live region, and every tile carries a spoken label with the name, symbol, number, category, group and period.
Does it run on my machine?
Entirely. The element data is embedded in the page's own JavaScript, the swatches and diagrams are drawn in your browser, and there is no network call of any kind. Once the page is cached it works offline.
