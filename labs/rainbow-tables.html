Salt and rainbow tables, built live in your browser

Most explanations of salting assert the lesson and get it slightly wrong. This one shows the mechanics instead: a lookup table hashing a wordlist, the same table cracking unsalted hashes, a salt reducing it to zero, a real rainbow table with chains and false alarms, and a fast-versus-slow hashing test timed on your machine. Every number is computed here, not narrated.

  • A lookup table you watch fill
  • Cracks unsalted MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256
  • A salt, and what it truly stops
  • A genuine rainbow table, chains and all
  • Fast vs slow hashing, timed here

Before you start

This tool runs entirely inside your browser. Nothing you paste or drop here is uploaded, logged or stored — which for security work is the whole point, not a footnote.

What it does not do

  • No uploads — the hashing happens on your own processor
  • The wordlist is a few thousand common passwords, not a full dictionary
  • It only cracks unsalted hashes, and reads at most 500 pasted digests
  • The rainbow table is deliberately toy-sized, over a 4-character keyspace
  • The speed test is one machine on one day, not a benchmark of record

Why that matters here

  • Any hash you paste stays in this tab
  • Nothing you enter ever appears in your Network tab
  • SHA uses the browser’s crypto.subtle; MD5 is implemented in the page
  • Works with no internet once the page is cached
  • Free, permanently

What this is really teaching

        

        

          

            
A lookup table is not a rainbow table

            
People use the terms interchangeably, and they are not the same thing. A lookup table stores every digest next to its plaintext — fast to search, but the space cost is brutal. A rainbow table stores only the two ends of long chains of alternating hash and reduction steps, trading time for space. Steps 1 and 4 build one of each so the difference is concrete.

          

          

            
A salt is not a secret

            
A salt is random bytes stored in the clear, right beside the hash. It does nothing to stop someone cracking one chosen user — they just hash the wordlist again with that user’s salt. What it stops is amortisation: one precomputed table can no longer crack a million accounts at once, because every account needs the work redone. Step 3 shows exactly that.

          

          

            
Slow hashes buy time, not secrecy

            
PBKDF2, bcrypt, scrypt and Argon2 are deliberately slow, so each guess in an offline attack costs more. Step 5 times SHA-256 against PBKDF2 and reports the ratio. bcrypt, scrypt and Argon2 go further with memory-hardness, which is what actually blunts a GPU — and which this page cannot measure fairly, so it does not pretend to.

          

          

            
Why rainbow tables faded

            
Rainbow tables were only ever good against fast, unsalted hashes. Once salting became standard, each user needed their own table and the whole approach collapsed. Modern GPUs now brute-force small keyspaces outright, faster than looking up a chain. They still matter for one thing: old, unsalted MD5 or SHA-1 dumps, which is why the topic will not quite die.

Questions people ask

        

          

            Is a rainbow table just a big lookup table?
            
No, and conflating the two is the most common mistake here. A lookup table maps every digest straight to its plaintext. A rainbow table stores only the start and end of long chains built by alternating a hash and a reduction function, then walks a target hash to find a matching endpoint. It trades computation at crack time for a much smaller stored table. Step 4 builds a real one so you can watch the walk.

          

          

            Does salting make a password uncrackable?
            
No. The salt is stored in the clear next to the hash, so an attacker targeting one account simply re-hashes the wordlist with that account’s salt, and a weak password still falls. What salting removes is the ability to attack everyone at once with a single precomputed table. Combine a salt with a deliberately slow hash and each of those per-user attacks also becomes expensive. Step 3 demonstrates both halves.

          

          

            Why does the rainbow demo use MD5 and not SHA-256?
            
The chain walk needs tens of thousands of fast, sequential hashes, and crypto.subtle is asynchronous, which makes that slow and awkward. MD5 is implemented directly in the page, runs synchronously, and is exactly the fast, unsalted, legacy hash that rainbow tables were ever effective against. The lookup-table steps still let you pick SHA-1 and SHA-256.

          

          

            Are the speed numbers a real benchmark?
            
No. They are one browser, on one machine, on one day, and they move with JIT warm-up and thermal state. The SHA-256 rate is also throttled by per-call overhead in JavaScript, so it sits far below what native code or a GPU manages. Use the ratio between the fast and slow hash, which is the honest signal; do not read either absolute rate as a hardware figure.

          

          

            Is my pasted hash uploaded anywhere?
            
No. Everything runs in this browser tab. SHA-1 and SHA-256 go through the browser’s built-in crypto.subtle, which is local, and MD5 is implemented in the page itself. There is no fetch, no upload and no server. A hash you paste never appears in your Network tab.

          

          

            Can this crack my real password hash?
            
Only if the plaintext happens to be one of the few thousand common passwords in the built-in wordlist and the hash is unsalted. That is the point: weak, unsalted hashes fall instantly, and anything else does not. This is a teaching demonstration, not a cracking rig, and it says its limits out loud in the output.
