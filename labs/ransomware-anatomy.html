How a ransomware incident actually unfolds

The encryption is the last twenty minutes of something that took three weeks. Everything expensive happens before it — the door, the foothold, the mapping, the deleted backups, the copy of your data taken quietly on a Tuesday. Step the whole chain here, switch the controls you already pay for on one at a time, and watch which stage each of them genuinely stops. Some of them stop nothing, and that is the most useful thing on the page.

The part people get wrong about the timeline

Almost everyone pictures ransomware as a moment: a file is opened and the screen goes red. The moment is real and it is the last twenty minutes of something that has usually been running for weeks. Nearly every chance you had to stop it was in the part nobody pictures.

The encryption is the end, not the attack

By the time anything is unreadable, somebody has already been inside long enough to learn your network, take your administrator credentials, work out what you can afford, copy the files worth copying and delete the recovery you were counting on. Encryption is deployed last because it is the only step that tells you they are there. Everything you would have wanted to catch happened while you had no idea.

Deleting your backups is a targeted step

It is not collateral damage from a tool that encrypts everything it can see. Locating the backup system, finding the account that controls it and clearing the repository is done deliberately and done early, because a working restore is the single thing that makes the ransom optional. If your backups are reachable from a domain administrator account, they are part of the estate being attacked rather than the thing that recovers it.

The data left before the files locked

Double extortion is the norm now, and it changes what a backup buys you. The encryption stops you working and a restore fixes that. The copy they took keeps working afterwards: a leak site, a countdown, a regulator, customers and a contract full of confidentiality clauses. “We have backups” is a complete answer to half of the incident and no answer at all to the other half.

Nobody is targeting you personally

Most of this is industrialised. Access is found by scanning the whole internet for a vulnerable build, or bought from somebody who specialises in getting in and selling on. The operator who arrives afterwards is running a playbook against a list, and you are a line on it. That is not reassuring, but it does mean the controls that work are ordinary and boring rather than bespoke.

Ransomware is one attack among many, and it borrows its first move from most of the others. The wider map — phishing, credential attacks, denial of service and the rest, in plain language — is the article.

How to read the three panels

The intrusion panel is the one that matters

Pick an entry route, switch controls on in the left-hand panel and step the chain. It halts at the stage the control you enabled genuinely bites, and the stages below grey out because they never happened. Watch what multi-factor authentication does to the exposed remote desktop route and then what it does to the phishing route, which is nothing at all. That difference is the whole argument for buying more than one thing.

Not every control halts anything

Each effect is labelled honestly as stopping the chain, blunting it, or only alerting. Segmentation halts nothing and caps the blast radius, which turns a company-ending event into a bad week in one business unit. Backups prevent none of the nine stages and change the ending completely. A page that drew every control as a wall would be easier to read and would be a lie.

The file panel draws the keys, not a cipher

A fabricated tree gets enumerated and then locked, and the key hierarchy is drawn beside it: a fresh symmetric key per file, wrapped with a public key that shipped inside the binary, with the private half on a machine you will never reach. Switch the immutable copy on and watch the delete get refused. Nothing on this page encrypts anything, and the key strings are labels from a counter.

The bill panel uses your numbers

Type in what a day down costs you, what you would be asked for, how many servers you have and how fast you can genuinely restore them, and it lays the paths side by side on the same day scale. The defaults are placeholders, not a benchmark — published figures for every one of those inputs disagree by an order of magnitude. The arithmetic is the point, and so is the fact that the first two steps are identical whichever way you go.

What this page will not do

Worth stating plainly rather than leaving anyone to wonder.

There is no encryption anywhere in it

No cipher, no cryptography API call, no key material and no file of yours read at any point. The tree is invented and lives in this tab. The hexadecimal strings shown as keys come from a counter, so scrubbing backwards shows the same value it showed going forwards — they are decoration over a story, and they are labelled as such in the panel.

There is no operational detail in it

No sample, no code, no tool named, no command shown, nothing about how deployment or evasion is actually done. Every stage is described at the altitude a board paper describes it, because helping somebody write that paper is the entire purpose. If you came here looking for how to do any of this, there is nothing on the page for you.

It does not prove your controls work

It shows where a control bites in a model I built. A real environment has misconfigurations, exceptions, an account somebody made in 2019 and a supplier with a permanent tunnel in. Treat the halt points as an argument for a control rather than as evidence about yours — the only thing that produces evidence about yours is testing it.

It does not tell you whether to pay

That decision has legal, insurance and regulatory dimensions that vary by country and by who is on the other end, and none of them belong on a web page. What the page can do honestly is show you what paying buys — a decryption tool of unknown quality, and no effect at all on the copy of your data they already took.

Questions people ask

Does this page encrypt anything on my machine?
No. There is no cipher anywhere in this page, no call to any cryptography API, and no file of yours is read at any point. The file tree is invented and lives in this tab's memory. The key strings on screen are labels produced by a counter so that stepping backwards shows the same value it showed going forwards. It is a diagram that moves, not a program that does anything.
Is this a guide to building ransomware?
No, and it is written to be useless as one. There is no sample, no code, no tool named, no command shown and no evasion technique described in a way anybody could act on. Every stage is named at the level a board paper names it, because helping somebody write that board paper is the entire purpose. The audience for this page is people who have to fund backups.
Why is paying the only way back without a backup?
Because of how the keys are arranged. Each file gets its own fresh symmetric key, and that key is immediately wrapped with a public key shipped inside the binary, then discarded from memory. The wrapped key sits on your own disk next to the file. Only the matching private key opens it, and that private key never came near your network. So the thing you need is right there in front of you and cannot be opened, and the only party who can open it is the one asking for money.
Does an offline backup really break the model?
It breaks the leverage, which is the part that matters. Finding and deleting your backups is a deliberate, targeted step taken before anything is encrypted, precisely because a restore is the only thing that makes the ransom optional. A copy that is genuinely offline, or immutable because the storage enforces retention rather than an account that can be taken over, survives that step. It does not stop the intrusion, it does not un-copy the data they stole, and it is worthless if nobody has ever tested a restore from it.
Does paying actually get the data back?
Paying buys a decryption tool written by the people who broke your estate, not a restore. Those tools are routinely slow, sometimes buggy, and partial or corrupted returns are normal rather than exceptional. You still have to rebuild the identity plane, because you are not handing an environment back to production while somebody else has held domain admin in it for three weeks. And the copy of your data they took before any encryption started is still theirs. Whether paying is even lawful depends on who is on the other end, and that is a question for a lawyer rather than for this page.
Which one control should we buy first?
The page deliberately refuses to answer that, because the answer changes with the door. Switch multi-factor authentication on and the exposed remote desktop route dies at stage one, while the phishing route is entirely unaffected and the unpatched appliance route does not even see a login prompt. That is the argument for depth: a single control halts one path, and the operator is running the same playbook against a list you are one line of. If you want a starting point rather than an answer, the two that change the ending rather than the story are backups you have restore-tested and somebody rostered to read the alerts you already generate.
Where do the numbers in the cost panel come from?
From you. The panel does arithmetic on figures you type, and the defaults are illustrative placeholders rather than a benchmark. Published figures for downtime cost, ransom size, restore rate and decryptor failure disagree with each other by an order of magnitude depending on who was surveyed and who paid for the survey, so quoting one would look authoritative and tell you nothing. The shape of the arithmetic is the finding: the first steps are identical whichever way you go, and the paths only separate later than people expect.
