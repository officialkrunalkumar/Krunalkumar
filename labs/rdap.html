Domain registration lookup (RDAP)

Classic WHOIS runs over raw TCP port 43, which a browser cannot open and the Fetch spec blocks outright — so every web WHOIS tool is a server querying on your behalf. RDAP is the ICANN-mandated successor, and it speaks HTTPS, so this one is not.

  • Registration, expiry and transfer dates
  • Transfer and delete locks
  • Nameservers and DNS provider
  • DNSSEC delegation
  • Domain age — the phishing signal that matters

This tool uses the network

Most of Labs runs with no server at all. This one is different, and the notice is different to match: using it sends a request to rdap.org and the authoritative registry for the TLD, and they will see it.

What leaves your browser

  • They see which domain you looked up, and your IP address.
  • Your IP address, as with any request you make
  • Nothing is sent until you press the button
  • One request, which redirects to the registry for that TLD
  • Many country-code TLDs have no RDAP service at all

What still holds

  • No proxy — the request is not relayed through me
  • I never see it, log it or store it
  • No account, no history, no cookies sent
  • Every request is echoed on screen as it goes out
  • Free, permanently

If you are investigating something sensitive, that matters: rdap.org and the authoritative registry for the TLD learns which domain you looked up and roughly when. Agreeing here does not affect the offline tools, and agreeing to those did not bring you through this notice.

Domain age is the strongest single signal

            
Newly registered domains are enormously over-represented in phishing and fraud, because the domain gets burned as soon as it is reported. A domain registered nine days ago that has just emailed you about an invoice is worth treating differently from one registered in 2009.

          

          

            
Transfer locks are the anti-hijack control

            
clientTransferProhibited stops a domain being moved without the registrar explicitly unlocking it. A registry-level lock is stronger still. High-profile domain hijacks are almost always domains that had neither.

          

          

            
GDPR removed most of what WHOIS was for

            
Registrant names, emails and addresses are redacted for anything in scope, so the registrar is often the only party named. That is a genuine privacy gain and a genuine loss for investigations; both are true.

          

          

            
Why coverage is patchy

            
RDAP is mandatory for gTLDs and optional for country-code TLDs. Many ccTLDs have not implemented it, and some that have omit CORS headers — in which case the lookup fails in a browser even though the data exists.

          

        

      


      

        

          
Questions people ask

        

          

            What is RDAP and why not just use WHOIS?
            
RDAP is the ICANN-mandated replacement: same registry data, structured JSON, over HTTPS, with proper internationalisation and access control. WHOIS is an unstructured text protocol from 1982 over raw TCP port 43, which no browser can open.

          

          

            Does this go through a proxy?
            
No. Your browser requests rdap.org, which redirects to the registry authoritative for that TLD, and your browser follows the redirect directly. I never see the query.

          

          

            Why does it fail for some domains?
            
Either the TLD has no RDAP service, or its registry omits the CORS headers a browser needs. Both are common with country-code TLDs, and the browser will not say which it was.

          

          

            Why is the registrant hidden?
            
GDPR. Registries redact personal contact data for registrants in scope, so you usually see the registrar and little else.

          

          

            Can I check whether a domain is available?
            
Indirectly — a 404 usually means unregistered. A registrar’s search is the reliable way, and note that some registrars have historically front-run availability searches.
