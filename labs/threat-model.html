STRIDE threat modelling tool

Most threat models die as a whiteboard photograph. This one asks you to describe the system in pieces, proposes the STRIDE threats that the classic mapping says apply to each piece, and makes you accept or reject every single one. What comes out is a page you can print and a file you can open again next quarter.

  • STRIDE per element, applied properly
  • Accept, reject or rewrite each proposal
  • Likelihood, impact and a mitigation library
  • Printable model and JSON you can re-import
  • Two worked examples, one of them mine

Before you start

This tool runs entirely inside your browser. Nothing you type here is uploaded or logged — which for security work is the whole point, not a footnote. The one thing it does keep is your draft, saved in this browser so a reload does not lose it. It goes nowhere else, and “Start empty” erases it.

What it does not do

  • No uploads — there is no server to upload to
  • It does not understand your architecture; it applies one published table
  • It produces no score of its own — the ratings are yours
  • A threat model is not a certification, an audit or a test

Why that matters here

  • A threat model names your weak points — it should not leave your machine
  • Your draft is kept in this browser only, under lab.threat-model
  • Nothing you type ever appears in your Network tab
  • Works with no internet once the page is cached
  • Free, permanently

Describe what exists before you think about what could go wrong. Four kinds of thing, and the lines between them — that is the whole vocabulary.

Trust boundaries

A trust boundary is a line where the level of trust changes: between the internet and your servers, between your account and a vendor’s, between one team’s service and another’s. Nearly every interesting threat lives on one of these lines.

    Elements

    An external entity is a person or system outside your control. A process is code you run. A data store is anywhere data rests. A data flow is data moving between two of them. Add the boxes first, then the arrows.

      Boundary map

      Drawn from what you entered, not positioned by hand. The question it answers is the one that matters: what sits where, and which flows cross a line.

      Every proposal below comes from one published table — the classic STRIDE-per-element mapping — applied to the type of thing each element is. The tool has not understood your system. These are questions for you to answer, and an answer of “no, not here, because…” is worth as much as a yes.

      Ctrl+P from any tab prints this and nothing else — the header, the tool and the rest of the page are dropped by the print stylesheet.

      Using STRIDE without kidding yourself

      The mapping is the only automatic part

      External entities can be spoofed and can repudiate. Processes are exposed to all six categories. Data stores can be tampered with, read, destroyed, and repudiated against when they hold a record of events. Data flows can be tampered with, read and blocked. That table is twenty years old and it is the whole of the automation here. Everything past it is your judgement.

      Rejections are the valuable half

      A model full of accepted threats tells you what worried somebody. A model that records why four of them do not apply tells you what was thought about and settled, and it is the half that survives contact with next year’s argument. The rejection reason box exists because “we considered that” is worthless without the sentence that follows it.

      Risk here is arithmetic, not insight

      Likelihood times impact, three levels each, six possible values. It sorts the list and nothing more. If you want a defensible severity number for one specific vulnerability, that is a different instrument — use the CVSS calculator, and remember that even a 9.8 is a comparison, not a priority.

      Model early, and model small

      A threat model is cheapest before the code exists and most expensive after the incident. A single feature, a single integration, one afternoon with the people who built it — that is the version that gets done. A model of the entire estate is the version that gets started, abandoned, and quoted for years as evidence that threat modelling does not work.

      What the two examples are for

      Both load with one click and come with some decisions already made, because a page of forty undecided proposals teaches nobody the shape of the work.

      A small SaaS with a payment provider

      The familiar shape: a web app, a billing worker, a customer database, and a vendor holding the card data so you do not have to. It is here because the interesting threats are not in the application — they are on the webhook you did not verify, the log that quietly recorded an email address, and the customer id in the URL that nobody tested swapping.

      An automation workflow touching a CRM

      This one is my own consulting work, modelled honestly rather than flatteringly. A self-hosted workflow engine reads a client’s shared mailbox, drafts a reply with a language model, and writes to their CRM. Almost every asset in it belongs to somebody else, I hold the keys to all of them, and the run history stores every message by default. That last one is the threat people miss.

      Where this sits next to compliance

      Auditors ask whether you do threat modelling. The honest answer is a model per significant change, kept in version control next to the thing it describes, with the rejections and the dates intact — which is what the JSON export here is for. What it is not is evidence that your system is secure, and a framework that treats it as such is measuring paperwork. I have written about that gap at length in security compliance, explained, and if you would rather someone sat in the room and ran the session with your engineers, that is part of what I do for a living.

      Questions people ask

      Does a threat model like this prove anything?
      No. It is a thinking tool and a record of a conversation, not a certification, an audit or a test. It says what a group of people thought could go wrong on a particular day. It says nothing about whether the mitigations were built, whether they work, or whether somebody has already got in. Anyone who hands you a threat model as evidence of security has misunderstood what it is for.
      Why STRIDE and not something else?
      Because it is the easiest framework to start with and the hardest to argue with. Six categories, a fixed mapping from element types to categories, and no scoring to disagree about. It is one framework among several: LINDDUN handles privacy properly, PASTA is risk and attacker centred, and an attack tree suits one specific question better than a whole system. Any of them would find things STRIDE misses.
      Where is my work saved?
      In this browser, on this device, under the localStorage key lab.threat-model. It is not sent anywhere and it is not readable by any other site. A private window, cleared site data or a different browser will not have it, which is exactly why the JSON export exists. Export the file if the model matters.
      How does the tool decide which threats apply?
      It applies the classic STRIDE-per-element table and nothing more. External entities can be spoofed and can repudiate. Processes are exposed to all six. Data stores can be tampered with, read, destroyed, and repudiated against when they are a record of events. Data flows can be tampered with, read and blocked. That table is the whole of the automation here. The tool has not understood your architecture, and the proposals are prompts for you to answer, not findings.
      Why are likelihood and impact only three levels?
      Because more levels invite a precision nobody has. Low, medium and high are judgements a room can actually agree on, and their product gives six distinct risk values, which is enough to sort the list. If you want a calibrated severity number for a specific vulnerability, that is what CVSS is for, and there is a calculator for it in these labs.
      Can I bring last quarter's model back?
      Yes. Export JSON writes a plain file, and Import JSON reads it back with every decision, rating, mitigation and rejection reason intact. The file is readable text, so it belongs in version control next to the system it describes rather than in a folder somebody has to remember.
      Is anything I type uploaded?
      No. There is no server involved. A threat model is a list of a system's weak points and their owner's names, which is one of the least sensible things anyone could paste into a website, so this one makes no network request at all and works with the network switched off.
