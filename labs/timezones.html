Timezone meeting planner

Pick a date, add the zones, and drag a slot across the day. Every offset is drawn to scale, the shared working window is computed rather than guessed, and a slot that lands on a different calendar day says so — because “3pm Tuesday for you is 5:30am Wednesday for them” is the mistake that actually costs meetings.

  • 23 and 25-hour days, drawn to their real length
  • India +05:30, Nepal +05:45, Chatham +12:45, to scale
  • The shared working window, computed not guessed
  • Skipped and repeated hours named out loud
  • Copy line and .ics — nothing is sent anywhere

Before you start

This tool runs entirely inside your browser. Nothing you paste or drop here is uploaded, logged or stored — which for security work is the whole point, not a footnote.

What it does not do

  • No calendar integration — it reads and writes nothing
  • No free/busy, no attendee lookup, no invitations sent
  • No public holidays, no half-days, no lunch breaks
  • Working hours are what you type, which is a guess about people
  • Zone rules come from your own browser’s IANA database

Why that matters here

  • Nothing you type is uploaded, logged or stored
  • No account, no history, nothing saved between visits
  • The .ics file is built in this tab, not on a server
  • Works with no internet once the page is cached
  • Free, permanently

    Notes

    
          
    
        
    

        
    
          
          
          
          
          
    It reads and writes no calendar, checks nobody’s availability, and trusts the IANA database inside your browser for every rule it draws. Nothing here is advice.
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    
          
    How the day is drawn
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            
    Where the offsets come from
    
            
    There is no timezone database in this page. It asks Intl.DateTimeFormat to render a known instant in a named zone, reads the wall clock back with formatToParts, and takes the difference — that difference is the offset. Your browser already ships the IANA rules to drive Intl, so this borrows them rather than carrying a copy that would go stale within a year. It also means a device with out-of-date system data will draw out-of-date transitions, and there is nothing this page can do about that.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    Nothing is a grid of hour cells
    
            
    Every band, tick and bar is placed as a percentage of the window rather than snapped to an hour column. India is +05:30, Nepal is +05:45 and the Chatham Islands are +12:45; a grid of equal cells would have to round them to the nearest hour, and that rounding is the exact mistake this page exists to stop. A half-hour zone’s ticks land on the half, visibly out of step with its neighbours. That misalignment is the picture, not a rendering fault.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    The day that is 23 hours long
    
            
    On a clock-change date a day is not 24 hours. Ask for the offset at 09:00 and again at 10:00 and the two answers differ; a search between them finds the second the clocks moved. Reading that one instant with both offsets then gives the skipped hour going forward and the repeated hour coming back, directly. Pick 29 March or 25 October 2026 with a European zone on the grid and the notes will name the hour that does not exist, or the one that happens twice.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    The mistake this exists to stop
    
            
    Most scheduling errors are not about the hour. They are about the date: 20:00 in Mumbai is 02:30 the next morning in Auckland while New Zealand is on standard time, and 03:30 once it puts its clocks forward — the hour moves, the date does not, and an invitation that says only “Wednesday” is wrong for one of you either way. Any zone whose local date differs from the anchor zone’s gets a labelled chip beside the time, the sentence underneath spells out how many calendar days apart they are, and the slot’s own screen-reader text speaks the date in every zone on the grid.
    
          
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    
          
    Questions people ask
    
        
    
          
    
            Is anything uploaded?
            
    No. There is no server to upload to. The zones, the date and the working hours you set never leave the tab, and the .ics file is built here with a blob URL rather than fetched from anywhere. You can watch your Network tab stay empty while you drag the slot.
    
          
    
          
    
            Where do the time zone rules come from?
            
    From the IANA database inside your own browser, read through Intl.DateTimeFormat. That is deliberate: a copy baked into this page would be stale the moment a government moved a transition, and several do every year. The trade is that an old phone, or a machine with out-of-date system data, will draw out-of-date rules. If a date matters, confirm it with the other person rather than with this page.
    
          
    
          
    
            Why does it say there is no shared window?
            
    Because on the hours you gave it, there is not one. India and the US east coast have no overlap at all on a nine-to-five, which is a real fact about the planet rather than a fault in the drawing. When that happens the notes name the two zones that cannot meet, and show the closest the day gets: the stretch where the most people are working, and who would be outside their own hours for it.
    
          
    
          
    
            Does it know about holidays or my colleague’s calendar?
            
    No, to both, and it never will. It cannot see any calendar, it does not check free/busy, it does not know who works where, and it has no idea about public holidays, Friday half-days, Ramadan hours or the fact that nobody in Madrid answers anything at 14:00. Working hours are whatever you type into the two pickers on each row. That is a guess about people, not a fact about them.
    
          
    
          
    
            Why is India half an hour out of step with everything?
            
    India Standard Time is UTC+05:30, one offset for the whole country, and Nepal went further at +05:45. The Chatham Islands run +12:45. None of that is unusual enough to round away, so this page does not: the ticks on those rows sit on the half or the quarter, out of line with their neighbours. If you have ever had a call start thirty minutes late, you have met the reason.
    
          
    
          
    
            Will the .ics land at the right time in someone else’s calendar?
            
    The DTSTART is written as a UTC instant with a trailing Z, so a calendar application will render it in the correct local time for whoever opens it. That is the part this can get right. What it cannot do is send the file, add attendees, check whether anyone is free, or make the meeting exist — the .ics is a draft that lands in your downloads folder and nowhere else.
