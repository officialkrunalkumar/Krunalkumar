Timing side-channel attack

A comparison that returns on the first wrong character takes measurably longer when more of your guess was right. That difference is one loop iteration, and it is enough. This runs the attack for real against a naive comparison and against a constant-time one, and it tells you when your browser’s clock was too coarse to support the answer.

  • Recovers a secret character by character
  • Naive compare against constant-time
  • Measures your clock’s real resolution
  • Median of many interleaved samples
  • Says so out loud when it is only noise

Before you start

This tool runs entirely inside your browser. Nothing you paste or drop here is uploaded, logged or stored — which for security work is the whole point, not a footnote.

What it does not do

  • No uploads — there is no server to upload to
  • It cannot make your browser’s clock finer than it is
  • It attacks a secret you type here, nothing on any server
  • Runs are capped in length so the tab stays usable

Why that matters here

  • The secret you type stays in this tab
  • No account, no history, nothing saved
  • Nothing you type ever appears in your Network tab
  • Works with no internet once the page is cached
  • Free, permanently

Browsers deliberately coarsen performance.now() as a Spectre mitigation, and this page is not cross-origin-isolated, so the clock here is clamped — commonly to around a tenth of a millisecond, sometimes to a whole millisecond. The difference this attack hunts is one loop iteration. Press “Measure the clock” and this line will be replaced by what your browser actually gave.

How a comparison gives itself away

        

        

          

            
The early return is the whole bug

            
A hand-written comparison walks two strings and returns the moment two characters disagree. So the number of loop iterations it runs is the length of the shared prefix plus one, and the time it takes carries that number out of the function. Guess the first character correctly and the comparison does twice the work it did for a wrong guess. Keep the slowest candidate, fix it, move to the next position, and a secret falls one character at a time instead of all at once.

          

          

            
The median, never the mean

            
Every measurement here shares a processor with a compositor, a garbage collector and whatever else is open. Any one of those interruptions is worth more milliseconds than the entire signal, and a single one of them drags a mean past every other candidate — handing you a wrong character with total confidence. The median does not notice it. The tool also reports the fraction of paired samples in which the winner really was slower, because a gap between two medians on its own says nothing about whether the two distributions overlap.

          

          

            
The clock is the hard part, not the maths

            
Browsers clamp performance.now() on purpose, as a Spectre mitigation, and only relax it for pages served cross-origin-isolated with COOP and COEP headers. This page is not one of those and is not trying to be. So the tool measures the resolution it actually got — the smallest non-zero gap over hundreds of consecutive reads — and prints it before anything else. That is also why every sample runs the comparison hundreds of thousands of times before the clock is read at all. If the attack comes out as noise on your machine, that is a statement about your browser’s timer and not about the vulnerability.

          

          

            
Where this bites in real code

            
Not passwords. A password is checked against a slow hash, and the hash takes the same time whatever you send it. The exposure is everywhere a secret is compared as bytes: an HMAC on a webhook, an API token, a signed cookie, a password-reset token, a licence key. The fix is a comparison that always does the same work — hmac.compare_digest, crypto.timingSafeEqual, subtle.verify — and, better still, comparing HMACs of both values rather than the values, so that lengths and contents are uniform whatever arrives.

          

        

      


      

        

          
Questions people ask

        

          

            The attack failed on my machine. Does that mean the bug is not real?
            
No, and the tool is built to say so. What failed is the measurement. Your browser's clock is deliberately coarse, your machine is busy, and the signal being hunted is one loop iteration. Read the measured resolution the tool prints: if it is a tenth of a millisecond or worse, a failure there is a statement about the timer. A real attacker repeats orders of magnitude more, measures over a network where the repetitions are cheap, or sits on the same host with a cycle counter that none of these limits apply to.

          

          

            Why does it compare thousands of times for a single sample?
            
Because one comparison of a short string takes a few nanoseconds and the clock cannot see anything that small. Running the comparison hundreds of thousands of times multiplies the one-iteration difference until it is larger than a clock tick. The tool calibrates that count from two things it measures on your machine: the cost of comparing one character, and how long a batch has to last before the scheduler's own jitter stops dominating it. It prints which of the two decided the number.

          

          

            Why the median rather than the average?
            
Because one interruption is worth more than the entire signal. A garbage collection or a scheduler switch in the middle of one batch adds milliseconds to a measurement whose real difference is microseconds, and a mean will hand that candidate the win. A median throws it away. Some real attacks go further and take a low percentile or the fastest sample, on the argument that noise only ever makes a measurement slower and never faster; the median is used here because it is the choice that is hardest to get wrong.

          

          

            Is checking a password against a bcrypt hash vulnerable to this?
            
Not in the way people expect. The comparison happens against the output of a deliberately slow hash, so the work is the same whatever you send and there is no prefix to walk. The real exposure is where a secret is compared as raw bytes: HMAC signatures on webhooks, API tokens, signed cookies, reset tokens, licence keys. Those are the comparisons worth auditing.

          

          

            What is the actual fix?
            
Compare in constant time, with a function that already exists: hmac.compare_digest in Python, crypto.timingSafeEqual in Node, subtle.ConstantTimeCompare in Go. Better still, hash or HMAC both values and compare those, because then both inputs are the same fixed length whatever arrived and neither the length nor the content of the candidate changes the work done. Note what the constant-time function in this lab still leaks: its loop runs for the longer of the two lengths, so length is not fully hidden. Comparing digests is what fixes that too.

          

          

            Does the secret I type leave my browser?
            
No. There is no server to send it to. The comparisons, the timing, the statistics and the plot all run in this tab, and nothing you type appears in your Network tab. The secret is not a real credential anyway — it is the thing the attack in front of you is trying to recover.
