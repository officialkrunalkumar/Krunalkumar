What this sheet is, and what it is not

The whole difficulty, in one paragraph

Everything needed to make a convincing fake certificate is a name field, a number and an A4 stylesheet. A tool that stops there hands people something that looks issued and is not, and somebody eventually staples it to an application. So this one is built the other way round: the disclaimer is in the body of the document, not the footer, and the reference is shaped so that it cannot be confused with a real one even at a glance.

Why the reference says PRACTICE

The completion certificates I issue carry IDs like KS-INT-2026-XXXXXX, and each one is a record that /verify can confirm. This page mints PRACTICE-20260831-K7Q3M8 instead, and there is no record behind it — it exists only so you can tell two of your own printouts apart. Paste one into /verify and it will correctly tell you it has never heard of it. That is the system working.

It is deliberately plain

No seal, no ribbon, no gold rule, no signature line. Those exist to make a reader trust a document faster than they can read it, and a document that certifies nothing has no business borrowing that trust. What is left is typography and figures — which is, in fairness, what a practice record actually is.

What it is genuinely good for

Marking progress. A sheet from March next to a sheet from August is a better record of practice than a number you half-remember, and it is a reasonable thing to keep in a folder or attach to a note to yourself. If you are printing it for someone else, print the resume instead — that one is genuinely about persuading a stranger.

How the numbers are worked out

Gross, net and accuracy

One word is five characters including spaces, which is the convention every typing test uses, so a score here means what a score elsewhere means. Gross WPM is characters typed divided by five, over the elapsed minutes. Net WPM subtracts one whole word for every character still wrong at the end. Accuracy is correct keystrokes over total keystrokes: a typo you went back and fixed still cost you, because in real work it did.

Consistency

Speed is sampled once a second and consistency reports how little those samples varied, as a percentage. It is the number that tells you whether a good score came from steady typing or from a fast burst either side of a stall. Steady is the one that survives a long document.

The costly keys

For every keystroke after the first, the gap since the previous one is filed under the character that was due rather than the one that arrived — so a key you hesitate over and then get wrong still shows up as expensive. Only keys pressed at least three times are ranked, and pauses over two seconds are capped, so one interruption cannot crown a random comma the slowest key on your board.

Pasting is blocked, not policed

A pasted passage would produce a number that means nothing on a page that then offers to print it. So paste and drop are refused in the typing field, and if text arrives in a block anyway the run is flagged, kept out of your history and refused a certificate. It is not a security control — anyone determined can edit the numbers on their own machine — but it closes the accidental version, which is the one that actually happens.

Questions people ask

Will this certificate mean anything to an employer?
No, and it says so on its own face. Nobody watched you take the test, nothing checked who was typing, and every number on it was produced by your own browser. It is a practice record — good for pinning above a desk, good for showing yourself that last month was slower. Presented to an employer as a credential it would be a small forgery, which is why the sheet is worded so that nobody can be misled by it.
Why does the reference look nothing like the IDs on /verify?
Because they are different things, and making them look alike would be the dishonest part. Certificates I actually issue carry an ID beginning KS- and exist as records that /verify answers for. This page mints references beginning PRACTICE- instead, and there is nothing behind them: the reference only lets you tell two of your own printouts apart. Typing a PRACTICE- reference into /verify will correctly find nothing.
How are gross and net WPM different?
Gross WPM counts every character you typed, five characters to a word, over the elapsed minutes — the same convention every other typing test uses. Net WPM then subtracts one whole word for each character still wrong when the clock stopped. Gross is how fast your fingers moved; net is how much usable text you produced, and net is the number the certificate carries.
What is the consistency figure?
Your speed is sampled once a second, and consistency is how little those samples varied. A hundred per cent would be a metronome, which no human is. A high number means you held an even rhythm; a low one usually means long pauses on particular keys, which is exactly what the table of costly keys underneath is pointing at.
Why can I not paste into the test?
Because a pasted passage produces a number that means nothing, and this page then offers to print that number on something shaped like a certificate. Pasting is blocked outright. If text arrives in a large block anyway, the run is marked as not typed key by key, no certificate is offered for it, and it is kept out of your history.
Is anything I type, or my name, sent anywhere?
No. The passage, your keystrokes, the name you print and your history all stay in this browser. There is no server behind this page to send them to, and the certificate is produced by your own browser's print dialog rather than rendered somewhere else. Your attempts are kept in this browser's local storage on this device, and Clear history deletes them.
Does it work on a phone?
It runs, and the sheet prints. But a touch keyboard measures a different skill from a physical one, so compare a phone score with your other phone scores rather than with what you managed on a laptop. The certificate records the score, not the keyboard, and that is one more reason not to treat it as evidence of anything.
