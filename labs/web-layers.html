Surface web, deep web, dark web

Three words used constantly and defined the same way twice by nobody. They are not degrees of the same thing — they describe how you get in. The three words, and what actually separates them

One thing to say plainly before any of it: this page explains how the layers work and it deliberately does not help anyone reach anything. Every .onion and .i2p string on it is fabricated and structurally invalid, there are no addresses, directories or off-site links anywhere on it, and nothing here makes a network request. It is a diagram of a mechanism, not a route.

Surface is what a crawler can follow a link to

A page is surface web when a crawler can find a link to it, fetch it without credentials and store what comes back. That is the whole definition, and it is a statement about access rather than about content. It is also the smallest of the three layers by a long way — the visible internet is the part that happens to be reachable by following links, which is a much narrower thing than the part that exists.

Deep is everything you can reach and a crawler cannot

Your webmail. Your bank statements. A shared document whose link nobody published. A library catalogue that only answers a form. A page a robots.txt rule excludes. None of it is hidden from you — it appears the moment you ask — and none of it is in any index. This is the overwhelming majority of what is out there, and calling it deep web makes it sound clandestine when it is mostly just your own life, logged in.

Dark is a different network, not a deeper part of this one

A .onion address is not a website that is hard to find. It is a name that does not exist in DNS at all, resolvable only inside Tor, and the name is itself the public key — reaching the service and verifying it are the same act. That is the only thing that makes something dark web. It is an architectural fact about naming and routing, not a claim about what is being hosted.

The measurement problem is the honest headline

You cannot enumerate what you cannot address. Counts of onion sites come from crawls seeded with whatever address lists the researchers could get, run for however long the grant allowed, categorised by whoever was doing the reading — and they disagree with each other by tens of percentage points. Anyone quoting you a precise percentage of the dark web is telling you about their methodology, not about the world.

This is the part you can poke. The narrative version — where the three words came from, what the famous iceberg picture is actually a misreading of, and what any of it means in practice — is the article.

How to read the three panels

The classifier reads the string and nothing else

It runs an ordered list of checks over the address and stops at the first one that settles the question, then lists the checks it never reached so you can see what was not asked. Two of those checks answer “I cannot know from here” on purpose: a noindex header and another site’s robots.txt both live in a response, and getting a response means making a request. The one thing it can answer for is this site’s own robots.txt, because I wrote it.

The circuit shows knowledge, not geography

Three relays, three separate keys, and no relay holding more than one of them. The guard knows your address and not your destination; the exit knows your destination and not your address; the middle knows neither and exists so that the other two never talk. Click any node and the panel spells out what that machine holds and what it cannot hold. Flip the HTTPS and sign-in switches and watch the last two columns change — neither of those is anything Tor controls.

The onion-service mode is the symmetric case

An onion service is not a site with a strange address; it is the case where neither end learns the other. You build a three-hop circuit to a rendezvous relay, the service builds its own three-hop circuit to the same relay, and the innermost layer is keyed between the two of you so the meeting point holds a blob it cannot open. Six relays, and the only machine that knows where the service physically is happens to be the service’s own guard.

The bars are ranges, and they do not sum to a hundred

That is deliberate. Each bar spans the range across published estimates rather than picking a point inside it, and where the estimates overlap badly the bars do too. The traffic view asks a different and much less contested question, and it is the one that changes the picture most: the large majority of what Tor carries is people reading the ordinary web from networks that filter or log it, involving no onion service at all.

What is genuinely down there, named

Not a defence and not a whitewash — the criminal use is real and the tool says so. But a list of the legitimate uses is the part that usually goes missing.

Newsroom drop boxes

SecureDrop is a whistleblower submission system run as an onion service by newsrooms including The Guardian, The New York Times and The Washington Post. The point of the architecture is that the newsroom cannot identify its own source even if it is compelled to try, and neither can anyone watching the newsroom’s network. ProPublica published a full onion version of its site in 2016 for the same reason.

Mirrors that survive a national block

BBC News launched an official onion mirror in 2019 so the site stays reachable from countries that block the ordinary domain. Software projects such as Debian run onion mirrors of their package archives so that installing software does not require trusting the network you happen to be on. Wikipedia is the one people assume is on this list and, as far as I can find, has no official onion service — worth saying rather than repeating.

Most Tor use is not the dark web at all

The great majority of Tor traffic is ordinary public websites reached through an exit relay — people reading the news from somewhere that filters it, or simply not wanting a browsing history attached to their address. Onion service traffic has sat in the low single digits as a share of the network’s own traffic for years, by the Tor Project’s own published metrics. The layer the whole phrase refers to is a rounding error in the network it lives on.

And a large slice of it is criminals robbing each other

Crawls consistently find that a substantial share of what looks like a criminal economy is phishing clones of other onion sites, built to intercept payments to the original. Another large share — often the single largest category — is simply dead: addresses that have gone offline and stayed on lists that circulate long after. What is left is real and serious, and it is smaller than the picture.

Questions people ask

Is the deep web the same as the dark web?
No, and the difference is not a matter of degree. The deep web is everything a search engine cannot index - your webmail, your bank statements, a shared document, a database behind a form. The dark web is a much smaller thing: sites that live on a separate overlay network such as Tor and have addresses that ordinary DNS cannot resolve at all. Almost everything people call dark web is deep web, and almost all of the deep web is ordinary.
Does this tool visit the URL I type?
No. It reads the string on your own machine and makes no request of any kind. That is also its main limitation: a robots.txt rule, a noindex header and a login redirect are all things you can only learn by asking the server. Where the address cannot settle the question the tool says so rather than guessing.
Are the .onion addresses on this page real?
No, and they cannot be made real. A version 3 onion address is 56 base32 characters derived from a public key; the illustrative strings here contain hyphens and ordinary English words, which are not valid in that label, so none of them resolves anywhere. This lab explains the architecture. It is not a way in, and there are no addresses, directories or links off this site anywhere on it.
Is the dark web really most of the internet?
No. The claim people are half-remembering is about the deep web, which is genuinely far larger than the indexed surface web, and even there the famous figures are guesses. The dark web is a small fraction of that. By the Tor Project’s own published metrics, onion service traffic has sat in the low single digits as a share of Tor’s own traffic for years.
Does Tor make you anonymous?
It breaks the link between your address and the destination inside the network, which is a real and useful property. It does not defeat an adversary who can watch both ends and correlate timing, it does not stop a malicious exit relay reading plain HTTP, it does not change your browser’s fingerprint, and it does nothing at all about signing into an account that identifies you. The caveat panel in the second tab lists these because they are the ways people are actually deanonymised.
Why does the tool call a page on this very site deep web?
Because this site’s robots.txt disallows /partials/, /assets/data/ and /scripts/. Those paths have no login and no secret; they are served instantly to anyone who asks. They will simply never appear in a search result. That is the cleanest demonstration I have that deep does not mean sinister.
Why do the percentages show ranges rather than numbers?
Because the published studies disagree with each other by tens of percentage points, depending on how the crawl was seeded, how long it ran, whether dead addresses were counted and who did the categorising. A single figure would look better and tell you less. Anyone quoting you a precise percentage of the dark web is overstating what is measurable.
